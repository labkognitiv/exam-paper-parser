# Wave quantities and phase

## The wave channel of 1825

In 1825, Ernst Heinrich Weber and his brother Wilhelm Eduard Weber built a specialized wave channel in Leipzig.
They constructed a narrow glass trough measuring 1.83 metres in length and roughly 30 centimetres deep.
This transparent canal allowed them to view progressive water waves from the side without optical distortion.

To observe fluid motion beneath the surface, the brothers suspended powdered amber particles in the water.
Amber has nearly the same density as water, so the particles remained suspended at constant depths.
Illuminated from behind, the amber particles revealed the hidden internal motion of the fluid.

As a surface wave travelled along the channel, the amber particles did not drift down the trough.
Instead, each particle traced a localized cyclic path and returned to its starting resting position.
The wave disturbance moved forward across space, but the fluid itself did not travel along the tank.

Using a seconds pendulum, the Webers timed how long each particle took to complete one cycle.
They noted that particles further down the canal reached their highest points at later moments.
Every particle oscillated at the same rate, but each reached a given stage of motion at a different instant.

How do we mathematically distinguish the repeating wave shape spread across space from the oscillation of a single point through time?
How do we quantify the exact difference in stage between any two oscillating points on a wave?

## Waves, oscillations, and units

A progressive wave transfers energy from one point to another without transferring matter.
The particles of the transmitting medium oscillate about fixed equilibrium positions as the disturbance passes.
Energy moves onward across space, while the particles remain bound to their local surroundings.

Describing waves requires standard SI units and prefixes.
Lengths are measured in metres (m), often using prefixes such as millimetres ($1\text{ mm} = 10^{-3}\text{ m}$) or nanometres ($1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$).
Time is measured in seconds (s), frequently involving milliseconds ($1\text{ ms} = 10^{-3}\text{ s}$) or microseconds ($1\text{ }\mu\text{s} = 10^{-6}\text{ s}$).

Angles describe fractions of a repeating cycle.
One full cycle corresponds to $360^\circ$ or $2\pi\text{ radians}$.
Half a cycle equals $180^\circ$ or $\pi\text{ radians}$, and a quarter cycle equals $90^\circ$ or $\pi/2\text{ radians}$.

## Displacement and amplitude

A progressive wave creates a repeating pattern of peaks and troughs across space.
At any given instant, different particles in the medium sit at different positions relative to their resting state.
We quantify these particle positions using displacement and amplitude.

The undisturbed position of a particle before any wave arrives is its equilibrium position.
When a wave passes, the particle moves away from this central point.
Displacement is a vector quantity that specifies both distance and direction from equilibrium.

> **Displacement**
> distance in a specified direction from a point.

In wave analysis, displacement is measured from the equilibrium position.
Displacement changes continuously as a particle oscillates.
It takes positive values when the particle is on one side of equilibrium and negative values on the opposite side.

While displacement varies from moment to moment, the wave maintains a maximum extent of vibration.
We call this extreme displacement the amplitude.
Amplitude is a scalar quantity and is always reported as a positive magnitude.

> **Amplitude**
> the maximum displacement of a particle or point on a wave from its equilibrium position.

On a graph, amplitude is the vertical distance from the equilibrium line to a crest or trough.
A common error is measuring the distance from a crest all the way to a trough.
That total crest-to-trough distance is the peak-to-peak displacement, which equals twice the amplitude ($2A$).

### Worked Example 1: Determining displacement and amplitude

A floating marker on a water wave oscillates vertically between a maximum height of $+6.0\text{ mm}$ and a minimum height of $-6.0\text{ mm}$.
At a particular instant, the marker is located at a height of $+3.5\text{ mm}$ relative to the still water surface.
State the amplitude of the wave and the instantaneous displacement of the marker at that moment.

**Answer**
The amplitude of the wave is $6.0\text{ mm}$.
The instantaneous displacement of the marker is $+3.5\text{ mm}$.

**Explanation**
The still water surface represents the equilibrium position ($y = 0$).
The maximum distance from the equilibrium line to the highest crest is $6.0\text{ mm}$.
Amplitude is measured from the equilibrium line to a peak or trough, never from peak to trough.
The instantaneous displacement is the signed distance from equilibrium at the specified moment, which is $+3.5\text{ mm}$.

## Period and frequency

A displacement-time graph tracks the movement of one medium particle as time passes.
As the wave passes through that location, the particle repeats its motion over regular time intervals.
We describe this temporal rhythm using period and frequency.

> **Period**
> the time taken for one complete oscillation or wave cycle.

The period is represented by the symbol $T$ and has the SI unit second (s).
One complete oscillation requires a particle to travel from a peak to a trough and back to a peak.
Measuring between two successive peaks or two successive troughs gives the period $T$.

Frequency measures how rapidly the oscillations repeat.
It is represented by the symbol $f$ and has the SI unit hertz (Hz).
One hertz equals one complete cycle per second ($1\text{ Hz} = 1\text{ s}^{-1}$).

> **Frequency**
> the number of complete oscillations or wave cycles per unit time.

Period and frequency are inverse quantities.
If a wave takes $0.01\text{ s}$ to complete one cycle, then $100$ complete cycles occur in one second.
We express this fundamental reciprocal relationship with the formula:
$$T = \frac{1}{f} \quad \text{and} \quad f = \frac{1}{T}$$
In these equations, $T$ is in seconds (s) and $f$ is in hertz (Hz).

In experiments, timing a single oscillation introduces substantial human reaction time error.
To reduce experimental uncertainty, physicists measure the total time for many complete oscillations.
Dividing the total time by the number of cycles yields an accurate measurement of the period.

### Worked Example 2: Calculating frequency from timing data

A student uses a stopwatch to time $20$ complete oscillations of a floating marker in a ripple tank.
The total time recorded for these $20$ cycles is $8.0\text{ s}$.
Calculate the period and the frequency of the wave.

**Answer**
The period of the wave is $T = 8.0\text{ s} / 20 = 0.40\text{ s}$.
The frequency of the wave is $f = 1 / T = 1 / 0.40\text{ s} = 2.5\text{ Hz}$.

**Explanation**
Dividing the total duration by the number of complete oscillations yields the period.
This calculation gives $T = 8.0\text{ s} / 20 = 0.40\text{ s}$ per oscillation.
The frequency is the reciprocal of the period, so $f = 1 / T$.
Substituting $0.40\text{ s}$ yields $f = 2.5\text{ Hz}$.

## Wavelength and wave speed

While period measures cycle duration in time, wavelength measures cycle repeat in space.
Wavelength is represented by the Greek letter lambda ($\lambda$) and has the SI unit metre (m).
It is the spatial length of one complete wave cycle.

> **Wavelength**
> the minimum distance between two adjacent points on a wave that are in phase.

Notice the two essential conditions in this Cambridge definition: the points must be adjacent, and they must be in phase.
The distance between two successive crests or two successive troughs equals one wavelength ($\lambda$).
The distance from a crest to the adjacent trough is only half a wavelength ($\lambda / 2$).

Cambridge examiners also accept two alternative descriptions of wavelength.
Wavelength is the distance moved by a wavefront or wave energy in one period.
It is also described as the distance between adjacent wavefronts.

As the wave moves through space, the disturbance travels forward at a characteristic wave speed.
Wave speed is the distance travelled by a wavefront per unit time.
In a uniform medium, wave speed remains constant.

Do not confuse wave speed with particle speed.
Wave speed describes how fast the overall disturbance and its energy propagate through the medium.
Particle speed describes how fast a localized piece of the medium moves about its equilibrium position.
While the wave moves steadily forward, each particle accelerates, stops momentarily at peaks, and reaches maximum speed at equilibrium.

### Worked Example 3: Identifying wavelength from crests and troughs

A snapshot of a water wave shows a crest at a horizontal position of $x = 0.40\text{ m}$.
The adjacent trough is located at $x = 1.00\text{ m}$.
Calculate the wavelength of the wave.

**Answer**
The half-wavelength distance is $\lambda / 2 = 1.00\text{ m} - 0.40\text{ m} = 0.60\text{ m}$.
Therefore, the full wavelength is $\lambda = 2 \times 0.60\text{ m} = 1.20\text{ m}$.

**Explanation**
The distance from a crest to the adjacent trough spans half of a full wave cycle.
The separation between the crest and trough is $1.00\text{ m} - 0.40\text{ m} = 0.60\text{ m}$.
Because this separation equals $\lambda / 2$, multiplying by two gives the full wavelength $\lambda = 1.20\text{ m}$.

## Reading wave graphs: space versus time

Examiners frequently test your ability to read two distinct kinds of wave graphs.
A displacement-distance graph captures a frozen snapshot of the wave at one instant in time.
It displays the displacements of many particles spread along the wave at that single moment.

In contrast, a displacement-time graph shows the history of one single particle over time.
Points separated by one full cycle on a displacement-time graph mark one period.
It plots how the displacement of that particular particle varies as successive waves pass.

Both graphs have identical vertical axes showing displacement $y$.
On both graphs, the vertical height from the centre line to a crest represents amplitude $A$.
The crucial difference lies on the horizontal axis.

On a displacement-distance graph, the horizontal axis shows distance $x$.
The horizontal distance across one full repeating cycle is the wavelength $\lambda$.
Never attempt to read a period from a distance axis.

On a displacement-time graph, the horizontal axis shows time $t$.
The horizontal duration across one full repeating cycle is the period $T$.
Never attempt to read a wavelength from a time axis.

### Worked Example 4: Extracting wave quantities from dual graphs

Graph 1 plots displacement $y$ against distance $x$, showing crests at $x = 2.0\text{ cm}$ and $x = 6.0\text{ cm}$ with crest height $3.0\text{ mm}$.
Graph 2 plots displacement $y$ against time $t$ for one particle, showing upward zero-crossings at $t = 1.0\text{ ms}$ and $t = 5.0\text{ ms}$.
Determine the amplitude, wavelength, period, and frequency of the wave.

**Answer**
The amplitude is $A = 3.0\text{ mm}$, and the wavelength is $\lambda = 6.0\text{ cm} - 2.0\text{ cm} = 4.0\text{ cm}$.
The period is $T = 5.0\text{ ms} - 1.0\text{ ms} = 4.0\text{ ms} = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s}$.
The frequency is $f = 1 / T = 1 / (4.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ s}) = 250\text{ Hz}$.

**Explanation**
Check the horizontal axis label before reading repeating wave intervals.
On Graph 1, the distance between crests gives wavelength $\lambda = 4.0\text{ cm}$.
On Graph 2, the cycle repeat duration gives period $T = 4.0\text{ ms}$, yielding frequency $f = 1 / T = 250\text{ Hz}$.
The amplitude on both graphs is the peak displacement of $3.0\text{ mm}$.

## What is phase?

Phase describes the point that an oscillating particle has reached within its repeating cycle of motion.
We can visualize this by mapping one complete oscillation around a circle of $360^\circ$ or $2\pi\text{ radians}$.
Starting at equilibrium corresponds to $0^\circ$, reaching maximum positive displacement is $90^\circ$, returning to equilibrium is $180^\circ$, and reaching the trough is $270^\circ$.

A widespread misconception is assuming that equal displacement guarantees equal phase.
Particles with identical displacement can move in opposite directions at the same instant.
For example, consider two particles crossing the equilibrium line ($y = 0$).

If one particle moves upward while the other moves downward, they are at opposite stages of their cycle.
They have the same position, but their motions are completely opposed.
Therefore, two particles are in phase only when they have identical displacement and are moving in the identical direction.

When two particles are in phase, they move in perfect synchrony.
They reach their maximum positive displacements at the exact same moment.
They also cross the equilibrium line simultaneously, moving in the exact same direction.

When two particles are in antiphase, or in opposite phase, their motions are completely inverted.
One reaches its positive peak at the exact instant the other reaches its negative trough.
Antiphase corresponds to half a cycle of separation, or a phase difference of $180^\circ$ ($\pi\text{ rad}$).

### Worked Example 5: Determining phase relationships

A progressive transverse wave moves horizontally to the right along a rope.
Particle P is at equilibrium ($y = 0$) moving upward, while particle Q is at equilibrium moving downward.
Particle R is also at equilibrium moving upward, positioned one full wavelength beyond P.
Explain the phase relationship between P and Q, and between P and R.

**Answer**
Particles P and Q are in antiphase, with a phase difference of $180^\circ$ ($\pi\text{ rad}$).
Particles P and R are in phase, with a phase difference of $0^\circ$ ($2\pi\text{ rad}$).

**Explanation**
Two particles are in phase only if their displacements and velocity directions match exactly.
Particles P and Q have zero displacement but opposite velocities, so they are in antiphase.
Particle R shares both the zero displacement and upward velocity of P, so they are in phase.

## Quantitative phase difference

Phase difference ($\Delta\phi$) quantifies the difference in cycle stage between two points or two waves.
We measure phase difference in angular units, using either degrees ($^\circ$) or radians (rad).
Always include an appropriate unit when stating a phase difference in exam questions.

Two waves can share the same frequency but reach their peaks at different times.
Similarly, two points along a wave profile sit at different stages of vibration because the wave takes time to travel between them.
We calculate phase difference by determining the fraction of a complete cycle that separates the two points.

If two points on a wave are separated by a spatial distance $\Delta x$, they represent a fraction $\Delta x / \lambda$ of a cycle.
Multiplying this fraction by the angle of one full cycle gives the phase difference:
$$\Delta\phi = \frac{\Delta x}{\lambda} \times 360^\circ \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta\phi = \frac{\Delta x}{\lambda} \times 2\pi\text{ rad}$$
Here, $\Delta x$ and $\lambda$ must share identical distance units.

If two oscillations are separated by a time shift $\Delta t$, they represent a fraction $\Delta t / T$ of a cycle.
Multiplying this temporal fraction by a full cycle angle gives the phase difference:
$$\Delta\phi = \frac{\Delta t}{T} \times 360^\circ \quad \text{or} \quad \Delta\phi = \frac{\Delta t}{T} \times 2\pi\text{ rad}$$
In this equation, $\Delta t$ and $T$ must share identical time units.

Certain phase differences appear frequently in physics problems.
A separation of one quarter cycle gives $\Delta\phi = 90^\circ$ ($\pi/2\text{ rad}$).
A separation of half a cycle gives $\Delta\phi = 180^\circ$ ($\pi\text{ rad}$).
A separation of one full cycle gives $\Delta\phi = 360^\circ$ ($2\pi\text{ rad}$), which is physically equivalent to $0^\circ$.

### Worked Example 6: Phase difference from spatial separation

A progressive sound wave in air has a wavelength of $0.80\text{ m}$.
Calculate the phase difference between two points along the wave separated by $0.20\text{ m}$.
State your answer in both degrees and radians.

**Answer**
The phase difference in degrees is $\Delta\phi = (0.20\text{ m} / 0.80\text{ m}) \times 360^\circ = 90^\circ$.
The phase difference in radians is $\Delta\phi = (0.20\text{ m} / 0.80\text{ m}) \times 2\pi\text{ rad} = \pi/2\text{ rad}$ (or $1.6\text{ rad}$).

**Explanation**
Phase difference equals the path separation divided by wavelength multiplied by a full circle angle.
The cycle fraction is $\Delta x / \lambda = 0.20\text{ m} / 0.80\text{ m} = 0.25$.
Multiplying $0.25$ by $360^\circ$ yields $90^\circ$.
Multiplying $0.25$ by $2\pi\text{ radians}$ yields $\pi/2\text{ radians}$.

### Worked Example 7: Phase difference between two wave traces

Two waves, R and S, of the same frequency have a period of $0.60\text{ s}$.
A crest of wave R occurs at $t = 0.15\text{ s}$, followed by a crest of wave S at $t = 0.25\text{ s}$.
Calculate the phase difference between wave R and wave S in degrees and radians.

**Answer**
The time lag is $\Delta t = 0.25\text{ s} - 0.15\text{ s} = 0.10\text{ s}$.
The phase difference in degrees is $\Delta\phi = (0.10\text{ s} / 0.60\text{ s}) \times 360^\circ = 60^\circ$.
The phase difference in radians is $\Delta\phi = (0.10\text{ s} / 0.60\text{ s}) \times 2\pi\text{ rad} = \pi/3\text{ rad}$ (or $1.0\text{ rad}$).

**Explanation**
A temporal time lag translates directly to an angular phase difference across one period.
The fraction of a cycle is $\Delta t / T = 0.10\text{ s} / 0.60\text{ s} = 1/6$.
Multiplying $1/6$ by $360^\circ$ gives $60^\circ$, and multiplying by $2\pi\text{ radians}$ gives $\pi/3\text{ radians}$.

## Synthesis of wave quantities and phase

Understanding progressive waves requires separating spatial geometry from temporal vibration.
Amplitude and displacement describe the vertical extent of motion on both spatial snapshots and temporal histories.
Wavelength characterizes the repeating pattern across distance, while period and frequency govern the repetition in time.

The Weber brothers' 1825 wave channel clearly displayed this physical duality.
Their illuminated amber particles proved that individual fluid elements oscillate locally without travelling down the canal.
Those particles traced the temporal period, while the visible surface crests mapped the spatial wavelength.

Phase difference connects these dual perspectives.
A spatial distance along a wave or a time shift between oscillations corresponds to a definite fraction of a cycle.
Whether expressed in degrees or radians, phase difference quantifies the exact offset between oscillating points.

You will also derive the wave equation that connects wave speed, frequency, and wavelength.
Mastering these fundamental quantities ensures a solid foundation for all wave physics.
