# Wave motion and energy transfer

## A wave passes, but what travels?

Around the year 1500, Leonardo da Vinci watched the wind sweep across a wheat field near Florence.
He noticed that tall stalks bent forward as each gust arrived and then sprang back upright.
A broad ripple seemed to race across the entire landscape.
Yet every single plant remained rooted firmly in the soil.

Leonardo also observed this curious behavior while watching calm water.
When a pebble struck the surface, concentric circular ripples spread outward from the splash toward the grassy banks.
A floating straw merely bobs up and down as each crest passes.
The straw did not travel outward across the pond with the expanding rings.

In his notebook, Leonardo recorded this striking puzzle about fluid motion.
He wrote that the wave flees the place of its creation, while the water does not.
The moving shape travels rapidly across the surface, but the substance itself stays in place.
This surprising contrast puzzled observers who assumed moving patterns required moving matter.

We encounter this same puzzle whenever a disturbance moves through a physical medium.
When an observable wave travels across a rope, a spring, or the surface of a pond, what is actually moving across space?
How can a disturbance travel to a distant boundary if the physical substance remains behind?
This lesson reveals the physical mechanism that connects moving disturbances to stationary matter.

## Foundations of oscillation and energy

Before we examine waves, let us recall how isolated particles behave when disturbed.
An undisturbed object rests at its equilibrium position where the resultant force on it equals zero.
When an external force displaces the object, internal restoring forces pull it back toward equilibrium.
The object overshoots and oscillates back and forth across its resting position.

Energy principles govern this repetitive back-and-forth motion.
Doing work on a mechanical system transfers energy to that system.
A moving mass possesses kinetic energy, while an elastic material displaced from rest stores potential energy.
As a particle oscillates, energy continuously shifts between these kinetic and potential forms.

## A travelling disturbance through neighbour interactions

Now imagine many particles connected side by side in a continuous medium.
When you disturb the first particle, you pull it away from its equilibrium position.
Each section of the rope pulls directly on the neighbouring section next to it.
This contact force pulls the neighbour away from equilibrium a moment later.

Because of inertia, the second section does not move instantaneously.
It responds with a brief delay, gains speed, and in turn pulls on the third section.
The disturbance passes progressively from one tiny element to the next along the chain.
In this way, wave motion represents a propagating disturbance, which is a disturbance travelling through a medium via neighbour interactions.

Notice what happens to any single element of the material as the disturbance travels past.
The element rises, reaches a maximum displacement, and then experiences a restoring force toward equilibrium.
It returns to its original resting place once the disturbance passes onward.
The pattern travels across the entire length, but the physical substance only oscillates locally.

### Worked Example 1: Tracking a marked point on a rope

A horizontal rope is clamped at one end and held taut by a student at the other end.
A piece of bright red ribbon is tied securely around the rope near its middle.
The student flicks the free end upward once to send a single pulse along the rope.
Describe the subsequent motion of the pulse and the motion of the red ribbon.

**Answer**
The upward pulse travels horizontally along the rope toward the clamped end at constant speed.
The red ribbon moves upward from its equilibrium position, reaches a crest, and moves downward.
The ribbon returns to rest at its original location once the disturbance passes.
The pulse propagates along the rope, while the ribbon undergoes zero net displacement.

**Explanation**
We must separate the motion of the travelling disturbance from the motion of the material.
The pulse is a geometric shape that propagates along the rope because neighbouring segments pull each other.
The ribbon is attached to one specific segment of rope fibres.
That segment can only oscillate vertically around its resting position and cannot travel horizontally with the pulse.

## Mechanical demonstrations: ropes, springs, and ripple tanks

Three classic mechanical systems demonstrate wave motion clearly.
These familiar systems are stretched ropes, helical springs, and shallow ripple tanks.
Each system lets us observe a travelling disturbance alongside the local motion of the medium.
Comparing them reveals the universal mechanism shared by all mechanical waves.

Consider first a long horizontal rope stretched under tension.
When you flick one end vertically, a curved pulse travels rapidly toward the far wall.
A small marker on the rope moves vertically upward and downward across the undisturbed line.
The marker demonstrates local motion perpendicular to the direction in which the disturbance propagates.

Next, consider a long helical spring resting on a smooth floor.
If you give one end a sharp push and pull along the spring axis, coils become squeezed together.
A compressed cluster of coils travels smoothly along the length of the spring.
Behind this compression, the coils expand back to their original resting spacing.

Individual coils on the spring do not travel from your hand to the far end.
Each coil shifts forward a short distance, pushes against its neighbour, and bounces back to rest.
Here, the local oscillation occurs back and forth along the same line as the travelling pulse.
The compressed pattern travels the entire length, while each coil remains near its own home position.

Finally, consider water waves generated in a laboratory ripple tank.
A motor dips a straight bar or a small sphere repeatedly into the shallow water surface.
Ripples spread outward across the surface toward sloping foam beaches that absorb the waves.
A light cork floating on the water surface bobs up and down near one fixed spot.

The cork demonstrates that water does not flow en masse across the tank.
Instead, surface water particles rise and fall locally while passing the disturbance onward to adjacent water.
If water were transported across the tank, the cork would be swept into the far beach.
Because the cork stays in place, we know the travelling wave pattern is not a stream of matter.

### Worked Example 2: Comparing motion in a spring and a ripple tank

A student sends a single compression pulse along a horizontal spring.
At the same time, another student generates a circular ripple on a water surface.
Compare the movement of a marked coil on the spring with that of a floating cork on the water.
State the key feature that both demonstrations have in common.

**Answer**
A marked coil on the spring oscillates back and forth parallel to the length of the spring.
A floating cork on the water bobs predominantly up and down perpendicular to the water surface.
In both cases, each medium element returns to its equilibrium position after the disturbance passes.
Both systems transfer energy across space while their physical medium remains in place.

**Explanation**
In each apparatus, we distinguish the path of the travelling wave from the local motion of the matter.
The compression in the spring advances horizontally while the coils shift along that same horizontal axis.
The water ripples spread horizontally while the water surface and cork oscillate locally around resting levels.
Neither demonstration transports matter from the source to the far boundary.

## Progressive waves and energy transfer

We have seen that a travelling disturbance moves across space while the medium stays in place.
Now we must ask what physical quantity is actually delivered to the distant boundary.
When the pulse on a rope reaches the far end, it can rattle a metal ring attached to the wall.
When a water wave reaches a floating gate, it lifts the gate and performs mechanical work.

Because the arriving wave can exert forces and cause movement, it must carry energy.
This insight gives us the fundamental definition required in Cambridge examinations.
A progressive wave transfers energy from place to place.
The term progressive means that the wave travels through space rather than remaining fixed in one location.

A progressive wave transfers energy from a source to a receiver with zero net matter transport.
Zero net matter transport means the medium elements oscillate locally without permanently relocating across space.
Mechanical work done at the source gives kinetic and potential energy to the first medium section.
Forces between neighbouring particles pass this energy forward step by step through the medium.

At the far end, the wave delivers this energy to an absorber or receiver.
The medium provides the physical particles that interact, but those particles do not travel along with the energy.
Once the disturbance passes, every medium element returns to its initial resting state.
Zero net displacement of particles does not mean zero energy transfer.

Examiners often contrast progressive waves with stationary waves.
In a stationary wave, energy is trapped within a fixed pattern and is not transferred along the medium.
In a progressive wave, energy moves continuously from one position to another through space.
Remembering this distinction will help you answer exam questions about wave classification.

### Worked Example 3: Energy transfer without transport of matter

A wave machine at one end of a test tank generates continuous water waves.
At the opposite end of the tank, a toy boat floats near a dock 15 metres away.
After several seconds, the waves reach the boat and cause it to rock vigorously up and down.
Explain how the wave machine transfers energy to the boat without transporting water along the tank.

**Answer**
The wave machine does work on the water, creating an oscillating disturbance at the source.
Neighbouring water molecules exert forces on each other, transferring kinetic and potential energy across the tank.
Water molecules oscillate locally about equilibrium, so no net transfer of water occurs along the tank.
The bobbing motion of the boat confirms that energy has reached the distant dock.

**Explanation**
Energy transfer requires a physical pathway, but it does not require bulk mass flow.
The input work at the generator creates local motion and pressure changes in the nearest water.
Each water layer pushes on the next layer, transmitting the mechanical energy forward across the tank.
The boat gains kinetic and potential energy from this arriving disturbance, while the water remains in the tank.

## Two-track wave analysis: disturbance speed versus particle speed

To understand wave motion clearly, you must separate your analysis into two independent tracks.
Track one tracks the overall wave pattern as it travels across space.
Track two tracks an individual particle of the medium as it oscillates about its equilibrium position.
Confusing these two tracks is the most frequent beginner error in physics examinations.

In track one, the wave pattern advances at a constant propagation speed determined by the medium.
The crest moves forward steadily, covering equal distances in equal time intervals.
The direction of track one is the direction of energy transfer away from the source.
This propagation speed remains steady even when individual particles momentarily come to a stop.

In track two, an individual particle undergoes changing motion with varying velocity and acceleration.
As a crest approaches, the particle speeds up, passes through equilibrium, and slows down.
At maximum displacement, the particle momentarily stops before reversing its direction of motion.
When a particle momentarily stops at its maximum displacement, the travelling wave pattern does not stop.

An individual particle on the string moves downwards through its equilibrium position with changing speed.
Its speed is greatest as it passes through equilibrium and drops to zero at its turning points.
Over one complete oscillation, the net displacement of the particle is exactly zero.
The wave pattern keeps moving forward at constant speed, while the particle remains bound to its resting position.

## Single pulses and continuous periodic wave trains

A wave disturbance can take the form of an isolated pulse or a continuous train of waves.
When you flick a rope once, you create a single travelling pulse that passes and leaves the medium calm.
If you connect the rope to a motor that vibrates repeatedly, you generate a continuous periodic wave train.
Both situations operate on the same neighbour-to-neighbour interaction principles.

A mechanical vibration generator drives one end of a taut horizontal string.
As the vibrator pin moves continuously up and down, it generates a steady succession of crests and troughs.
The curved string shows the instantaneous displacement of the medium from its straight equilibrium axis.
At any single instant, the string forms a smooth undulating curve frozen in space.

Although the wave pattern appears to glide smoothly along the string, each particle acts independently.
Successive particles along the string repeat the motion of the source with a progressive time delay.
A particle close to the generator begins oscillating before a particle located further down the string.
Because each neighbour reacts a little later, different points along the string occupy different positions at any given instant.

## Mechanical waves and waves in a vacuum

The examples we have studied so far all require a physical substance to transmit the disturbance.
Ropes, springs, water, and air all consist of material particles that exert forces on their neighbours.
Waves that require a material medium to propagate are called mechanical waves.
Without particles to push and pull on one another, mechanical waves cannot travel through a vacuum.

However, not all progressive waves in the universe are mechanical waves.
Progressive waves also include electromagnetic waves, such as visible light, radio waves, and microwaves.
Electromagnetic waves transfer energy through oscillating electric and magnetic fields rather than vibrating matter.
Because these fields exist in empty space, electromagnetic waves can travel freely through a complete vacuum.

In later lessons, you will learn to calculate specific wave properties like frequency and wavelength.
You will also classify waves as transverse or longitudinal and examine waves that form stationary patterns.
For now, remember the core universal property that unites all progressive waves.
Every progressive wave transfers energy from one position to another without transporting matter.

## Returning to Leonardo's wheat field and water ripples

Let us now return to the historical observation that opened our investigation.
Leonardo da Vinci watched wheat stalks sway in the breeze and ripples spread across a pond.
He wondered what was moving across space if the grain and the water remained in their places.
We now have the complete scientific explanation to answer Leonardo's question.

When a wave travels across a medium, the travelling entity is a disturbance that carries energy.
The wind does work on the first wheat stalks, which push on their neighbours and spring back upright.
In the pond, the splash does work that spreads outward as kinetic and potential energy through inter-molecular forces.
The water molecules bob locally around their resting positions without travelling with the wave crests.

What travels across space is the energy and the organized pattern of disturbance.
The physical substance never journeys from the source to the distant boundary.
Leonardo's insight captured the defining property of every progressive wave.
Energy moves forward across space, while the particles of the medium oscillate locally about their equilibrium positions.

## Lesson recap and next steps

In this introductory lesson, we established the foundational physical model of wave motion.
A progressive wave is a wave that transfers energy from place to place.
This energy transfer occurs without any net transport of the medium through which the wave travels.
Neighbouring particles exert contact forces on one another, passing the disturbance forward with a slight time delay.

We also learned to separate wave analysis into two distinct kinematic tracks.
The wave pattern propagates through space at a constant speed determined by the medium.
Meanwhile, individual particles of the medium oscillate about their equilibrium positions with varying speed.
Mechanical waves require material particles to propagate, whereas electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum.

In the next lesson, we will make this qualitative model quantitative.
You will learn to define and measure key wave quantities, including amplitude, period, frequency, wavelength, and wave speed.
We will also examine how to determine the phase difference between two points along a wave train.
These measurements will turn our visual understanding of wave motion into precise mathematical tools.
