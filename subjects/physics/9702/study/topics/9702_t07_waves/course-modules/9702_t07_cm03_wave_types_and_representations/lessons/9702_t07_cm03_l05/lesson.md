# Transverse and longitudinal waves and wave graphs

## The mystery of the two earthquake pulses

On 12 June 1897, a colossal earthquake shook Assam in northeastern India.
The violent shock generated powerful vibrations that travelled through deep rock layers across the planet.
Sensitive pendulum seismographs in Europe, Asia, and Australia recorded the ground vibrations.

In 1900, British seismologist Richard Dixon Oldham analysed these worldwide seismogram traces.
He discovered that seismic energy consistently arrived in two distinct pulses separated in time.
The first pulse was a fast preliminary tremor named the primary wave or P-wave.

A slower, more disruptive pulse arrived minutes later and was named the secondary wave or S-wave.
Oldham showed that P-waves and S-waves travel at different speeds because they produce different types of ground motion.
In a P-wave, rock particles oscillate back and forth along the direction of the wave.

In an S-wave, rock particles vibrate from side to side across the direction of travel.
In 1906, Oldham made an even more astonishing discovery about Earth's interior.
He found that S-waves completely fail to pass through the deep core of the Earth.

Because transverse shear waves cannot travel through liquids, Oldham proved that Earth possesses a molten liquid outer core.
In 1909, Andrija Mohorovicic used the arrival times of both waves to locate the boundary between Earth's crust and mantle.
How do the relative directions of particle oscillation and wave travel distinguish these two fundamental wave types?
How can we decode both wave types from spatial and temporal displacement graphs?

## Wave motion and wave parameters reminder

A progressive wave transfers energy from one location to another through a medium.
As the wave passes, particles of the medium oscillate about fixed equilibrium positions.
The medium itself does not experience any net movement of matter.

We describe wave motion using specific physical quantities.
Displacement is the instantaneous distance and direction of an oscillating particle from its undisturbed equilibrium position.
Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of displacement from the equilibrium position.

Wavelength is the shortest distance between two adjacent points oscillating in identical phase.
Two points oscillate in phase when they are at identical positions and moving in identical directions.
The period is the time taken for a particle to complete one full cycle of oscillation.
Frequency is the number of complete oscillations per unit time, given by $f = 1 / T$.

The wave speed $v$ is the speed at which energy propagates through the medium.
All progressive waves satisfy the fundamental wave equation.
$$v = f\lambda$$
Here $v$ is in $\text{m s}^{-1}$, $f$ is in hertz (Hz), and $\lambda$ is in metres (m).

## Transverse waves

Waves are classified into two broad categories based on the direction of particle oscillations relative to energy propagation.
To classify any wave, we identify two vectors: the direction of energy transfer and the direction of oscillation.
We call this comparison the two-arrow test.

In a transverse wave, particles oscillate at right angles to the direction of wave travel.
Cambridge International requires a precise definition based on energy transfer.

> **Transverse wave**
> a wave in which the displacement of particles is perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer

Consider a long rope held horizontally and shaken up and down at one end.
A wave profile of crests and troughs travels horizontally away from your hand.
Every point on the rope moves purely vertically, at ninety degrees to the horizontal energy transfer.

A two-arrow diagram contrasts the horizontal energy flow with vertical particle motion.
Top panel shows a wave travelling along a rope with perpendicular oscillations.
Common examples of transverse waves include electromagnetic waves, waves on strings, surface ripples on water, and seismic S-waves.
Electromagnetic waves are unique because they do not require a material medium to propagate.

### Worked Example 1: Applying the two-arrow test for transverse waves

A student plucks a horizontal guitar string so that it vibrates vertically.
State whether the wave travelling along the string is transverse or longitudinal, and explain your choice using energy propagation.

**Answer**
The wave travelling along the string is a transverse wave.
The displacement of the string particles is perpendicular to the direction of energy propagation along the string.

**Explanation**
Energy travels horizontally along the length of the string from the plucked point toward the fixed bridges.
Plucking a guitar string displaces the string segments at right angles to the wave speed.
Because particle oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer, the wave is transverse.

## Longitudinal waves

The second fundamental category of wave motion is the longitudinal wave.
In a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate along the same line as the wave propagation.

> **Longitudinal wave**
> a wave in which particle displacement is parallel to the direction of energy propagation

To picture a longitudinal wave, imagine a long helical slinky spring resting on a smooth bench.
Pushing and pulling one end forward and backward sends pulses travelling along the spring.
Each individual coil moves forward and backward parallel to the length of the spring.

A common misconception is that longitudinal waves push the particles of the medium continuously forward.
Particles do not travel from source to receiver.
Instead, each particle executes local oscillations about a fixed equilibrium position, transferring energy forward through collisions.

Sound waves in gases, liquids, and solids are longitudinal waves.
Seismic P-waves and compression pulses in springs are also longitudinal.
Because sound in air requires particle collisions, sound waves cannot travel through a vacuum.

### Worked Example 2: Describing air particle motion in a sound wave

A loudspeaker emits a sound wave that travels horizontally to the right through air.
Describe the motion of an air particle at a point in the path of the wave.

**Answer**
The air particle oscillates back and forth about a fixed equilibrium position.
The oscillations of the particle are parallel to the direction of travel of the sound wave.

**Explanation**
Sound is a progressive longitudinal wave.
A progressive wave causes particles in the medium to vibrate without net transfer of matter.
For a longitudinal wave, the direction of particle oscillation is collinear with the direction of energy propagation.
Therefore, the particle moves horizontally left and right as the wave travels to the right.

## Compressions and rarefactions

Because particles in a longitudinal wave oscillate parallel to energy propagation, they move closer together and farther apart periodically.
This motion creates alternating regions of high and low particle density along the medium.
We call these distinctive physical regions compressions and rarefactions.

Along the stretched helical spring, coils crowd into compressions and spread apart in rarefactions.
A compression is a region where particles are closer together than their normal equilibrium spacing.
In a compression, the particle density and pressure are higher than their undisturbed equilibrium values.

A rarefaction is a region where particles are farther apart than their normal equilibrium spacing.
In a rarefaction, the particle density and pressure are lower than their undisturbed equilibrium values.
As the wave progresses, these high-pressure and low-pressure regions travel continuously forward at wave speed.

We determine the wavelength of a longitudinal wave by measuring between matching physical states.
The distance from the centre of one compression to the centre of the next compression is one wavelength $\lambda$.
Similarly, the distance from one rarefaction to the next rarefaction is also one wavelength $\lambda$.

Students often confuse the distance between adjacent opposite regions with a full wavelength.
The shortest distance between a compression and an adjacent rarefaction equals half of a wavelength.
These adjacent opposite states are in antiphase, having a phase difference of 180 degrees or $\pi$ radians.
We write this half-wavelength spatial separation as $\lambda / 2$.

### Worked Example 3: Finding wavelength from compression and rarefaction intervals

A longitudinal wave of frequency 16 kHz travels through a solid bar from left to right.
The distance between a compression and the adjacent rarefaction is 0.19 m.
Calculate the wavelength of the wave and its speed in the bar.

**Answer**
The wavelength is calculated from the half-wavelength separation.
$$\lambda = 2 \times 0.19\text{ m} = 0.38\text{ m}$$
Using the wave equation, the speed of the wave in the bar is $6100\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
$$v = f\lambda = 16 \times 10^3\text{ Hz} \times 0.38\text{ m} = 6100\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

**Explanation**
A compression and the adjacent rarefaction represent opposite phases of the wave cycle.
The spatial separation between opposite phases corresponds to half a wavelength, $\lambda / 2 = 0.19\text{ m}$.
Multiplying by two gives the full wavelength $\lambda = 0.38\text{ m}$.
Substituting this wavelength into $v = f\lambda$ with $f = 16\text{ kHz}$ gives the wave speed of $6100\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

## Graphical representations of waves

Wave motion varies simultaneously across space and through time.
Because paper is two-dimensional, a single graph can only show displacement against one variable at a time.
Physicists use two primary graphs: displacement-distance graphs and displacement-time graphs.

Always apply a strict three-step routine when reading any wave graph.
First, read the labels and units on both axes carefully.
Second, extract relevant numerical values such as peak values or repeat intervals.
Third, connect those extracted values to the correct wave definitions.

A displacement-distance graph plots displacement against position along the medium at one single instant in time.
It acts like a photograph or spatial snapshot of the entire wave frozen at time $t_0$.
The distance from the horizontal equilibrium line to a crest gives the amplitude $A$.
The horizontal distance between two consecutive crests gives the wavelength $\lambda$.

A displacement-time graph records the oscillation history of one single particle at a fixed position.
It acts like a video recording of that single particle over elapsed time.
The vertical peak still represents the amplitude $A$.
However, the horizontal distance between consecutive crests now represents the period $T$.

Checking the horizontal axis label determines whether the waveform reveals a wavelength or a period.
Two identical wavy curves can look completely indistinguishable at first glance.
If the axis is distance $x$, the horizontal repeat interval is wavelength $\lambda$.
If the axis is time $t$, the horizontal repeat interval is period $T$.

Never treat a displacement graph as the physical flight path of a particle.
For a transverse wave, the graph happens to resemble the frozen shape of a wavy rope.
For a longitudinal wave, particles oscillate horizontally while displacement is plotted vertically.
In both cases, the graph is an abstract plot of displacement values, not a trajectory.

### Worked Example 4: Extracting wave parameters from distance and time graphs

A wave is represented by two graphs: graph 1 shows displacement against distance, and graph 2 shows displacement against time.
Explain what wave property is determined from the horizontal repeat distance in each graph, and state how to calculate wave speed.

**Answer**
The horizontal repeat distance in graph 1 is the wavelength $\lambda$.
The horizontal repeat distance in graph 2 is the period $T$.
The frequency is $f = 1 / T$, and the wave speed is calculated using $v = f\lambda$.

**Explanation**
Graph 1 plots displacement against distance, showing the spatial profile of the wave at one instant.
The distance between consecutive points in identical phase on this graph is the wavelength $\lambda$.
Graph 2 plots displacement against time for one particle, so its repeat interval is the oscillation period $T$.
Inverting period gives frequency $f = 1 / T$, which combines with wavelength to give speed $v = f\lambda$.

## Instantaneous particle motion on a travelling wave

Cambridge examinations frequently ask students to determine the instantaneous direction of motion of a particle on a travelling wave.
Figure 4.1 shows a transverse wave travelling from left to right with points R and T marked on the profile.
Point R is at a positive displacement crest, while point T is at equilibrium on a rising slope.

Students often guess particle motion based on position, assuming points above the axis move upward.
That guess is incorrect because position does not determine the direction of velocity.
To find particle motion correctly, we use the wave profile shift technique.

Shifting the wave profile slightly in the direction of wave travel reveals the instantaneous motion of each point.
Draw the original wave profile as a solid line at time $t_0$.
Then sketch the same profile shifted forward by a small distance $\Delta x$ as a dashed line at time $t_0 + \Delta t$.

Now look at a chosen point at fixed horizontal position $x$.
If the dashed profile is below the solid curve, the particle must move downward.
If the dashed profile is above the solid curve, the particle must move upward.
At a positive displacement crest, the particle reaches its turning point and has zero instantaneous velocity.

For a wave travelling to the right, any point on an upward slope moves downward.
Conversely, any point on a downward slope moves upward.
Particles at crests and troughs are momentarily stationary before reversing direction.

### Worked Example 5: Determining particle velocity on a travelling wave

A transverse wave travels from left to right across a water surface.
Point T is located at an equilibrium position on a rising slope of the wave profile.
State the direction of motion of the water particle at point T at this instant.

**Answer**
The particle at point T is moving vertically downwards.
An arrow drawn at point T points vertically downwards.

**Explanation**
The wave profile travels horizontally to the right.
A short time later, the profile is shifted slightly to the right.
At the fixed horizontal position of point T, the approaching surface has a negative displacement.
Therefore, the water particle at T must move downward to match the advancing wave profile.

## Longitudinal wave graphs and particle crowding

Longitudinal waves can also be represented on a displacement-distance graph.
Figure 4.1 displays a row of discrete medium particles aligned directly with a sinusoidal displacement graph.
Because longitudinal oscillations are horizontal, plotting vertical displacement requires a strict sign convention.
Displacements to the right of equilibrium are plotted as positive, while displacements to the left are plotted as negative.

Translating displacement values into actual particle positions proves that compressions form at downward zero-crossings.
Consider a particle at a point where the graph crosses the horizontal axis with a negative slope.
Particles just to the left have positive displacement and are shifted rightward toward this point.
Particles just to the right have negative displacement and are shifted leftward toward this point.

At a downward zero-crossing, particles converge from both sides into a high-density compression.
The slope of this displacement curve represents the displacement gradient.
A negative displacement gradient causes particles to crowd together, producing maximum density and pressure.
This directly disproves the common misconception that displacement peaks represent compressions.

Now consider an upward zero-crossing where the graph has a positive displacement gradient.
Particles to the left have negative displacement and are shifted further to the left.
Particles to the right have positive displacement and are shifted further to the right.
Particles diverge away from this point, producing a low-density rarefaction.

What happens at the crests and troughs of a longitudinal displacement graph?
At a positive peak, neighbouring particles are all shifted rightward by almost the same maximum amount.
Because their displacements are nearly equal, the relative spacing between adjacent particles remains normal.
Therefore, particles at displacement peaks and troughs experience normal equilibrium pressure and density.

### Worked Example 6: Identifying compressions and rarefactions from a longitudinal graph

A displacement-distance graph represents a longitudinal wave travelling to the right.
Displacement to the right is defined as positive.
Point A is a downward zero-crossing and point B is a positive displacement crest.
Identify the physical density conditions of the medium at point A and at point B.

**Answer**
Point A is the centre of a compression with maximum density and pressure.
Point B is at normal equilibrium density and pressure.

**Explanation**
At point A, the graph crosses the axis with a negative gradient.
Particles to the left are shifted rightward and particles to the right are shifted leftward, crowding particles together into a compression.
At point B, particles have maximum positive displacement.
Because adjacent particles have identical displacement, their relative separation is unchanged from equilibrium, maintaining normal density.

## Polarisation as a diagnostic test

The fundamental difference in oscillation geometry leads to an important experimental test.
An unpolarised wave has oscillations occurring in multiple planes perpendicular to energy propagation.
A plane-polarised wave has oscillations confined to one single plane containing the direction of energy propagation.
Polarisation is the process that restricts transverse oscillations to a single plane.

Only transverse waves can be polarised.
Longitudinal waves can never be polarised because their particle oscillations are already parallel to energy travel.
There are no sideways oscillations to filter or restrict.

Because sound waves are longitudinal, their oscillations cannot be restricted to a single transverse plane.
In Cambridge examinations, the fact that sound cannot be polarised is diagnostic evidence that sound is a longitudinal wave.
Light and microwaves can be polarised, proving experimentally that electromagnetic radiation is transverse.

This geometric distinction also resolves the puzzle from Oldham's 1897 earthquake analysis.
Transverse S-waves require shear elasticity to propagate, which fluids cannot support.
When seismic waves strike Earth's molten outer core, longitudinal P-waves pass through while transverse S-waves are completely blocked.
Decoding the direction of wave oscillations explains phenomena from guitar strings to the deep interior of our planet.
