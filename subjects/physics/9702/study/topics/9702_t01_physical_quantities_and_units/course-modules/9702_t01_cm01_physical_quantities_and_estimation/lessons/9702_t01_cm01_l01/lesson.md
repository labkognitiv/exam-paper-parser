# Physical quantities: magnitude and unit

## Start with a result

A laboratory display shows **12**. What has been measured?

We cannot tell. It could mean a wait of 12 seconds, a length of 12 metres, or a mass of 12 kilograms. The number tells us **how much**, but it does not tell us what measurement the number belongs to.

Now compare it with **12 s**. The letter **s** tells us that the measurement is a time. Physics measurements need both pieces of information.

## The two parts of a physical quantity

### Physical quantity {#plain-meaning-physical-quantity}

> **Plain meaning:** A **physical quantity** is a property that can be measured and reported.

Length, mass and time are examples of physical quantities. When we report a measurement, every physical quantity has:

1. a **numerical magnitude**, which is the number
2. a **unit**, which names the measurement scale

Consider this record:

\[
\text{length}=1.8\,\mathrm{m}
\]

The **numerical magnitude** is **1.8**. The **unit** is **m**, read as metres. Together, \(1.8\,\mathrm{m}\) report a length.

The word **length** names the quantity. The number and unit give its measured value.

Ask yourself: in \(4.6\,\mathrm{s}\), which part tells you how much, and which part tells you what scale is being used? The number **4.6** tells you how much. The unit **s** tells you that the scale is seconds.

### Keep the pair together

A bare number is incomplete as a physics result. Writing **8.4** after timing an event does not tell a reader whether the result is seconds, metres, kilograms or another measurement.

The unit is not decoration added after the calculation. It is part of the reported physical quantity.

Suppose a timer measures a duration of **8.4 s**. Which record is complete?

- **A.** \(8.4\)
- **B.** \(8.4\,\mathrm{s}\)

**Feedback for A:** Not yet. \(8.4\) gives the numerical magnitude, but it does not state the unit. A reader cannot know what physical quantity was measured.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. \(8.4\) is the numerical magnitude and \(\mathrm{s}\) is the unit, so \(8.4\,\mathrm{s}\) is a complete record of a duration.

## The same number can mean different things

Two records can have the same numerical magnitude but describe different physical quantities:

\[
5\,\mathrm{s}
\]

\[
5\,\mathrm{m}
\]

Both records contain the number **5**. In \(5\,\mathrm{s}\), the unit is seconds, so the record describes a time. In \(5\,\mathrm{m}\), the unit is metres, so the record describes a length.

The number alone does not decide the physical meaning. The unit matters too.

What should you notice before saying that two measurements are the same? Check both the **numerical magnitude** and the **unit**.

## A unit must suit the quantity

When a calculation gives a number, keep the unit from the measurement with the answer. An inappropriate unit changes the meaning of the record.

For example, a mass reported as \(0.75\,\mathrm{m}\) is not correct. Metres are a unit of length, not mass. If the mass has numerical magnitude \(0.75\) and unit kilograms, the complete result is:

\[
0.75\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

The **magnitude** can be correct while the full result is still wrong because its unit is wrong.

### Worked example 1: sharing a measured mass

Four identical containers have a total mass of **2.8 kg**. Find the mass of one container.

First decide what is being shared. The total **mass** is shared equally between four identical containers. Keep kg attached to the total.

\[
\text{mass of one container}=\frac{2.8\,\mathrm{kg}}{4}
\]

\[
\text{mass of one container}=0.7\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

**Answer: \(0.7\,\mathrm{kg}\).**

The numerical magnitude is **0.7**. The unit is **kg**, read as kilograms.

Check by rebuilding the total:

\[
4\times0.7\,\mathrm{kg}=2.8\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

The check matches the given mass. Writing only \(0.7\) would leave the mass incomplete.

## A different measurement can use the same arithmetic

The arithmetic can look similar while the physical quantity is different. The unit tells us which quantity the answer reports.

### Worked example 2: sharing a measured duration

Five equal intervals have a total duration of **15 s**. Find the duration of one interval.

This time the total is a **duration**, so retain seconds throughout the calculation.

\[
\text{duration of one interval}=\frac{15\,\mathrm{s}}{5}
\]

\[
\text{duration of one interval}=3\,\mathrm{s}
\]

**Answer: \(3\,\mathrm{s}\).**

The numerical magnitude is **3**. The unit is **s**, read as seconds.

Check:

\[
5\times3\,\mathrm{s}=15\,\mathrm{s}
\]

The answer is not \(3\,\mathrm{kg}\) or \(3\,\mathrm{m}\). The number 3 can occur in many measurements, but the unit keeps the meaning accurate.

Two equal intervals have a total duration of \(6\,\mathrm{s}\). A student writes the duration of one interval as \(3\,\mathrm{m}\). Which response is right?

- **A.** The number 3 is right, but the unit must be \(\mathrm{s}\), so the answer is \(3\,\mathrm{s}\).
- **B.** The number 3 is right, so \(3\,\mathrm{m}\) is also right.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Dividing \(6\,\mathrm{s}\) by 2 gives \(3\,\mathrm{s}\). The unit must continue to describe a duration.

**Feedback for B:** The numerical magnitude is right, but the record is not. Metres describe length, whereas the original measurement is a duration in seconds.

## A common mistake: treating the unit as optional

It is tempting to stop when a calculator shows a number. The arithmetic may be complete, but the physics report is not.

If the result of a length calculation is \(1.25\,\mathrm{m}\), then:

- \(1.25\) is incomplete because the unit is missing.
- \(1.25\,\mathrm{s}\) is wrong because seconds do not describe a length.
- \(1.25\,\mathrm{m}\) is complete because the numerical magnitude and appropriate unit are together.

Before you move on, ask two questions: **What is the number? What is the unit?**

## Core recap

- A **physical quantity** is reported with a **numerical magnitude** and a **unit**.
- The numerical magnitude tells you how much. The unit identifies the measurement scale.
- A bare number, such as \(8.4\), is not a complete physical result.
- Equal numerical magnitudes can describe different quantities, such as \(5\,\mathrm{s}\) and \(5\,\mathrm{m}\).
- Carry the appropriate unit through a calculation and include it in the final answer.
