# Estimating physical quantities

An unlabeled suitcase is waiting beside an airport desk. You do not have a balance, but you need to decide whether it is more likely to have a mass near **0.05 kg**, **5 kg**, or **500 kg**.

You can make a useful decision before measuring. You use what you know about familiar objects, compare sizes or loads, and reject values that do not fit the situation. That is an **estimate**.

## What an estimate is for

In the previous lesson, you met the idea that every **physical quantity** needs two parts: a **numerical magnitude** and a **unit**. For example, `3` is only a bare number. `3 kg` tells us that the quantity is mass and gives its size in kilograms.

An **estimate** is an approximate value. It is not a random number and it does not need to match a later measurement exactly. It should be a value that is physically believable.

When an exam asks for a reasonable estimate, it is testing your sense of scale. You need to choose a value with the right kind of unit and a believable size.

Ask yourself: *What real object or event do I already know that is similar to this one?*

## A repeatable route

Use this route whenever you estimate a physical quantity.

1. Name the **quantity** to be estimated and choose an appropriate **unit**.
2. Choose a familiar **benchmark** of the same kind of quantity.
3. Compare the target with the benchmark. Is it smaller, similar, or larger?
4. State a simple approximate value with its unit.
5. Check the answer against the real situation. Reject scales that are clearly too small or too large.

A **benchmark** is a familiar reference that helps you judge size. A known **1 kg** packet of food can help you estimate a bag's mass. Your own height can help you estimate the height of a doorway. The time for a short spoken word can help you estimate a brief event.

The benchmark does not have to be identical to the target. It only has to give you a sensible comparison.

### Worked example: a lunchbox mass

A packed lunchbox contains a sandwich, fruit, and a filled drink bottle. Estimate its **mass** in kilograms.

First choose a benchmark. A labelled **1 kg** food packet is a familiar mass. The lunchbox feels lighter than several such packets, but it is much heavier than a few grams.

So a sensible estimate is:

\[
\text{mass of lunchbox} \approx \mathbf{1\ kg}
\]

The symbol \(\approx\) means "is approximately equal to". It tells the reader that this is an estimate, not a balance reading.

Now check the scale. A value of **0.001 kg** is only one gram, which is far too small for a packed lunchbox. A value of **100 kg** is comparable with a person, so it is far too large. **1 kg** sits between these impossible scales and fits the situation.

**Teacher question:** Would a soft toy of similar size always have the same mass as the lunchbox?

No. Size can help, but what an object contains also matters. The packed food and drink make the lunchbox much more massive than an empty soft toy of similar size.

## Bracket the scale before choosing a value

Sometimes you cannot remember a close benchmark. You can still find a reasonable estimate by testing widely separated scales. This is called **bracketing**.

Suppose you estimate the height of a classroom door in metres. Compare **0.2 m**, **2 m**, and **20 m**.

- **0.2 m** is shorter than a ruler. A person cannot walk through it.
- **20 m** is about the height of a multi-storey building. It is far too tall for a classroom door.
- **2 m** is a little taller than many people. It fits a doorway.

So a sensible estimate is about **2 m**. Notice the order of thought: reject impossible scales first, then choose the value that fits.

**Teacher question:** A desk is about the width of two large textbooks placed side by side. Which estimate is more believable for its width?

- **A:** \(0.6\ \mathrm{m}\)
- **B:** \(60\ \mathrm{m}\)

**Feedback for A:** Correct. A width of **0.6 m** is similar to the size of a desk. It is also much smaller than the width of a classroom.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. **60 m** is the length of several classrooms placed end to end. The scale is far too large for one desk.

## Estimate a short time by comparing events

Estimation is not only for objects. You can also estimate **time**.

Imagine the time taken for one blink. A blink is much shorter than waiting for a kettle to boil, but it is long enough to notice. A reasonable estimate is about **0.2 s**.

Here, \(\mathrm{s}\) means seconds. The value **0.2** means two tenths of one second.

### Worked example: time for ten taps

A student taps a pencil on a desk at a steady, comfortable pace. Estimate the time for **ten taps**.

Choose a benchmark. One steady tap can be close to **one tap each second**. Ten taps should therefore take about ten times that time.

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{estimated time for ten taps}
&\approx 10 \times 1\ \mathrm{s} \\
&\approx \mathbf{10\ s}
\end{aligned}
\]

The multiplication is appropriate because there are ten similar intervals. The estimate is not a claim that every tap takes exactly one second.

Check the result. **0.01 s** would be too brief to make ten separate comfortable taps. **1000 s** is more than fifteen minutes, which is much too long. **10 s** is believable.

A common mistake is to choose a number because it looks neat, without checking what it means in real life. Always picture the event.

## A spatial estimate can use a simple shape

For a **volume** estimate, imagine an object fitting inside a familiar box or cube. Volume describes how much three-dimensional space an object occupies.

The unit \(\mathrm{cm^3}\) is read as **cubic centimetres**. It means a volume built from small cubes that are each one centimetre long, one centimetre wide, and one centimetre high.

### Worked example: volume of a melon

Estimate the volume of a medium melon in cubic centimetres.

Imagine a box that is about **12 cm** long, **12 cm** wide, and **12 cm** high. A melon is rounded, so it would not fill every corner of this box. First find the box's volume as a scale reference.

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{box volume}
&= 12\ \mathrm{cm} \times 12\ \mathrm{cm} \times 12\ \mathrm{cm} \\
&= 144\ \mathrm{cm^2} \times 12\ \mathrm{cm} \\
&= 1728\ \mathrm{cm^3}
\end{aligned}
\]

The rounded melon takes up less space than the whole box. A reasonable estimate is therefore about:

\[
\text{volume of melon} \approx \mathbf{1300\ \mathrm{cm^3}}
\]

Check the scale. **13 cm³** is only a small spoonful, so it is far too small. **130 000 cm³** would be a very large container, so it is far too large. **1300 cm³** is of the same general scale as the imagined box.

The multiplication here is a geometry bridge for making an estimate. You do not yet need to classify units or learn unit prefixes. Those are the next lesson's job.

## Do not add pretend precision

An estimate begins with rough evidence. Your written answer should be just as honest as your evidence.

If a suitcase seems to have a mass near three kilograms, write **about 3 kg**. Writing **3.0000 kg** suggests that you measured it very precisely. You did not.

More digits do not make an estimate more scientific. A stronger estimate comes from a clear benchmark and a good physical check.

**Teacher question:** You compare a bag with familiar **1 kg** packets and decide it is near three packets. Which final record is better?

- **A:** about \(3\ \mathrm{kg}\)
- **B:** \(3.0000\ \mathrm{kg}\)

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The word "about" matches the rough comparison. The unit \(\mathrm{kg}\) states that the quantity is mass.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. The extra zeroes claim a precision that the packet comparison cannot support. They do not improve the physical evidence.

## When an estimate is not a guess

It is tempting to think that any nearby number is acceptable. That misses the key decision. A good estimate has a short physical story behind it:

- a familiar benchmark;
- a comparison with the target;
- an appropriate unit; and
- a check that rules out impossible scales.

For example, saying a loaded suitcase has a mass of **5 kg** is not useful by itself. Saying it is about **5 kg** because it feels like several known **1 kg** packets, and is clearly not light enough to be grams or heavy enough to be hundreds of kilograms, shows the required reasoning.

## Core recap

- An **estimate** is an approximate, physically believable value.
- Keep the two parts of a physical quantity: a **numerical magnitude** and a **unit**.
- Use a familiar **benchmark**, compare scales, then state a simple approximate value.
- **Bracket** the scale by rejecting values that are clearly too small or too large.
- Use `about` or \(\approx\) when the evidence is rough. Do not add unsupported digits.
- Check what the answer means in the real situation before accepting it.
