# Scalar and vector quantities

## What this lesson is about

A satnav can report that a cyclist is moving at **18 m s⁻¹**. That tells us
how fast the cyclist is moving. It does not tell us whether the cyclist is
heading east, west, uphill or downhill.

Now imagine that the satnav reports a **velocity of 18 m s⁻¹ east**. The
number and unit still matter, but the direction is also part of the
information. Physics has two names for these different kinds of physical
quantity: **scalar** and **vector**.

You already know that a physical quantity needs a numerical **magnitude** and
a **unit**. Magnitude means size. This lesson asks one extra question: does the
quantity also need a direction before its physical meaning is complete?

By the end, you should be able to state the exact definitions, classify common
quantities, and write a complete description of a scalar or vector quantity.

## A scalar is complete without a direction

<a id="definition-scalar-quantity"></a>

> **Definition to learn: Scalar quantity.** a physical quantity that has magnitude only

The exact definition says **magnitude only**. A scalar still has a number and
an appropriate unit. For example, **mass = 2.4 kg**, **time = 35 s** and
**temperature = 293 K** are complete scalar descriptions. Adding a compass
direction to any of them would not add a physical property of that quantity.

Ask yourself: would a different direction make this quantity a different
physical quantity? For mass, time and temperature, the answer is no. A mass of
2.4 kg is not made into a different mass by saying "east".

Common scalar quantities in this course include **mass**, **time**,
**temperature**, **distance**, **speed**, **energy**, **work**, **power** and
**electrical resistance**. Their units can differ, but direction is not part of
their complete meaning.

Notice that **speed** is scalar. Saying that a cyclist has a speed of
18 m s⁻¹ tells us how fast the cyclist moves. It does not identify a direction.

### Worked example: classify by what must be reported

Classify **power**, **time** and **speed**.

For each quantity, ask whether a direction is needed to make the report
complete.

- A lamp with **power = 60 W** is described completely. The lamp does not have
  a northward or westward power. Therefore **power is scalar**.
- A journey lasting **time = 12 s** is described completely. A duration has no
  physical direction. Therefore **time is scalar**.
- A runner with **speed = 5.0 m s⁻¹** is described completely as to how fast
  the runner moves. Therefore **speed is scalar**.

The unit does not decide the category. The question is whether direction is
needed by the physical quantity itself.

Suppose a display says **speed = 6.0 m s⁻¹**. Is that a complete report?

- **A:** Yes, because speed is scalar.
- **B:** No, because every moving object needs a direction.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Speed has magnitude only. The value 6.0 and the
unit m s⁻¹ state the speed completely.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. The object may be moving in a direction, but
the quantity **speed** does not include that direction. **Velocity**, not
speed, needs direction.

## A vector needs a direction as well

<a id="definition-vector-quantity"></a>

> **Definition to learn: Vector quantity.** a physical quantity that has magnitude and direction

A vector has a magnitude, a unit where appropriate, and a direction. Leaving
out the direction leaves the description incomplete.

For example, **force = 12 N** is not a complete vector description. A force of
12 N to the right and a force of 12 N to the left have the same magnitude, but
they are physically different forces. A complete report could be
**force = 12 N to the right**.

The direction can be given in words such as **upward**, **downward**, **east**
or **west**. Later lessons will show ways to represent and combine vectors.
For now, the important idea is that direction is required.

Common vector quantities in this course include **displacement**, **velocity**,
**acceleration**, **force**, **weight** and **momentum**. Their directions can
change while their magnitudes stay the same. That changes the vector.

### Worked example: same magnitude, different vector

Two students pull a crate. Each student pulls with a force of **40 N**.

- Student A pulls **40 N east**.
- Student B pulls **40 N west**.

The two forces have equal magnitudes because both are 40 N. They are not the
same vector because their directions are opposite.

So **force is vector**. The number 40 and the unit N are not enough on their
own. The direction is a required part of the report.

Compare this with **distance of 40 m**. Distance does not need east or west, so
distance is scalar. **Displacement of 40 m** does need a direction, so
displacement is vector. They may use the same unit, metre, but they are
different physical quantities.

## A reliable classification test

When you meet a quantity in a question, use this three-step test.

1. **Name the physical quantity.** Do not decide from the situation alone.
2. **Ask whether magnitude and unit are enough.** If they are enough, it is a
   scalar.
3. **Ask whether direction is also needed.** If direction is needed, it is a
   vector.

Both categories describe physical quantities. Both have a magnitude. Both can
have an appropriate unit. **Direction is the extra property that separates a
vector from a scalar.**

### Worked example: a mixed list

Classify **energy**, **acceleration**, **force**, **temperature**, **momentum**
and **electrical resistance**.

Work through them one at a time.

- **Energy** has a magnitude and unit, such as 120 J. It does not point in a
  direction. It is a **scalar**.
- **Acceleration** describes a change in velocity. A complete acceleration
  needs a direction, such as 3.0 m s⁻² downward. It is a **vector**.
- **Force** needs a direction, such as 8.0 N upward. It is a **vector**.
- **Temperature** has no physical direction. A temperature of 295 K is
  complete. It is a **scalar**.
- **Momentum** needs a direction of motion, such as 4.0 kg m s⁻¹ west. It is a
  **vector**.
- **Electrical resistance** has a magnitude and unit, such as 12 Ω. A compass
  direction would add no meaning to resistance. It is a **scalar**.

The sorted result is:

- **Scalars:** energy, temperature, electrical resistance
- **Vectors:** acceleration, force, momentum

This is the reasoning used in examination classification tables. Do not rely
only on memorising a list. Use the direction test when an unfamiliar example
appears.

## A common mistake: the unit or the story does not decide

It is tempting to think that every quantity in a motion question must be a
vector. That is not true. A moving car can have vector quantities such as
velocity and acceleration, and scalar quantities such as time, speed, energy
and distance.

It is also tempting to use the unit as a shortcut. That fails for **speed** and
**velocity**. Both can use m s⁻¹. Speed is scalar, while velocity is vector.
The unit tells you the kind of measurement, but it does not by itself tell you
whether direction belongs to the quantity.

Teacher question: if a physical quantity is written in newtons, must it be a
vector? Think about the quantity, not only its unit. A force is a vector
because direction is part of a force. The classification follows physical
meaning.

Suppose a record states **displacement = 4.5 m**. Is the record complete?

- **A:** Yes, because it gives a magnitude and the unit metre.
- **B:** No, because displacement also needs a direction.

**Feedback for A:** Not correct. Magnitude and unit are necessary, but
displacement is vector. The record could describe many different movements.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. A complete statement could be
**displacement = 4.5 m north**. The direction makes clear which displacement
is meant.

## Reporting scalar and vector quantities completely

Earlier in this topic, you learned to include a numerical magnitude and unit
when reporting a physical quantity. Keep that habit here.

For a **scalar**, report:

- **magnitude** and **unit**

For a **vector**, report:

- **magnitude**, **unit**, and **direction**

### Worked example: audit four reports

A lab book contains these entries.

1. time = **20 s**
2. force = **6.0 N**
3. velocity = **3.5 m s⁻¹ west**
4. displacement = **2.0 m upward**

Decide which reports are complete.

**1. Time = 20 s** is complete. Time is scalar, so magnitude and unit are
enough.

**2. Force = 6.0 N** is incomplete. Force is vector. It needs a direction,
such as **6.0 N downward**.

**3. Velocity = 3.5 m s⁻¹ west** is complete. Velocity is vector, and its
magnitude, unit and direction are all present.

**4. Displacement = 2.0 m upward** is complete. Displacement is vector, and
the direction upward is included.

The decision order matters. First identify the quantity. Then decide scalar or
vector. Finally, check whether all the needed information has been written.

## Core recap

- A **scalar quantity** is **a physical quantity that has magnitude only**. A
  complete scalar report needs magnitude and unit.
- A **vector quantity** is **a physical quantity that has magnitude and
  direction**. A complete vector report needs magnitude, unit and direction.
- Both scalars and vectors have magnitude. Direction is the extra requirement
  for a vector.
- **Speed** and **velocity** can share the unit m s⁻¹, but speed is scalar and
  velocity is vector. Classify by physical meaning, not by unit alone.
- This lesson identifies vectors. The next lesson uses the fact that vectors
  have direction when adding, subtracting and resolving coplanar vectors.
