# Gravitational potential: energy per kilogram

## Starting from very far away

Imagine a small space probe far from every planet. It is so far away that the gravitational pull from the planet of interest is negligible. In gravitational-potential questions, we call this position **infinity** and assign it a gravitational potential of zero.

Now bring the probe closer to the planet. Gravity attracts the probe towards the planet. The probe can move inwards because gravity does work on it. This changes the probe's energy.

**Gravitational potential** tells us about this energy change for each kilogram of mass. It is a property of a position in a gravitational field, not a property of the test mass placed there.

Teacher question: two test masses of different mass are placed at the same point near Earth. Do they have the same gravitational potential? Yes. They are at the same position in the same field, so the potential is the same even though their potential energies would be different.

<a id="definition-gravitational-potential"></a>
> **Definition to learn: gravitational potential.**
> work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point

This is the exact definition to learn. Break it into small parts:

- **Work done** is energy transferred by a force.
- **Per unit mass** means divide the work done by the mass. The standard unit of gravitational potential is joules per kilogram, \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).
- A **small test mass** is chosen so that it does not noticeably alter the gravitational field being investigated.
- **From infinity** gives every calculation the same reference position. The gravitational potential at infinity is defined as zero.
- **The point** is the position in the field where we want the potential.

The definition does not mean that potential is a force. It is an energy change for each kilogram. Its unit confirms this:

\[
\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{kg}}
=\frac{\mathrm{N\,m}}{\mathrm{kg}}
=\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}\,m}}{\mathrm{kg}}
=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}
\]

Read \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) as "joules per kilogram". The equivalent base unit \(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\) is useful for checking calculations, but \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) makes the energy-per-mass meaning clearer.

## Why gravitational potential is negative

Near an isolated mass, gravitational potential is negative. This sign is not arbitrary.

Start at infinity, where the potential is zero. Gravity is attractive, so it pulls an object inwards. As an object moves from infinity towards the mass, gravity does positive work on it. If the object is brought in slowly and kept from speeding up, an external agent must do negative work by removing energy from it.

The work done per unit mass in bringing the test mass from infinity is therefore negative. So the gravitational potential at a finite distance from an isolated mass is negative.

Teacher question: if the reference value at infinity is zero, should a point closer to an attracting planet have a positive or a negative gravitational potential? Negative. Energy has been released as the mass moves inward under gravity.

Do not say only "gravity is negative". Gravity is a force with a direction. The potential is negative because of the chosen zero reference at infinity and the work associated with moving towards an attractive mass.

An object is moved slowly from infinity towards a planet. Which statement is correct?

- **A:** Gravitational potential becomes negative because gravity does work as the object moves inward.
- **B:** Gravitational potential becomes negative because the object's mass becomes negative.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The object has positive mass throughout. The negative sign comes from the energy reference: potential is zero at infinity, and the attractive gravitational interaction releases energy as the object moves inward.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. Mass does not become negative. The sign describes the gravitational potential relative to the chosen zero at infinity, not the sign of the object's mass.

## Using the words "per unit mass"

The definition gives a straightforward numerical route when the work and mass are known. Divide the relevant work by the test mass. Keep the sign of the work.

### Worked example: work done for a small test mass

A \(0.300\,\mathrm{kg}\) test mass is brought slowly from infinity to a point near an isolated planet. The external agent does \(-2.40\times10^6\,\mathrm{J}\) of work. Find the gravitational potential at the point.

The question gives work done and mass. "Per unit mass" means divide the work by the mass:

\[
\text{gravitational potential}
=\frac{\text{work done}}{\text{mass}}
\]

Substitute with the negative sign retained:

\[
\text{gravitational potential}
=\frac{-2.40\times10^6\,\mathrm{J}}{0.300\,\mathrm{kg}}
\]

\[
\text{gravitational potential}
=-8.00\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}
\]

**Answer: \(-8.00\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).**

The negative answer is expected. The point is finite distance from an attracting planet, while infinity is the zero reference. The unit is \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\), so the calculation has correctly found energy change per kilogram.

### A common sign mistake

Sometimes a student divides the magnitudes and writes a positive answer. That loses the physical meaning. The magnitude tells us how much energy is involved per kilogram. The negative sign tells us that the position is below the zero-potential reference at infinity.

Also distinguish **gravitational potential** from **gravitational potential energy**. Potential is energy per kilogram at a position. Potential energy depends on both position and the object's mass. The exact relationship and the point-mass potential equation are taught in the next lesson.

## Moving away from a planet

It can be helpful to reason in the opposite direction. To move a mass slowly from a point near a planet out to infinity, an external agent must supply energy against the attractive gravitational force. The gravitational potential increases from a negative value to zero.

### Worked example: use the zero at infinity

A \(2.00\,\mathrm{kg}\) test mass is moved slowly from point P near an isolated planet to infinity. The external agent does \(+3.60\times10^6\,\mathrm{J}\) of work. Find the gravitational potential at P.

The positive external work is the change in energy per kilogram from P to infinity:

\[
\frac{+3.60\times10^6\,\mathrm{J}}{2.00\,\mathrm{kg}}
=+1.80\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}
\]

This is the increase in gravitational potential from P to infinity. At infinity the potential is \(0\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\). Therefore P must have been the same amount below zero:

\[
\text{potential at P}=-1.80\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}
\]

**Answer: \(-1.80\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).**

Check the direction of the energy transfer. Moving away from an attracting planet needs positive external work, so the potential rises towards zero. The starting point must be negative.

Teacher question: is \(-1.80\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\) lower or higher than \(-8.00\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\)? It is higher because it is closer to zero.

A point has gravitational potential \(-3.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\). A second point has gravitational potential \(-8.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\). Which point is closer to the zero reference at infinity?

- **A:** The point with \(-3.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).
- **B:** The point with \(-8.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\).

**Feedback for A:** Correct. \(-3.0\times10^6\) is less negative and therefore closer to zero. For an isolated attracting mass, positions farther away have potentials closer to the zero value at infinity.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. \(-8.0\times10^6\) is more negative, so it is farther below the zero reference. It corresponds to a position with a larger energy change per kilogram from infinity.

## Core recap

- **Gravitational potential** is the **work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point**.
- Its unit is **\(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}\)**, meaning energy change for each kilogram.
- Infinity is the chosen zero-potential reference.
- Near an isolated attracting mass, gravitational potential is **negative** because energy is released as a mass moves inward from infinity.
- Keep the sign when dividing work by mass.
- The next lesson introduces the point-mass equation for gravitational potential and connects potential to gravitational potential energy.
