# Gravitational fields: force per unit mass and field lines

## Start with a situation

A dropped object is pulled towards a planet even though nothing touches it. We describe the changed space around the planet as a **gravitational field**.

What would happen if you placed a small mass near a planet? It would feel a gravitational force towards the planet. A larger test mass feels a larger force, so force alone is not a fair way to describe the field. We need force for each kilogram of test mass.

## A field of force

### Gravitational field {#definition-gravitational-field}

> **Definition to learn: gravitational field.** a region of space where a mass experiences a gravitational force

A **region of space** is an area or volume around an object. A gravitational field is not an object that you can see or hold. It is a model that tells us where a mass would experience a pull.

The mass that creates the field is the **source mass**. A small mass used to find out about the field is a **test mass**. We keep it small so its own gravity does not noticeably change the situation. Gravity is attractive, so the force on a test mass points towards the source mass.

Ask yourself: if a test mass is twice as large, should the force on it be twice as large? Yes. This is why we divide by mass when measuring field strength.

## Gravitational field strength

### Gravitational field strength {#definition-gravitational-field-strength}

> **Definition to learn: gravitational field strength.** gravitational force per unit mass acting on a small test mass

The words **per unit mass** mean "for each kilogram". A field strength of **\(8\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\)** means each **1 kg** test mass would feel an **8 N** gravitational force at that point.

The symbol **\(g\)** means gravitational field strength. Do not confuse it with a mass in grams. Here, \(g\) is a property of the field at one position.

### Formula for field strength {#formula-gravitational-field-strength}

> **Formula to learn: Gravitational field strength.**
>
> \[
> g = \frac{F}{m}
> \]

Here **\(g\)** is field strength in \(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\), **\(F\)** is the gravitational force in \(\mathrm{N}\), and **\(m\)** is the test mass in \(\mathrm{kg}\). Use this formula when \(F\) is the **gravitational** force on the small test mass. Do not substitute a contact force or a resultant force without first establishing that it is the gravitational force.

The unit follows from the formula:

\[
\frac{\mathrm{N}}{\mathrm{kg}}=\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}
\]

Read this as "newtons per kilogram".

### Worked example 1: finding field strength

A small test mass of **2.0 kg** experiences a gravitational force of **18 N**. Find the gravitational field strength.

The question asks for force per unit mass, so use \(g=F/m\).

\[
g=\frac{F}{m}
\]

\[
g=\frac{18\,\mathrm{N}}{2.0\,\mathrm{kg}}
\]

\[
g=9.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}
\]

**Answer: \(g=9.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).**

Check the meaning. Each kilogram would feel **9.0 N**. A 2.0 kg test mass therefore feels \(2.0\times9.0=18\,\mathrm{N}\), which matches the data.

At a point in a field, a **0.50 kg** test mass feels **4.0 N**. Which statement is correct?

- **A.** The field strength is \(8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).
- **B.** The field strength is \(4.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).

**Feedback for A:** Correct. \(g=4.0\,\mathrm{N}/0.50\,\mathrm{kg}\), so \(g=8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).

**Feedback for B:** Not this time. \(4.0\,\mathrm{N}\) is the force on the whole 0.50 kg mass. Divide by mass to find force for each kilogram.

### Worked example 2: separating the field from the test mass

At one point near a moon, a **0.50 kg** test mass feels **4.0 N** towards the moon. A **2.0 kg** test mass is placed at the same point. Find its gravitational force.

First find the field strength from the first mass.

\[
g=\frac{4.0\,\mathrm{N}}{0.50\,\mathrm{kg}}=8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}
\]

The field at that point has not changed. Rearrange \(g=F/m\) by multiplying both sides by \(m\).

\[
F=gm
\]

\[
F=(8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}})(2.0\,\mathrm{kg})
\]

\[
F=16\,\mathrm{N}
\]

**Answer: \(16\,\mathrm{N}\) towards the moon.**

Check the unit:

\[
\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\times\mathrm{kg}=\mathrm{N}
\]

The kilograms cancel. The force is four times larger because the mass is four times larger. The field strength remains \(8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\).

## Showing a field with field lines

We cannot draw gravity itself, but **field lines** communicate what the field would do to a small test mass. An arrow on a field line shows the direction of the gravitational force on a small test mass at that point.

For an isolated spherical source mass, every arrow points **inwards**, towards its centre. Draw lines around the sphere that point towards the centre from all directions. These are **radial** field lines because they lie along radii that meet at the centre.

Use these drawing rules:

1. Put arrows on field lines. They show force direction on a small test mass.
2. Around an isolated spherical mass, every arrow points towards the centre.
3. Do not draw field lines crossing. At one point, a force cannot have two different directions.

A field line is not the path that a released object must follow. It is a direction map. An object with sideways motion can follow a curved path even though the gravitational force at each instant points towards the source mass.

Suppose a small test mass is at point P to the right of an isolated spherical planet. Which arrow represents the field direction at P?

- **A.** An arrow pointing left, towards the planet's centre.
- **B.** An arrow pointing right, away from the planet.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Gravity is attractive, so the field direction is towards the source mass.

**Feedback for B:** This reverses the direction. An outward arrow would show a repulsive interaction, not the gravitational pull from an isolated mass.

## A common confusion: field and field strength

The **gravitational field** is the region where a mass experiences a gravitational force. **Gravitational field strength** tells you how much gravitational force acts for each kilogram at one position.

So "there is a gravitational field around the planet" describes where gravity acts. "The field strength is \(8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\)" gives a numerical measure of the pull at one point.

Another error is to use total force as field strength. If a **3.0 kg** test mass feels **24 N**, then

\[
g=\frac{24\,\mathrm{N}}{3.0\,\mathrm{kg}}=8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}
\]

The field is described by \(8.0\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\). The \(24\,\mathrm{N}\) is the force on that particular test mass.

## Core recap

- A **gravitational field** is **a region of space where a mass experiences a gravitational force**.
- **Gravitational field strength** is **gravitational force per unit mass acting on a small test mass**.
- Use **\(g=F/m\)**, with \(g\) in \(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\), \(F\) in \(\mathrm{N}\), and \(m\) in \(\mathrm{kg}\).
- A larger test mass feels a larger force at the same point, but the field strength there is unchanged.
- Field-line arrows show force direction on a small test mass. Around an isolated spherical mass, they point towards the centre.

You are now ready to treat a uniform sphere as a point mass outside the sphere and use Newton's law of gravitation.
