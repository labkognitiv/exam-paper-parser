# Centre of gravity and the moment of a force

## The puzzle of the unbalanced beam

Around 250 BC, Archimedes investigated how uneven objects balance on a support.
A straight uniform stick balances right at its physical middle.
An uneven object, like a heavy wooden mallet, tips immediately when supported at its midpoint.
Why does this happen?

Earth pulls downward on every piece of matter in an object simultaneously.
Because an uneven object contains more matter in some sections than others, each section feels a different downward tug.
Adding up thousands of tiny pulls across an irregular body seemed impossible to ancient builders.

Archimedes realized that you do not need to track each piece separately.
All those separate pulls combine into a single overall effect.
They behave as if the entire downward pull gathers at one specific point.

Support the object at that unique balance point, and it rests horizontally without tipping.
Furthermore, the turning tendency of any force depends on the pull and its distance from the pivot.

How can an extended body with weight distributed along its length be represented as a single force?
How do we calculate the exact turning tendency produced by that force about any pivot?

## Force arrows and weight

A force is a vector quantity with both magnitude and direction.
We represent a force on a diagram using a straight arrow pointing in its direction of action.
Every force has an imaginary straight line extending infinitely along its direction.
We call this extended line the line of action of the force.

Mass measures the amount of matter in an object and has the unit kilogram (kg).
Near Earth's surface, gravity exerts a downward force on each kilogram of mass.
This gravitational field strength is represented by $g$, where $g = 9.81\text{ N kg}^{-1}$.

Weight is the downward gravitational force acting on an object.
We calculate weight using the formula:
$$W = mg$$
In this formula, $W$ is weight in newtons (N).

The symbol $m$ is mass in kilograms (kg).
The symbol $g$ is gravitational field strength in newtons per kilogram ($\text{N kg}^{-1}$).
Weight always acts vertically downward toward the centre of the Earth.

## The centre of gravity

Gravity does not pull on an object at only one spot.
It pulls downward on every individual particle of matter throughout the body.
Drawing thousands of tiny arrows would make force diagrams cluttered and difficult to use.

Physicists simplify this by using a model.
We replace all the distributed gravitational pulls with one equivalent downward force.
We draw this single force, the total weight $W$, acting through one unique point.

> **Centre of gravity**
> the point through which the whole weight of a body may be considered to act

In a uniform object, mass is spread evenly throughout its volume.
The centre of gravity of a uniform symmetrical object lies at its geometric centre.
For example, a uniform meter rule has its centre of gravity at the 50 cm mark.

In contrast, a non-uniform object has more mass in some regions than in others.
Its centre of gravity shifts away from the geometric midpoint toward the region of greater mass concentration.
The balance point always follows the mass distribution.

Consider a broom with a light wooden handle and a heavy brush head.
If you support the broom at its middle, it tips down toward the heavy brush head.
To balance the broom, you must move the support closer to the brush head.

The centre of gravity does not even need to lie inside the material of the object.
For a uniform circular ring or a hollow cylinder, the point lies in empty space at the centre.
No matter where it lies, the whole weight may be considered to act through this point.

### Worked Example 1: Locating the centre of gravity

A steel pry bar tapers from a thick, heavy base to a thin, narrow tip.
The bar has a total length of 1.20 m.
Explain whether its centre of gravity is located at the 0.60 m midpoint, closer to the thick base, or closer to the thin tip.

**Answer**
The centre of gravity is located closer to the thick base.

**Explanation**
The thick base contains more steel per unit length than the thin tip.
Because mass is distributed unevenly, the bar is non-uniform.
The centre of gravity always shifts toward the region of greater mass concentration.
Therefore, the centre of gravity lies between the thick base and the 0.60 m midpoint.

## Turning effects and the pivot

Forces do more than push objects in straight lines.
A force can also cause an object to rotate or turn.
The fixed point or axis about which an object turns is called a pivot.
A door hinge is a familiar example of a pivot.

Pushing a door close to the hinge requires a large force to make it move.
Pushing the outer edge near the handle opens the door easily with the same force.
The turning effect depends on the size of the force and how far it acts from the pivot.

However, measuring along the object is not always correct.
The turning effect depends specifically on the shortest distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.

The shortest distance between a point and a straight line is always perpendicular to that line.
Perpendicular means meeting at a right angle, or $90^\circ$.
We write this perpendicular distance as $d_\perp$.

## Defining the moment of a force

The scientific term for the turning effect of a force is its moment.
Physicists define this quantity precisely using the applied force and its perpendicular distance.

> **Moment of a force**
> the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force

We calculate the moment using the formula:
$$M = Fd_\perp$$
In this formula, $M$ represents the moment of the force.
The symbol $F$ represents the magnitude of the applied force.
The symbol $d_\perp$ represents the perpendicular distance from the chosen point to the line of action of the force.

The word product means the result of multiplying two quantities together.
Force is measured in newtons (N) and distance is measured in metres (m).
Multiplying newtons by metres gives the coherent SI unit of moment, the newton metre ($\text{N m}$).

We can also express the newton metre in SI base units.
Recall from mechanics that $1\text{ N} = 1\text{ kg m s}^{-2}$.
Multiplying by metres gives $\text{kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$.

Energy also has base units of $\text{kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$ and is measured in joules (J).
However, moments must never be written in joules.
A moment is a turning tendency about a point, not an amount of energy transferred.

A moment always acts in a specific rotational direction about the pivot.
We describe this rotational sense as either clockwise or anticlockwise.
Clockwise means turning in the same direction as the hands of an analogue clock.
Anticlockwise means turning in the opposite direction.

An object does not need to be moving for a moment to exist.
The moment measures the turning tendency produced by the force.

### Worked Example 2: Calculating a direct moment

A mechanic applies a downward vertical force of 45 N to the end of a horizontal spanner.
The line of action of the force is 0.28 m to the right of the central nut.
Calculate the moment of the force about the nut, and state its direction.

**Answer**
$$\text{Moment} = 13\text{ N m clockwise}$$

**Explanation**
First, identify the pivot and the line of action.
The central nut acts as the pivot point.
The force acts vertically downward at 0.28 m to the right of the nut.

Because the spanner is horizontal and the force is vertical, they meet at $90^\circ$.
Thus, the perpendicular distance $d_\perp$ is $0.28\text{ m}$.

Now apply the moment formula:
$$M = Fd_\perp = 45\text{ N} \times 0.28\text{ m} = 12.6\text{ N m}$$
Rounding to two significant figures gives $13\text{ N m}$.
A downward push to the right of the pivot tends to turn the spanner clockwise.

## Forces that produce zero moment

What happens if you push a door directly toward its hinges along the plane of the door?
The door will not turn at all, no matter how hard you push.
Why does this happen?

Draw the line of action through your force arrow.
The line of action passes directly through the pivot.
The distance from a line to a point lying on that line is zero.
Therefore, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action is $d_\perp = 0\text{ m}$.

Substitute $d_\perp = 0\text{ m}$ into the moment formula:
$$M = F \times 0\text{ m} = 0\text{ N m}$$
A force produces zero moment whenever its line of action passes through the pivot.
This rule is vital when solving problems with pivots or hinges.
Forces acting directly at the pivot create no turning effect about that pivot.

### Worked Example 3: Force directed through a pivot

A rigid rod of length 0.75 m is pivoted at point P.
A pulling force of 80 N is applied at the free end, directed straight along the rod toward P.
Calculate the moment of this force about point P.

**Answer**
$$\text{Moment} = 0\text{ N m}$$

**Explanation**
Identify the line of action of the applied force.
The force acts along the length of the rod directly toward pivot P.
The line of action passes directly through the pivot.
Therefore, the perpendicular distance from P to the line of action is zero ($d_\perp = 0\text{ m}$).

Using $M = Fd_\perp$:
$$M = 80\text{ N} \times 0\text{ m} = 0\text{ N m}$$
The length of the rod does not create a moment because it is parallel to the force.

## The turning effect of an object's weight

Every physical object has mass, so Earth's gravity pulls on it.
This means an object's own weight can produce a turning effect about a pivot.
To find this turning effect, combine the weight model with the moment equation.

First, calculate the total weight using $W = mg$.
Next, place this downward force arrow at the object's centre of gravity.
Finally, determine the perpendicular distance from the pivot to this vertical line of action.

Because weight always acts vertically downward, its line of action is a vertical line.
The shortest line from any pivot to a vertical line is always horizontal.
Therefore, the perpendicular distance for a weight force is the horizontal separation between the pivot and the centre of gravity.
Multiplying weight by this horizontal separation gives the moment.

### Worked Example 4: Moment of a horizontal beam's weight

A uniform wooden beam has a mass of 6.5 kg and a length of 2.4 m.
The beam is supported by a pivot located 0.40 m from its left end.
Calculate the magnitude and direction of the moment produced by the beam's weight about the pivot.
Use $g = 9.81\text{ N kg}^{-1}$.

**Answer**
$$\text{Moment} = 51\text{ N m clockwise}$$

**Explanation**
First, find the total weight of the beam using $W = mg$:
$$W = 6.5\text{ kg} \times 9.81\text{ N kg}^{-1} = 63.765\text{ N}$$
Because the beam is uniform, its centre of gravity lies at its geometric midpoint.
The midpoint is half of 2.4 m, which is 1.20 m from the left end.

The pivot is located 0.40 m from the left end.
Both the pivot and the beam are horizontal.
Find the horizontal distance from the pivot to the centre of gravity:
$$d_\perp = 1.20\text{ m} - 0.40\text{ m} = 0.80\text{ m}$$

Now calculate the moment about the pivot:
$$M = W d_\perp = 63.765\text{ N} \times 0.80\text{ m} = 51.012\text{ N m}$$
Rounding to two significant figures gives $51\text{ N m}$.
The centre of gravity lies to the right of the pivot.
The downward weight force therefore tends to rotate the beam clockwise about the pivot.

## Perpendicular distance for angled bodies and forces

In many real systems, objects or forces are inclined at an angle.
Calculating moments for angled setups requires careful geometric analysis.
Never simply multiply the force by the slanted length of the object.
The formula strictly demands the perpendicular distance to the line of action.

Consider an inclined beam of length $L$ pivoted at its lower end.
The centre of gravity G is located at distance $L$ along the beam.
The beam is tilted at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal.
The weight $W$ acts vertically downward through point G.

Draw the vertical line of action through G.
The perpendicular distance from the pivot to this vertical line is the horizontal base of the right-angled triangle.
From basic trigonometry, the adjacent side next to angle $\theta$ is:
$$d_\perp = L \cos\theta$$
Therefore, the moment of the weight about the pivot is:
$$M = W (L \cos\theta)$$

You can also reach this exact same result from another perspective.
Instead of finding the perpendicular distance, resolve the weight vector into components.
One component acts parallel to the beam, passing straight through the pivot.
As we learned earlier, a force passing through the pivot produces zero moment.

The other component acts perpendicular to the beam, with magnitude $W_\perp = W \cos\theta$.
Multiplying this perpendicular force component by the distance $L$ along the beam gives:
$$M = W_\perp L = (W \cos\theta) L$$
Notice that both methods produce the exact same formula.
You may use either method, but finding $d_\perp$ directly is usually faster.

### Worked Example 5: Moment of an inclined sign

A uniform rectangular sign has a mass of 8.0 kg.
The sign is hinged to a wall at point H.
The centre of gravity G of the sign is 0.75 m from H along the sign.

The sign is held at an angle of $35^\circ$ above the horizontal.
Use $g = 9.81\text{ N kg}^{-1}$.
Calculate the moment of the sign's weight about hinge H.

**Answer**
$$\text{Moment} = 48\text{ N m clockwise}$$

**Explanation**
First, calculate the weight of the sign:
$$W = mg = 8.0\text{ kg} \times 9.81\text{ N kg}^{-1} = 78.48\text{ N}$$
The weight acts vertically downward through G.
The hinge H is the pivot.

The shortest distance from H to the vertical line of action is horizontal.
Construct a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $0.75\text{ m}$ and angle $35^\circ$ to the horizontal.
The horizontal adjacent side is:
$$d_\perp = 0.75\text{ m} \times \cos 35^\circ \approx 0.75\text{ m} \times 0.8192 = 0.6144\text{ m}$$

Now calculate the moment:
$$M = W d_\perp = 78.48\text{ N} \times 0.6144\text{ m} \approx 48.22\text{ N m}$$
To two significant figures, matching the given data, $M = 48\text{ N m}$.
Because the weight pulls downward to the right of hinge H, the moment acts clockwise.

## Solving for force or distance

In many physics problems, the moment is already known.
You must then find the force needed to produce that moment, or the distance required.
We do this by rearranging $M = F d_\perp$ using algebra.

To find the required force $F$, divide both sides of the equation by $d_\perp$:
$$F = \frac{M}{d_\perp}$$
The distance unit (metres) cancels in the division, leaving newtons:
$$\frac{\text{N m}}{\text{m}} = \text{N}$$

To find the required perpendicular distance $d_\perp$, divide both sides by $F$:
$$d_\perp = \frac{M}{F}$$
Here the force unit (newtons) cancels, leaving metres:
$$\frac{\text{N m}}{\text{N}} = \text{m}$$
Always check your units during algebraic rearrangements to ensure your equation is set up correctly.

### Worked Example 6: Rearranging for force and distance

A tightening tool must apply a moment of $42\text{ N m}$ to a bolt.
Calculate the force required if the force is applied perpendicularly at a distance of $0.35\text{ m}$ from the bolt.
A worker can exert a maximum force of $150\text{ N}$.
Calculate the minimum perpendicular distance from the bolt for this force.

**Answer**
For the first calculation, the required force is $120\text{ N}$.
For the second calculation, the minimum perpendicular distance is $0.28\text{ m}$.

**Explanation**
For the force calculation, we know $M = 42\text{ N m}$ and $d_\perp = 0.35\text{ m}$.
Rearrange the moment equation to make force the subject:
$$F = \frac{M}{d_\perp} = \frac{42\text{ N m}}{0.35\text{ m}} = 120\text{ N}$$
The unit metres cancels out, leaving newtons.

For the distance calculation, we know $M = 42\text{ N m}$ and $F = 150\text{ N}$.
Rearrange to make perpendicular distance the subject:
$$d_\perp = \frac{M}{F} = \frac{42\text{ N m}}{150\text{ N}} = 0.28\text{ m}$$
The unit newtons cancels out, leaving metres.

## Boundaries of this lesson

In this lesson, we analyzed the turning effect of a single force acting on an object.
Real mechanical structures often involve multiple forces acting simultaneously.
It is important to know where this lesson stops.

First, a pair of equal and opposite forces acting along different lines of action is called a couple.
Couples produce rotation without any linear motion.
We will study couples and their torque in the next lesson.

Second, multiple forces can act on opposite sides of a pivot.
When opposing turning effects balance each other exactly, an object remains in rotational equilibrium.
This balancing is governed by the principle of moments, which belongs to a later lesson.

Finally, complete mechanical equilibrium requires both zero resultant force and zero resultant moment.
We will master those combined equilibrium conditions later in this topic.

## Archimedes and the balance point resolved

We can now answer the questions that opened our lesson.
How can an extended body with weight distributed along its length be represented as a single force?
How do we calculate the exact turning tendency produced by that force about any pivot?

Archimedes resolved the first question by recognizing the centre of gravity.
You do not need to add up thousands of individual downward pulls.
All distributed gravitational forces combine into one equivalent downward force: the total weight $W = mg$.

This entire weight acts through a single point called the centre of gravity.
For uniform shapes, this point lies at the geometric centre.
For non-uniform bodies, it shifts toward the heavier sections.

He resolved the second question through the geometry of turning effects.
The turning tendency produced by any force is its moment.
To calculate the moment, multiply the force by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to its line of action ($M = Fd_\perp$).

Because weight acts vertically downward, its perpendicular distance from a pivot is always measured horizontally.
If a force points directly through the pivot, its perpendicular distance is zero, producing no turning effect at all.

Core summary of this lesson:
- The centre of gravity is the point through which the whole weight of a body may be considered to act.
- Weight is calculated using $W = mg$ and acts vertically downward.
- Moment of a force: the product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the point to the line of action of the force.
- The formula for the moment of a force is $M = Fd_\perp$.
- The coherent SI unit of moment is the newton metre ($\text{N m}$), equivalent to base units $\text{kg m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}$.
- Moments act in either a clockwise or anticlockwise sense.
- A force whose line of action passes through the pivot produces zero moment.
