# Distance, displacement, speed and velocity

## One journey, two useful descriptions

Sam walks **300 m east** from a bus stop, then walks **100 m west** to a shop. A fitness tracker records every metre of Sam's route. A map only compares Sam's final position with the bus stop.

Both descriptions are useful, but they answer different questions. The tracker gives the **distance** travelled. The map gives the **displacement**. Before using an equation, always ask: *Do I need the whole route, or only the start-to-finish change?*

In this lesson, take east as the positive direction. A westward displacement then has a negative sign. The sign is not decoration. It carries direction information.

## Distance and displacement

**Distance** is the total length of the path travelled. It is a **scalar**, so it has size but no direction. Distance is measured in metres, written \(\mathrm{m}\). It cannot be negative.

**Displacement** describes the change in position from a chosen reference point. It is a **vector**, so it needs both size and direction.

> **Definition to learn: displacement.** distance in a specified direction from a point

The word **from** matters. Start at the reference point and compare it with the final point. The word **direction** matters too. A statement such as \(200\,\mathrm{m}\) is not a complete displacement. Write \(200\,\mathrm{m\ east}\), or use a signed value only after stating the positive direction.

For Sam's journey:

\[
\text{distance}=300\,\mathrm{m}+100\,\mathrm{m}=400\,\mathrm{m}
\]

The displacement uses direction. East is positive and west is negative:

\[
\text{displacement}=+300\,\mathrm{m}+(-100\,\mathrm{m})=+200\,\mathrm{m}
\]

So Sam's displacement is **\(200\,\mathrm{m\ east}\)**.

Notice that the path can be long while the displacement is small. If Sam returned to the bus stop, the distance would increase but the final displacement would be \(0\,\mathrm{m}\).

**Teacher question:** If an athlete completes one lap and finishes exactly where they began, what is their displacement?

It is \(0\,\mathrm{m}\), because the start and finish are the same point. The distance is the full lap length.

Which statement describes a walker who travels \(60\,\mathrm{m}\) north and then \(60\,\mathrm{m}\) south?

- **A.** Distance is \(0\,\mathrm{m}\) and displacement is \(120\,\mathrm{m}\).
- **B.** Distance is \(120\,\mathrm{m}\) and displacement is \(0\,\mathrm{m}\).

**Feedback for A:** This reverses the two ideas. Adding the route lengths gives distance, not displacement.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. The person covers \(60+60=120\,\mathrm{m}\), but finishes at the starting point.

## Speed and velocity

Distance tells us how much route was covered. **Speed** tells us how quickly that distance was covered. Speed is scalar, so a direction is not needed.

**Velocity** tells us how quickly displacement changes. It is a vector.

> **Definition to learn: velocity.** rate of change of displacement

The phrase **rate of change** means an amount divided by the time taken. Because displacement has direction, velocity has direction. A velocity can be written as \(5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}\ east}\). The unit \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) is read as "metres per second".

> **Formula to learn: Average speed.**
>
> \[
> \bar{v} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}
> \]

Here \(\bar{v}\) means average speed in this controlled formula. Total distance is in \(\mathrm{m}\), and total time is in \(\mathrm{s}\), so the unit is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Include every part of the journey and every part of its time, including any time spent stationary. Do not add separate speeds and divide by two unless the time intervals are equal.

> **Formula to learn: Average velocity.**
>
> \[
> \vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{s}}{\Delta t}
> \]

\(\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}\) is average velocity. The arrow shows that it is a vector. \(\Delta\vec{s}\) means the change in displacement from start to finish, and \(\Delta t\) means the total time interval. Use the resultant displacement, not the total distance travelled. Its unit is also \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), but an answer must include direction when the sign convention has not already supplied it.

**Teacher question:** Why can average speed and the magnitude of average velocity have the same unit but different values?

They divide by the same total time, but they can use different numerators. Distance follows the route. Displacement goes straight from start to finish.

### Worked example 1: A return journey on one line

A delivery robot moves \(180\,\mathrm{m}\) east, then \(60\,\mathrm{m}\) west. The entire journey takes \(40\,\mathrm{s}\). Find its average speed and average velocity.

First decide which total belongs to each quantity. Average speed needs total distance. Average velocity needs displacement.

**Step 1: Find total distance.**

\[
\text{total distance}=180\,\mathrm{m}+60\,\mathrm{m}=240\,\mathrm{m}
\]

**Step 2: Find average speed.**

\[
\bar{v}=\frac{240\,\mathrm{m}}{40\,\mathrm{s}}
\]

\[
\bar{v}=6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

**Step 3: Find displacement.** East is positive.

\[
\Delta s=(+180\,\mathrm{m})+(-60\,\mathrm{m})=+120\,\mathrm{m}
\]

The displacement is \(120\,\mathrm{m\ east}\).

**Step 4: Find average velocity.**

\[
\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{+120\,\mathrm{m}}{40\,\mathrm{s}}
\]

\[
\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}=+3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

**Answer:** average speed is **\(6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)**. Average velocity is **\(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}\ east}\)**.

**Check:** \(240\,\mathrm{m}\) is larger than the \(120\,\mathrm{m}\) displacement, so the speed is larger than the magnitude of the average velocity. Both answers have metres divided by seconds.

## Constant velocity and \(s=vt\)

Sometimes an object keeps the same velocity throughout an interval. This means its speed and direction do not change. In that special case, displacement can be found from velocity and time.

> **Formula to learn: Displacement in uniform motion.**
>
> \[
> s = vt
> \]

In this formula, \(s\) is displacement in \(\mathrm{m}\), \(v\) is constant velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(t\) is time in \(\mathrm{s}\). The condition is **uniform motion**, meaning constant velocity. The formula does not apply unchanged if the velocity changes.

The units confirm the relationship:

\[
\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\times\mathrm{s}
\]

\[
=\mathrm{m}
\]

Metres per second multiplied by seconds leaves metres, the unit of displacement.

### Worked example 2: A journey with two directions

At constant velocity, a drone travels \(90\,\mathrm{m}\) north in \(15\,\mathrm{s}\), then \(120\,\mathrm{m}\) east in \(20\,\mathrm{s}\). Find its average speed and the magnitude and direction of its average velocity.

The total journey time is:

\[
\Delta t=15\,\mathrm{s}+20\,\mathrm{s}=35\,\mathrm{s}
\]

**Step 1: Find average speed from the route length.**

\[
\text{total distance}=90\,\mathrm{m}+120\,\mathrm{m}=210\,\mathrm{m}
\]

\[
\bar{v}=\frac{210\,\mathrm{m}}{35\,\mathrm{s}}
\]

\[
\bar{v}=6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

**Step 2: Find the magnitude of displacement.** The north and east parts are perpendicular. The displacement is the straight line joining the start and finish. Pythagoras gives:

\[
\Delta s=\sqrt{(90\,\mathrm{m})^2+(120\,\mathrm{m})^2}
\]

\[
\Delta s=\sqrt{8100\,\mathrm{m^2}+14400\,\mathrm{m^2}}
\]

\[
\Delta s=150\,\mathrm{m}
\]

**Step 3: Find the direction.** Measured east of north, the eastward side is opposite the angle and the northward side is adjacent.

\[
\tan\theta=\frac{120}{90}
\]

\[
\theta=53^\circ
\]

The displacement is \(150\,\mathrm{m}\) at \(53^\circ\) east of north.

**Step 4: Find average velocity.**

\[
\left|\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}\right|=\frac{150\,\mathrm{m}}{35\,\mathrm{s}}
\]

\[
\left|\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}}\right|=4.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

**Answer:** average speed is **\(6.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)**. Average velocity is **\(4.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) at \(53^\circ\) east of north**.

**Check:** the displacement, \(150\,\mathrm{m}\), is shorter than the \(210\,\mathrm{m}\) route. The average velocity magnitude is therefore smaller than the average speed. The direction completes the vector answer.

An object travels \(50\,\mathrm{m}\) east at a constant velocity of \(5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Which calculation finds the travel time?

- **A.** \(t=sv=50\times5.0=250\,\mathrm{s}\)
- **B.** \(t=\dfrac{s}{v}=\dfrac{50}{5.0}=10\,\mathrm{s}\)

**Feedback for A:** Multiplying \(\mathrm{m}\) by \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) does not give seconds. The rearrangement is incorrect.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. Divide displacement by constant velocity. The units are \(\mathrm{m}\div\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=\mathrm{s}\).

## Mistakes worth repairing

**Mistake: Using total distance for average velocity.** This is tempting because both calculations use total time. The failure is in the numerator. Average velocity uses the straight-line displacement from start to finish.

**Mistake: Writing only \(4.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for a velocity.** That gives a size but no direction. Add a direction, or state a sign convention and retain the sign.

**Mistake: Calling every negative velocity a slowing object.** A negative sign only says that the object moves in the chosen negative direction. Whether it is slowing down depends on how its velocity changes. Acceleration is the next lesson.

**Mistake: Taking the mean of two stage speeds.** The average speed is total distance divided by total time. A simple mean of speeds only works when the time intervals are equal.

## Core recap

- **Distance** is total path length and is scalar.
- **Displacement** is distance in a specified direction from a point. It is a vector.
- **Velocity** is rate of change of displacement. It is a vector and needs direction.
- Use \(\bar{v} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}\) for average speed.
- Use \(\vec{v}_{\mathrm{avg}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{s}}{\Delta t}\) for average velocity.
- Use \(s = vt\) only for **uniform motion**, when velocity is constant.
