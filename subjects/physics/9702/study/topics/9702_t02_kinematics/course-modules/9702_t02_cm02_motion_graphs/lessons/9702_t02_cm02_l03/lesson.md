# Reading displacement-time and velocity-time graphs and Gradient: velocity and acceleration

## What a motion graph can tell you

Imagine following a walker past a library. You choose the library door as a **reference point**. Positions to the east are positive. Positions to the west are negative.

Each time you look, you could write down two things: the time on a clock and where the walker is relative to the door. A graph puts those pairs of values in one place. It lets you describe motion clearly without needing a photograph at every instant.

A motion graph does more than show where an object is or how fast it moves. Its **gradient**, or slope, can give a physical rate of change. On a displacement-time graph, the gradient gives velocity. On a velocity-time graph, the gradient gives acceleration.

The very first decision is always: *Which quantity is on the vertical axis?* A horizontal line or a steep slope has completely different physical meanings on different graphs.

## Read the axes before you describe the motion

On both graph types in this lesson, the horizontal axis is **time**, measured in seconds, \(\mathrm{s}\). Time always increases from left to right.

The vertical axis determines the meaning of every point, slope and line:

- A **displacement-time graph** has displacement, usually in metres, \(\mathrm{m}\), on the vertical axis.
- A **velocity-time graph** has velocity, usually in metres per second, \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), on the vertical axis.

Always ask yourself: *What quantity is on the vertical axis?*

For example, the point \((4.0\ \mathrm{s}, +7.0\ \mathrm{m})\) on a displacement-time graph means that at \(4.0\ \mathrm{s}\), the object is \(7.0\ \mathrm{m}\) on the chosen positive side of the reference point. It does not mean the object has travelled a total distance of \(7.0\ \mathrm{m}\).

## Displacement-time graphs show position relative to a reference point

**Displacement** tells us where an object is compared with a chosen reference point. It has both magnitude and direction.

<a id="definition-9702_def_displacement"></a>

> **Definition to learn: displacement.** distance in a specified direction from a point

The word **from** matters: start at the reference point and compare it with the final position.

On a displacement-time graph:

- A point above \(s=0\) is on the positive side of the reference point.
- A point below \(s=0\) is on the negative side.
- A point on \(s=0\) is at the reference point itself.
- A horizontal section means displacement does not change with time, so the object is **stationary**.
- Values that become more positive show motion in the positive direction.
- Values that become more negative show motion in the negative direction.

A displacement of \(-5\ \mathrm{m}\) tells you where the object is located. It does not, by itself, tell you which way the object is moving. You must inspect whether displacement is increasing or decreasing over time.

## Gradient of a displacement-time graph gives velocity

For any graph, the gradient of a line is calculated as the change in the vertical quantity divided by the change in the horizontal quantity:

\[
\text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}
\]

On a displacement-time graph, the vertical axis is displacement in metres, \(\mathrm{m}\), and the horizontal axis is time in seconds, \(\mathrm{s}\). The unit of this gradient is:

\[
\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

Metres per second is the unit of velocity.

<a id="definition-9702_def_velocity"></a>

> **Definition to learn: velocity.** rate of change of displacement

The mathematical calculation of gradient, \(\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\), is the exact rate of change of displacement. Therefore:

- The **gradient of a displacement-time graph equals velocity**.
- A **positive gradient** means velocity is in the chosen positive direction.
- A **negative gradient** means velocity is in the opposite, negative direction.
- A **horizontal section** has zero gradient, so velocity is zero (the object is stationary).
- A **steeper line** represents a greater magnitude of velocity (higher speed).

### Straight lines and curved lines

For a **straight line**, the gradient is constant throughout that section. This represents **uniform velocity** (constant velocity). When calculating the gradient of a straight line, choose two points far apart on the line to minimize reading uncertainty:

\[
v = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1}
\]

If the displacement-time graph is **curved**, the gradient changes from instant to instant. The object has changing velocity (it is accelerating or decelerating).

To find the velocity at one specific instant on a curve, you must draw a **tangent** to the curve at that point:

1. Place a ruler so that it touches the curve at the chosen time without crossing it.
2. Draw a long straight line representing the tangent.
3. Choose two points far apart on this tangent line.
4. Calculate the gradient of the tangent: \(v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\).

A straight line connecting two distant points on the curve gives only the *average velocity* across that time interval. Only the tangent gives the *instantaneous velocity*.

An object is at displacement \(-6.0\ \mathrm{m}\) and has velocity \(+2.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Which interpretation is correct?

- **A:** It is located \(6.0\ \mathrm{m}\) on the negative side of the reference point and is moving in the positive direction.
- **B:** It is located \(6.0\ \mathrm{m}\) on the positive side of the reference point and is moving in the negative direction.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The negative sign on displacement identifies its position on the negative side of the reference point. The positive sign on velocity shows that its position is changing in the positive direction.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. This reverses both meanings. The displacement coordinate gives position, while the sign of velocity gives the direction of travel.

### Worked example 1: Velocity from a displacement-time graph

A student tracks a cart moving along a straight track. The displacement-time graph is a straight line that passes through the points \((2.0\ \mathrm{s}, +3.0\ \mathrm{m})\) and \((7.0\ \mathrm{s}, +18.0\ \mathrm{m})\).

Determine the velocity of the cart.

**Step 1: Identify the graph type and method.**

The vertical axis is displacement and the line is straight. The velocity is constant and equals the gradient of the line.

**Step 2: Calculate the change in displacement and time.**

\[
\Delta s = s_2 - s_1 = +18.0\ \mathrm{m} - (+3.0\ \mathrm{m}) = +15.0\ \mathrm{m}
\]

\[
\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 = 7.0\ \mathrm{s} - 2.0\ \mathrm{s} = 5.0\ \mathrm{s}
\]

**Step 3: Calculate the gradient.**

\[
v = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{+15.0\ \mathrm{m}}{5.0\ \mathrm{s}} = +3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

**Answer:** The velocity is **\(+3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)** (or \(3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in the positive direction).

**Check:** The line slopes upwards, so the gradient and velocity must be positive. The units \(\mathrm{m} \div \mathrm{s} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) match velocity.

A displacement-time graph for a runner curves so that it becomes steeper as time increases. What does this indicate about the runner's motion?

- **A:** The runner is moving with increasing speed in the positive direction.
- **B:** The runner is moving with constant acceleration and constant speed.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. As the curve gets steeper, the gradient of the tangent increases, which means the instantaneous velocity increases.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. A steeper gradient means a changing velocity, so the speed cannot be constant.

## Velocity-time graphs show velocity at each instant

On a **velocity-time graph**, the vertical axis gives the velocity of the object at each instant:

- A point above \(v=0\) means motion in the chosen positive direction.
- A point below \(v=0\) means motion in the chosen negative direction.
- A point at \(v=0\) means the object is momentarily **stationary**.
- A horizontal section at \(v=0\) means the object remains stationary.
- A horizontal section away from zero means the object travels with **constant velocity**. It is NOT stationary.
- A line that crosses the time axis from positive to negative (or vice versa) indicates that the object has **reversed its direction of motion**.

## Gradient of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration

To find how rapidly velocity is changing, calculate the gradient of the velocity-time graph:

\[
\text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
\]

The vertical axis is velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and the horizontal axis is time in \(\mathrm{s}\). The unit of this gradient is:

\[
\frac{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{\mathrm{s}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

Metres per second per second is the unit of acceleration.

<a id="definition-9702_def_acceleration"></a>

> **Definition to learn: acceleration.** rate of change of velocity

<a id="formula-9702_formula_acceleration"></a>

> **Formula to learn: Acceleration.**
>
> \[
> a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}
> \]

Because acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, the **gradient of a velocity-time graph equals acceleration**:

- A **straight line** indicates that the gradient is constant, meaning the motion has **constant acceleration** (uniform acceleration).
- A **positive gradient** means acceleration is positive (pointing in the positive direction).
- A **negative gradient** means acceleration is negative (pointing in the negative direction).
- A **horizontal line** has zero gradient, so acceleration is zero (velocity is constant).
- A **curved line** indicates changing acceleration. The instantaneous acceleration at any point is found by drawing a **tangent** to the curve and finding its gradient.

### Speeding up versus slowing down

A negative gradient does not always mean an object is slowing down. You must compare the sign of the velocity with the sign of the acceleration:

| Velocity sign | Gradient (acceleration) sign | Direction comparison | Effect on speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive (\(v > 0\)) | Positive (\(a > 0\)) | Same direction | Speed increases (accelerating forward) |
| Positive (\(v > 0\)) | Negative (\(a < 0\)) | Opposite directions | Speed decreases (slowing down) |
| Negative (\(v < 0\)) | Negative (\(a < 0\)) | Same direction | Speed increases in negative direction |
| Negative (\(v < 0\)) | Positive (\(a > 0\)) | Opposite directions | Speed decreases while moving backward |

On a velocity-time graph, a straight line slopes downward from \(+12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) to \(+4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Which statement is correct?

- **A:** The acceleration is negative and the object is slowing down.
- **B:** The acceleration is positive because the velocity values remain above zero.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The slope is downward, so \(\Delta v\) is negative and acceleration is negative. Since the velocity is positive, acceleration opposes motion and the object slows down.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. Velocity values above zero confirm that the object moves in the positive direction, but acceleration depends on the slope of the line, which is negative.

### Worked example 2: Acceleration from a velocity-time graph

A test vehicle moves along a straight test track. At time \(t = 2.0\ \mathrm{s}\), its velocity is \(-4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). At time \(t = 6.0\ \mathrm{s}\), its velocity has changed to \(+8.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in a straight-line trace.

(a) Calculate the acceleration of the vehicle.  
(b) State the time at which the vehicle reverses direction.

**Step 1: Calculate the acceleration from the gradient.**

The velocity-time graph is a straight line, so acceleration is constant.

\[
\Delta v = v_2 - v_1 = (+8.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}) - (-4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}) = +12.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]

\[
\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 = 6.0\ \mathrm{s} - 2.0\ \mathrm{s} = 4.0\ \mathrm{s}
\]

Divide the velocity change by the time taken:

\[
a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{+12.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}{4.0\ \mathrm{s}} = +3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

**Step 2: Find the time of direction reversal.**

The vehicle reverses direction when its velocity crosses zero (\(v = 0\)). Using \(v = u + at\):

\[
0 = -4.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} + (3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})(t - 2.0\ \mathrm{s})
\]

\[
3.0(t - 2.0) = 4.0
\]

\[
t - 2.0 = 1.33\ \mathrm{s} \implies t = 3.3\ \mathrm{s}
\]

**Answer:** (a) The acceleration is **\(+3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)** (or \(3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) in the positive direction). (b) The vehicle reverses direction at **\(t = 3.3\ \mathrm{s}\)**.

**Check:** The velocity rises from negative to positive, so the gradient must be positive. The vehicle stops momentarily at \(3.3\ \mathrm{s}\) as it changes direction from negative to positive.

## Comparing displacement-time and velocity-time graphs

Never interpret a motion graph without verifying the label on the vertical axis:

| Graph feature | Displacement-time graph | Velocity-time graph |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point on time axis (\(y = 0\)) | At the reference point (\(s = 0\)) | Momentarily stationary (\(v = 0\)) |
| Horizontal section on axis | Stationary at reference point | Stationary |
| Horizontal section off axis | Stationary at fixed position | Moving with constant velocity |
| Straight sloping section | Constant velocity (\(v = \text{gradient}\)) | Constant acceleration (\(a = \text{gradient}\)) |
| Gradient of the graph | Velocity (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)) | Acceleration (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)) |
| Curved line | Changing velocity (draw tangent for instantaneous \(v\)) | Changing acceleration (draw tangent for instantaneous \(a\)) |
| Line crosses time axis | Passes through reference point | Reverses direction of motion |

A horizontal line appears at \(+5.0\) on a motion graph from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 4.0\ \mathrm{s}\). Which statement explains why reading the axis label is critical?

- **A:** If the axis is displacement in \(\mathrm{m}\), the object is stationary; if the axis is velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the object travels at constant speed.
- **B:** A horizontal line always represents an object at rest regardless of the vertical axis.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. On a displacement-time graph, a constant displacement means no motion. On a velocity-time graph, a non-zero constant velocity means steady motion in that direction.

**Feedback for B:** Not correct. A horizontal line away from the axis on a velocity-time graph represents steady motion at that velocity, not rest.

## Mistakes worth repairing

**Mistake: Treating a horizontal line on a velocity-time graph as stationary.**  
A horizontal line at \(+6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) means the object moves steadily forward at \(6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). It is stationary only if the line lies on the axis where \(v = 0\).

**Mistake: Assuming every point where \(v = 0\) is a direction reversal.**  
A reversal occurs only if the sign of velocity changes across the point (from positive to negative or negative to positive). If an object slows to rest and then accelerates in the original direction, velocity touches zero without changing sign.

**Mistake: Calculating the gradient of a curve using distant points.**  
Selecting two distant points on a curved line gives only the average rate of change over that time interval. To determine instantaneous velocity on a displacement-time curve or instantaneous acceleration on a velocity-time curve, you must construct a tangent at that exact time.

**Mistake: Dropping negative signs when computing \(\Delta v\).**  
Always write out \(\Delta v = v_2 - v_1\). When changing from \(-4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) to \(+8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the calculation is \((+8) - (-4) = +12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), not \(8 - 4 = 4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

**Mistake: Equating negative acceleration with slowing down.**  
Negative acceleration means the acceleration vector points in the chosen negative direction. If an object is moving in the negative direction (\(v < 0\)), negative acceleration causes it to speed up in that negative direction.

## Core recap

- Always check the **vertical axis label and units** before reading a motion graph.
- On a **displacement-time graph**, the vertical position is displacement \(s\). Its **gradient is velocity**: \(\text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = v\).
- On a **velocity-time graph**, the vertical position is velocity \(v\). Its **gradient is acceleration**: \(\text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = a\).
- A **straight line** indicates a constant rate of change (constant velocity on \(s-t\), constant acceleration on \(v-t\)).
- A **curved line** requires a **tangent** drawn at the point of interest to determine instantaneous velocity or instantaneous acceleration.
- **Velocity** is the rate of change of displacement. **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: \(a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\).
- An object reverses direction when its continuous velocity-time graph crosses the time axis (\(v = 0\)).
- Speed increases when velocity and acceleration share the same sign; speed decreases when they have opposite signs.
