# Each of Newton’s laws of motion and the concept of weight

## Why do objects change their state of motion?

Every day, you observe objects speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction:

- When a train departs from a platform, it gradually increases its speed along the track.
- When an aircraft cruises at high altitude, it can travel thousands of kilometres in a straight line at an unchanging velocity without passengers feeling any sensation of acceleration.
- When you drop an apple, it falls towards the ground, gaining downward speed every millisecond until it strikes the floor.
- When a skater glides over smooth, level ice, they continue moving forward for a remarkably long distance with almost no noticeable decrease in speed.

For centuries before modern physics developed, thinkers like Aristotle believed that an object required a continuous push just to keep moving. In everyday life, sliding objects eventually come to a stop, so it was easy to assume that rest is the "natural" state of matter and that motion requires a continuous force.

Sir Isaac Newton, building on the experiments and insights of Galileo Galilei, overturned this misconception completely. Newton realized that friction and air resistance are themselves forces that oppose motion. In the absence of opposing forces, a moving object does not slow down at all: it continues moving at the same speed in a straight line forever.

Newton formulated three universal laws of motion that govern how forces affect the movement of all macroscopic bodies. In this lesson, you will master each of Newton's three laws, learn how to distinguish true interaction pairs from forces in equilibrium, and explore the precise relationship between mass, gravitational field strength, and weight.

## Newton’s first law of motion

Newton's first law describes what happens to a body when there is no net force acting on it.

In Cambridge International AS Level Physics, this law has an exact controlled definition that you must learn word for word:

<a id="definition-9702_def_newtons_first_law"></a>

> **Definition to learn: Newton's first law of motion.** a body remains at rest or continues at constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force

Notice every single word in this definition:
- **"remains at rest"**: If an object is initially stationary, it stays stationary as long as the resultant force is zero.
- **"continues at constant velocity"**: Constant velocity means both constant speed and unchanging direction (straight-line motion). No force is required to maintain motion at constant velocity.
- **"unless acted on by a resultant force"**: A change in velocity (an acceleration) occurs if, and only if, a non-zero resultant (net) force acts on the body.

### Resultant force and equilibrium

Recall from Lesson 1 that the **resultant force** is the single vector sum of all individual forces acting on a body:

\[
\Sigma \vec{F} = \vec{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \dots
\]

When the vector sum of all forces acting on a body is zero (\(\Sigma \vec{F} = 0\)), the forces are balanced, and the body is said to be in **equilibrium**:

1. **Static equilibrium**: The body is at rest (\(v = 0\)) and remains at rest because the resultant force is zero. For example, a heavy crate resting motionless on a level warehouse floor.
2. **Dynamic equilibrium**: The body is moving with a constant velocity (\(v \neq 0\)) in a straight line. The resultant force is zero, so the acceleration is zero (\(a = 0\)). For example, a car driving along a straight, horizontal motorway at a steady speed of \(30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) has a forward engine driving force that exactly equals the backward resistive forces of friction and air drag.

If an exam question states that a vehicle or object moves at "constant velocity", "steady speed in a straight line", or "uniform speed along a level path", Newton's first law immediately tells you that:

\[
\Sigma \vec{F} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad a = 0
\]

## Newton’s second law of motion

Newton's first law explains what happens when the resultant force is zero. Newton's second law explains what happens when the resultant force is **not** zero.

In Lesson 2, you saw that Newton fundamentally defined force in terms of momentum:

<a id="definition-9702_def_force"></a>

> **Definition to learn: force (Newton's second law).** the rate of change of momentum of a body

The mathematical statement of Newton's second law relates resultant force to the rate of change of linear momentum:

<a id="formula-9702_formula_newtons_second_law"></a>

> **Formula to learn: Newton's second law of motion.**
>
> \[
> F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = ma
> \]

Let us review the symbols and conditions:
- **\(F\)** is the **resultant force** acting on the body, measured in newtons (\(\mathrm{N}\)).
- **\(\Delta p\)** is the change in linear momentum, measured in \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{N\,s}\).
- **\(\Delta t\)** is the time interval over which the momentum change occurs, measured in seconds (\(\mathrm{s}\)).
- **\(m\)** is the mass of the body, measured in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)).
- **\(a\)** is the acceleration of the body, measured in metres per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

### Linking \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\) and \(F = ma\)

Recall the algebraic derivation from Lesson 2:

\[
F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{m(v - u)}{\Delta t} = m\left(\frac{v - u}{\Delta t}\right)
\]

Because acceleration is defined as \(a = \frac{v - u}{\Delta t}\), this gives:

\[
F = ma \quad \text{(strictly for constant mass } m\text{)}
\]

### Physical meaning of Newton's second law

Newton's second law tells you three fundamental facts about how forces produce acceleration:
1. **Direct proportionality**: The acceleration of an object of constant mass is directly proportional to the resultant force acting on it (\(a \propto F\)). If you double the resultant force, the acceleration doubles.
2. **Inverse proportionality**: For a given resultant force, the acceleration is inversely proportional to the mass of the body (\(a \propto \frac{1}{m}\)). An object with twice the mass experiences half the acceleration under the same net force.
3. **Identical direction**: Acceleration is a vector quantity that points in the **exact same direction** as the resultant force. If the net force acts horizontally to the left, the body accelerates horizontally to the left, regardless of which way the body was originally traveling.

If a body travels with uniform (constant non-zero) acceleration, Newton's second law guarantees that the resultant force acting on it is **constant and non-zero**.

## Weight as the gravitational force on a mass

Gravity is one of the fundamental interactions in nature. Any massive object in the universe creates a gravitational field around itself.

When a second object with mass \(m\) is placed in this gravitational field, it experiences an attractive downward force towards the centre of the planet. This gravitational force is what we call **weight**.

In Cambridge International AS Level Physics, you must recall the formula for weight:

<a id="formula-9702_formula_weight"></a>

> **Formula to learn: weight.**
>
> \[
> W = mg
> \]

Let us examine each quantity in this formula:
- **\(W\)** is the **weight** of the object, measured in newtons (\(\mathrm{N}\)). Weight is a vector quantity pointing vertically downwards towards the centre of the Earth (or the parent celestial body).
- **\(m\)** is the **mass** of the object, measured in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)). Mass is a scalar quantity representing the amount of matter and the property that resists changes in motion (inertia).
- **\(g\)** is the **acceleration of free fall** (or **gravitational field strength**), measured in metres per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)) or newtons per kilogram (\(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\)).

On the surface of the Earth, the standard value used in Cambridge examinations is:

\[
g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 9.81\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}
\]

### Distinction between mass and weight

Confusing mass and weight is one of the most persistent errors made by physics students. The table below highlights the crucial distinctions:

| Characteristic | Mass (\(m\)) | Weight (\(W\)) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical meaning** | Property of a body that resists change in motion (inertia); amount of matter | Gravitational force exerted on a mass by a gravitational field |
| **Type of quantity** | Scalar (magnitude only, always positive) | Vector (magnitude and vertical downward direction) |
| **SI unit** | Kilogram (\(\mathrm{kg}\)) | Newton (\(\mathrm{N}\)) |
| **Dependence on location** | Invariant: identical everywhere in the universe | Varies depending on the local gravitational field strength \(g\) |
| **Measuring instrument** | Balance (e.g. beam balance, inertial balance) | Calibrated spring balance or newton meter |

For example, consider an astronaut with a mass of \(80.0\,\mathrm{kg}\):
- On **Earth**, where \(g_{\mathrm{Earth}} = 9.81\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\):
  \[
  W_{\mathrm{Earth}} = 80.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 9.81\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}} = 785\,\mathrm{N}
  \]
- On the **Moon**, where \(g_{\mathrm{Moon}} \approx 1.62\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\):
  The astronaut's mass remains exactly \(80.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), but their weight drops to:
  \[
  W_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 80.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 1.62\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}} = 130\,\mathrm{N}
  \]
- On the dwarf planet **Pluto**, where \(g_{\mathrm{Pluto}} \approx 0.66\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\):
  The mass is still \(80.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), while their weight is:
  \[
  W_{\mathrm{Pluto}} = 80.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 0.66\,\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}} = 52.8\,\mathrm{N}
  \]

### Why all objects fall with the same acceleration in free fall

Have you ever wondered why a heavy lead ball and a small wooden marble fall at the exact same acceleration in a vacuum?

Newton's second law and the weight equation explain this beautifully:

1. By definition of weight, the gravitational force pulling down on an object of mass \(m\) is \(F_{\mathrm{net}} = W = mg\).
2. By Newton's second law, the downward acceleration of the object is:
   \[
   a = \frac{F_{\mathrm{net}}}{m} = \frac{mg}{m} = g
   \]

The mass \(m\) appears in both the numerator (the gravitational pull) and the denominator (the inertial resistance to acceleration), cancelling out completely. A body with ten times the mass experiences ten times the gravitational pull, but it also has ten times the inertia, requiring ten times as much force to accelerate at the same rate. Therefore, every object in a uniform gravitational field accelerates downward at the exact rate \(g\), provided air resistance is negligible.

## Newton’s third law of motion

Forces never exist in isolation. A force is not a standalone object; it is an interaction between two separate physical bodies.

Whenever body A pushes or pulls on body B, body B simultaneously pushes or pulls on body A.

In Cambridge International AS Level Physics, Newton's third law has an exact controlled definition:

<a id="definition-9702_def_newtons_third_law"></a>

> **Definition to learn: Newton's third law of motion.** when two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction

### The four criteria for a true Newton's third law pair

To determine whether two forces form a genuine Newton's third law interaction pair, they must satisfy all four of the following criteria without exception:

1. **Equal in magnitude**: The two forces have the exact same size (\(|F_{A \text{ on } B}| = |F_{B \text{ on } A}|\)) at every single instant.
2. **Opposite in direction**: If force 1 points vertically upwards, force 2 points vertically downwards. In vector notation:
   \[
   \vec{F}_{A \text{ on } B} = -\vec{F}_{B \text{ on } A}
   \]
3. **Act on two different bodies**: Force 1 acts on Body B (exerted by Body A); Force 2 acts on Body A (exerted by Body B). Two forces that act on the same body can **never** form a Newton's third law pair.
4. **Same type (nature) of force**: Both forces must arise from the exact same fundamental interaction. If force 1 is a gravitational pull, force 2 must be a gravitational pull. If force 1 is an electrostatic contact normal force, force 2 must be an electrostatic contact normal force. If force 1 is friction, force 2 must be friction.

```
       Body A                             Body B
   +------------+                     +------------+
   |            | <--- F_(B on A) --- |            |
   |            |                     |            |
   |            | --- F_(A on B) ---> |            |
   +------------+                     +------------+
   
   Properties:
   - Equal magnitude: |F_(A on B)| = |F_(B on A)|
   - Opposite direction
   - Act on DIFFERENT bodies (one on A, one on B)
   - Same physical nature (both contact, or both gravitational)
```

### Dissecting the classic book on a table

Consider a physics textbook resting stationary on a flat horizontal table. Two vertical forces act on the book:

1. The downward gravitational pull of the Earth on the book: its weight \(W\).
2. The upward push of the table surface on the book: the normal contact force \(R\).

Because the book remains at rest, Newton's first law tells us that the resultant force on the book is zero:

\[
R - W = 0 \implies R = W
\]

Many students look at this equation and jump to the incorrect conclusion that \(R\) and \(W\) form a Newton's third law pair because they are "equal and opposite".

**This is completely false.** Let us apply our four criteria:

- Do \(R\) and \(W\) act on different bodies? **No.** Both forces act on the **same body** (the book).
- Are \(R\) and \(W\) of the same type? **No.** Weight \(W\) is a **gravitational** force caused by the Earth's mass. Normal contact force \(R\) is an **electrostatic** contact force caused by the repulsion between the outer electrons of the table and the book.
- If you pull the table away, the table's normal contact force vanishes instantly, but the book's weight remains \(mg\). A true third law pair can never be separated or destroyed independently.

The equality \(R = W\) for a book at rest on a table is an example of **equilibrium under Newton's first law**, not Newton's third law!

What are the true Newton's third law pairs?

1. **For the gravitational pull of the Earth on the book (\(W\)):**
   - Body A is the Earth; Body B is the book.
   - Force 1: Earth pulls downward on the book gravitationally with magnitude \(W\).
   - Force 2 (Third law partner): Book pulls upward on the Earth gravitationally with magnitude \(W\).
   - Both are gravitational forces; they act on two different bodies (book and Earth); they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
2. **For the upward normal contact force of the table on the book (\(R\)):**
   - Body A is the table; Body B is the book.
   - Force 1: Table pushes upward on the book with contact normal force \(R\).
   - Force 2 (Third law partner): Book pushes downward on the table with contact normal force \(R\).
   - Both are contact normal forces; they act on two different bodies (book and table); they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

## Apparent weight and accelerating frames (lifts and elevators)

When you stand on a bathroom scale, what does the scale actually measure?

A bathroom scale does not directly read the gravitational pull of the Earth on your body. The scale measures the **normal contact force \(R\)** that its top surface exerts upward on the soles of your feet.

By Newton's third law, the soles of your feet push down on the scale with an equal force \(R\). Inside the scale, springs or electronic pressure sensors compress in response to this contact force \(R\), and the display translates this force into a reading (either in newtons, or in kilograms by dividing by standard \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

This upward contact force \(R\) is what you physically perceive as your **apparent weight**.

Let us analyze a person of mass \(m\) standing on a scale inside an elevator (lift) that can accelerate vertically.

```
            +-----------------------+
            |        Elevator       |
            |                       |
            |         ( o )         |
            |          /|\  Person  |
            |          / \  mass m  |
            |        =======        |
            |        [Scale]        |
            +-----------------------+
            
        Free-body diagram of the person:
        
                     ^ Normal contact force R (apparent weight)
                     |
                    (m)
                     |
                     v True weight W = mg (gravitational force)
```

Two vertical forces act on the person:
1. Downward gravitational force (true weight): \(W = mg\)
2. Upward normal contact force from the scale: \(R\)

Taking the **upward direction as positive**, the resultant vertical force acting on the person is:

\[
F_{\mathrm{net}} = R - mg
\]

By Newton's second law:

\[
R - mg = ma \implies R = m(g + a)
\]

where \(a\) is the vertical acceleration of the lift (positive when accelerating upwards, negative when accelerating downwards).

Let us analyze the key physical cases:

### Case 1: Lift stationary or moving at constant velocity (\(a = 0\))

By Newton's first law, the person is in equilibrium:

\[
R - mg = 0 \implies R = mg
\]

The scale reading equals the person's true weight. It does not matter whether the lift is stationary, moving upwards at \(5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), or moving downwards at \(5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). As long as the velocity is constant, the acceleration is zero, and \(R = mg\).

### Case 2: Lift with upward acceleration (\(a > 0\))

When the acceleration points upwards:

\[
R = m(g + a) > mg
\]

The normal contact force is greater than true weight, so the scale reading increases, and the person feels heavier.

This upward acceleration occurs in two distinct situations:
1. The lift is **moving upwards and speeding up** (e.g. departing from the ground floor). Velocity is upwards (\(v > 0\)), and acceleration is upwards (\(a > 0\)).
2. The lift is **moving downwards and slowing down** (e.g. braking to a stop as it reaches the ground floor). Velocity is downwards (\(v < 0\)), but the acceleration points upwards (\(a > 0\)) to reduce the downward speed.

### Case 3: Lift with downward acceleration (\(a < 0\))

If the acceleration points downwards with magnitude \(a_{\mathrm{down}}\):

\[
mg - R = m a_{\mathrm{down}} \implies R = m(g - a_{\mathrm{down}}) < mg
\]

The normal contact force is less than true weight, so the scale reading decreases, and the person feels lighter.

This downward acceleration occurs in two distinct situations:
1. The lift is **moving downwards and speeding up** (e.g. departing downwards from the top floor). Velocity is downwards (\(v < 0\)), and acceleration is downwards (\(a_{\mathrm{down}} > 0\)).
2. The lift is **moving upwards and slowing down** (e.g. braking to a stop as it arrives at the top floor). Velocity is upwards (\(v > 0\)), but the acceleration points downwards (\(a_{\mathrm{down}} > 0\)) to reduce the upward speed.

### Case 4: Free fall (\(a_{\mathrm{down}} = g\))

If the elevator cable snaps and the lift falls freely under gravity without air resistance, its downward acceleration is \(a_{\mathrm{down}} = g\):

\[
R = m(g - g) = 0
\]

The contact force between the person's feet and the scale drops to zero. The person floats relative to the floor in apparent weightlessness.

> **Crucial observation:** Throughout all four cases, the **gravitational force** acting on the person remains completely unchanged at \(W = mg\). Acceleration changes only the **contact force** \(R\), never the gravitational pull exerted by the Earth.

## Interacting blocks in contact

Newton's third law also governs the contact forces between separate objects pushed across a surface.

Consider two blocks, X and Y, placed in contact on a smooth horizontal frictionless floor:
- Block X has mass \(m_X\).
- Block Y has mass \(m_Y\).
- An external horizontal force \(F\) is applied to block X, pushing both blocks together to the right.

```
         Applied force
             F --->  +-------+-------+
                     |       |       |
                     |   X   |   Y   |  ---> acceleration a
                     |  (mX) |  (mY) |
                     +-------+-------+
                     ----------------- Frictionless floor
```

### Finding the system acceleration

Because the blocks remain in contact, they move together as a single combined system of total mass \((m_X + m_Y)\).

By Newton's second law applied to the whole system:

\[
F = (m_X + m_Y)a \implies a = \frac{F}{m_X + m_Y}
\]

### Finding the internal contact force between the blocks

Now isolate block Y and draw its free-body diagram:
- Vertically: normal reaction from the floor balances the weight of Y.
- Horizontally: there is only one force acting on block Y, which is the forward contact push exerted by block X on block Y, denoted \(N_{XY}\).

Applying Newton's second law specifically to block Y:

\[
N_{XY} = m_Y a = m_Y \left(\frac{F}{m_X + m_Y}\right) = \left(\frac{m_Y}{m_X + m_Y}\right)F
\]

Notice that because \(m_Y < m_X + m_Y\), the contact force \(N_{XY}\) is strictly **less than** the applied force \(F\). Part of the applied force \(F\) is needed just to accelerate block X itself.

Now isolate block X and draw its free-body diagram:
- Horizontally, block X experiences two forces:
  1. The external applied push \(F\) pointing to the right.
  2. The backward contact push exerted by block Y on block X, denoted \(N_{YX}\), pointing to the left.

By Newton's third law, the force that block Y exerts on block X is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force that block X exerts on block Y:

\[
N_{YX} = N_{XY} = \left(\frac{m_Y}{m_X + m_Y}\right)F
\]

Let us check Newton's second law on block X:

\[
F_{\mathrm{net}, X} = F - N_{YX} = F - \left(\frac{m_Y}{m_X + m_Y}\right)F = F\left(1 - \frac{m_Y}{m_X + m_Y}\right) = \left(\frac{m_X}{m_X + m_Y}\right)F = m_X a
\]

Everything is perfectly consistent:
- The force of X on Y and the force of Y on X are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (Newton's third law).
- They act on different bodies (one on Y, one on X), so they do not cancel each other out on either individual block.
- The net force on each individual block equals its mass times the system acceleration (Newton's second law).

## Original worked examples

### Worked Example 1: Person in an accelerating lift

A student of mass \(72.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) stands on a digital bathroom scale placed on the floor of an elevator in a high-rise building. Take the acceleration of free fall as \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

Determine:
1. The student's true weight.
2. The reading on the scale (in newtons and equivalent mass in kilograms) when the lift accelerates vertically upwards from rest at \(1.50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).
3. The reading on the scale when the lift travels upwards at a constant speed of \(4.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
4. The reading on the scale when the lift approaches the top floor, moving upwards but slowing down with a deceleration of \(2.20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).
5. State the magnitude and direction of the gravitational force acting on the student during phase 4.

#### Solution

##### Step 1: Calculate the student's true weight

Using the controlled formula for weight:

\[
W = mg
\]

Substitute \(m = 72.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\):

\[
W = 72.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 706.32\,\mathrm{N}
\]

Rounding to three significant figures gives:

\[
W = 706\,\mathrm{N} \quad \text{directed vertically downwards}
\]

##### Step 2: Upward acceleration from rest (\(a = +1.50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\))

Let upward be positive. The two vertical forces acting on the student are the upward normal contact force \(R\) from the scale and the downward weight \(W = mg\).

Apply Newton's second law:

\[
F_{\mathrm{net}} = R - mg = ma
\]

Rearrange for the normal contact force \(R\):

\[
R = m(g + a) = 72.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times (9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} + 1.50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}) = 72.0 \times 11.31 = 814.32\,\mathrm{N}
\]

To three significant figures, the scale measures a contact force of:

\[
R = 814\,\mathrm{N}
\]

Because the scale is calibrated to display mass by dividing the contact force by standard \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the displayed reading in kilograms is:

\[
m_{\mathrm{displayed}} = \frac{R}{g} = \frac{814.32\,\mathrm{N}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}} = 83.009\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 83.0\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

The student temporarily feels heavier because the scale must not only support their weight but also accelerate their mass upwards.

##### Step 3: Traveling upwards at constant speed (\(v = 4.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\))

Constant velocity means zero acceleration (\(a = 0\)).

By Newton's first law, the forces are in equilibrium:

\[
F_{\mathrm{net}} = R - mg = 0 \implies R = mg = 706.32\,\mathrm{N} \approx 706\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The displayed mass is:

\[
m_{\mathrm{displayed}} = \frac{706.32\,\mathrm{N}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}} = 72.0\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

##### Step 4: Moving upwards but slowing down (\(a = -2.20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\))

The elevator is moving upwards, but its speed is decreasing. Therefore, the acceleration vector points downwards:

\[
a_{\mathrm{down}} = 2.20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \quad \text{or} \quad a = -2.20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

Apply Newton's second law:

\[
R = m(g + a) = 72.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times (9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} - 2.20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}) = 72.0 \times 7.61 = 547.92\,\mathrm{N}
\]

To three significant figures:

\[
R = 548\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The displayed mass on the scale is:

\[
m_{\mathrm{displayed}} = \frac{547.92\,\mathrm{N}}{9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}} = 55.853\,\mathrm{kg} \approx 55.9\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

The student feels lighter because the floor is accelerating away downwards relative to their forward motion.

##### Step 5: Gravitational force during phase 4

The gravitational force acting on the student is their true weight:

\[
W = mg = 706\,\mathrm{N} \quad \text{directed vertically downwards}
\]

The acceleration of the elevator does not alter the Earth's gravitational field or the student's mass. The gravitational force remains \(706\,\mathrm{N}\) downwards throughout all phases.

### Worked Example 2: Connected crates and force pair auditing

Two wooden crates, crate A of mass \(m_A = 15.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and crate B of mass \(m_B = 10.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), are connected by a light horizontal inextensible cable. They rest on a smooth, horizontal, frictionless laboratory floor.

A constant horizontal pulling force of \(F = 75.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right is applied to crate B, pulling crate A behind it via the connecting cable.

```
                  Crate A              Cable              Crate B
               +------------+                       +------------+
               |            |   Tension T           |            |   Pull F = 75.0 N
               |   15.0 kg  | <-----------o-------> |   10.0 kg  | ----------------->
               |            |                       |            |
               +------------+                       +------------+
               --------------------------------------------------- Frictionless floor
```

1. Calculate the acceleration of the two crates.
2. Calculate the tension \(T\) in the connecting cable.
3. Systematically tabulate every force acting on crate A, crate B, and the connecting cable, identifying the exact body on which the force acts, its direction, and its Newton's third law partner force.

#### Solution

##### Step 1: Calculate the system acceleration

Because the cable is inextensible, both crates and the cable accelerate together at the same rate \(a\). The total mass of the system is:

\[
M = m_A + m_B = 15.0\,\mathrm{kg} + 10.0\,\mathrm{kg} = 25.0\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

The cable is light (negligible mass). Treating both crates as one combined system, the internal tension forces cancel out, leaving the external force \(F = 75.0\,\mathrm{N}\) as the net horizontal force.

By Newton's second law:

\[
a = \frac{F}{M} = \frac{75.0\,\mathrm{N}}{25.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 3.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \quad \text{to the right}
\]

##### Step 2: Calculate the tension \(T\) in the cable

Isolate crate A. The only horizontal force acting on crate A is the tension force \(T\) exerted by the cable pulling to the right.

Apply Newton's second law to crate A:

\[
T = m_A a = 15.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 3.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 45.0\,\mathrm{N}
\]

##### Step 3: Check consistency by isolating crate B

The horizontal forces on crate B are:
- The external pull \(F = 75.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right.
- The backward tension force \(T = 45.0\,\mathrm{N}\) exerted by the cable to the left.

The resultant horizontal force on crate B is:

\[
F_{\mathrm{net}, B} = F - T = 75.0\,\mathrm{N} - 45.0\,\mathrm{N} = 30.0\,\mathrm{N} \quad \text{to the right}
\]

Using Newton's second law:

\[
m_B a = 10.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 3.00\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 30.0\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The values match exactly.

##### Step 4: Systematic Newton's third law force audit

The table below catalogs every force acting in this system and pairs it with its genuine Newton's third law partner:

| Primary force | Exerted by | Acts on | Type of force | Direction | Newton's third law partner force | Exerted by | Acts on |

## Active checks

### Check 1: Identifying a true Newton's third law pair

A heavy dictionary rests stationary on a wooden desk in a quiet library. Which force forms a genuine Newton's third law interaction pair with the downward gravitational force exerted by the Earth on the dictionary?

- **A.** The upward gravitational force exerted by the dictionary on the Earth.
- **B.** The upward normal contact force exerted by the desk surface on the dictionary.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Newton's third law pairs must involve the same two interacting bodies (Earth and dictionary) and the same physical nature of force (gravitational). If the Earth exerts a downward gravitational force on the dictionary, the dictionary simultaneously exerts an equal upward gravitational force on the Earth.

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. The upward normal contact force and the downward gravitational force both act on the same body (the dictionary), and they are of completely different physical types (contact normal electrostatic vs gravitational). Their equality in magnitude is due to equilibrium under Newton's first law (\(\Sigma F = 0\)), not Newton's third law.

---

### Check 2: Bathroom scale in a moving elevator

A passenger of mass \(60.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) stands on an accurate bathroom scale inside an elevator. At a particular instant, the scale displays a reading of \(520\,\mathrm{N}\). Taking \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), which description correctly identifies the elevator's motion?

- **A.** The elevator is moving upwards and slowing down.
- **B.** The elevator is moving downwards and slowing down.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The passenger's true weight is \(W = mg = 60.0 \times 9.81 = 589\,\mathrm{N}\). The scale reading is the normal contact force \(R = 520\,\mathrm{N}\). Because \(R < W\), the resultant force \(F_{\mathrm{net}} = R - mg = 520 - 589 = -69\,\mathrm{N}\) is directed downwards, meaning the acceleration is downwards. An elevator with downward acceleration can either be moving downwards and speeding up, or moving upwards and slowing down. Thus, option A is physically correct.

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. If the elevator were moving downwards and slowing down, its acceleration would have to be directed upwards to reduce the downward speed. An upward acceleration requires a resultant upward force (\(R > W\)), which would cause the scale to read greater than \(589\,\mathrm{N}\) (for example, \(650\,\mathrm{N}\)). Because the scale reads only \(520\,\mathrm{N}\), the acceleration is downwards.

### Check 3: Gravitational force on an accelerating spacecraft

An automated space probe of mass \(450\,\mathrm{kg}\) is launched vertically upwards from the surface of the Earth. At a certain instant shortly after liftoff, its rocket engines produce a vertical upward thrust of \(6200\,\mathrm{N}\). Taking \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) near the launchpad, what is the magnitude of the gravitational force acting on the space probe at this instant?

- **A.** \(4410\,\mathrm{N}\)
- **B.** \(1790\,\mathrm{N}\)

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The gravitational force acting on the probe is its weight, given by \(W = mg = 450\,\mathrm{kg} \times 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 4414.5\,\mathrm{N} \approx 4410\,\mathrm{N}\). Gravitational force depends strictly on the probe's mass and the local gravitational field strength; it does not change because the rocket engines are firing or because the probe is accelerating upwards.

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. You calculated the resultant upward force acting on the probe (\(F_{\mathrm{net}} = \text{Thrust} - W = 6200 - 4414.5 = 1785.5\,\mathrm{N} \approx 1790\,\mathrm{N}\)). The question asked specifically for the gravitational force, which is the weight \(W = mg = 4410\,\mathrm{N}\).

### Check 4: Contact force between pushing blocks

Two metal blocks, block P of mass \(3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and block Q of mass \(1.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), are placed in contact on a frictionless horizontal bench. A horizontal pushing force of \(12\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right is applied to block P, accelerating both blocks together. What is the magnitude of the horizontal force that block P exerts on block Q?

- **A.** \(3.0\,\mathrm{N}\)
- **B.** \(12\,\mathrm{N}\)

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The total mass is \(3.0 + 1.0 = 4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\). The system acceleration is \(a = \frac{F}{M} = \frac{12\,\mathrm{N}}{4.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The only horizontal force acting on block Q is the contact push from block P, which by Newton's second law must equal \(m_Q a = 1.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{N}\).

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. You assumed that the full \(12\,\mathrm{N}\) applied force is transmitted through to block Q. However, part of the \(12\,\mathrm{N}\) force must accelerate block P itself (\(F_{\mathrm{net}, P} = m_P a = 3.0 \times 3.0 = 9.0\,\mathrm{N}\)). The contact force between the blocks is \(12 - 9.0 = 3.0\,\mathrm{N}\).

## Mistakes worth repairing

### Mistake 1: Believing normal contact force and weight are a Newton's third law pair

This is the number one misconception in mechanics examinations. Students see that for a stationary object on a level floor, the upward normal reaction \(R\) equals the downward weight \(W\), and assume they form a third law pair.

Remember:
- Newton's third law pairs act on **two different bodies**. Both \(R\) and \(W\) act on the **same body**.
- Newton's third law pairs are of the **same physical type**. Weight is gravitational; normal contact force is electromagnetic (contact repulsion).
- When a person stands in an accelerating lift, \(R \neq W\). Yet Newton's third law pairs are **always** equal, no matter how violently the system accelerates!

### Mistake 2: Thinking Newton's third law pairs cancel each other out

Students often wonder: "If every force has an equal and opposite reaction force, why does anything ever accelerate? Shouldn't all forces cancel to zero?"

The resolution is simple:
- Forces cancel out to produce equilibrium only when they act on the **same object**.
- Newton's third law pairs act on **different objects**.
- When you kick a football, your foot exerts a forward force on the ball, causing the ball to accelerate forward. The ball exerts an equal backward force on your foot. Because the forward force acts on the ball and the backward force acts on your foot, they cannot cancel each other out. To find the acceleration of the ball, you sum only the forces acting on the ball!

### Mistake 3: Confusing mass and weight

In casual conversation, people say "I weigh \(70\,\mathrm{kg}\)". In physics, this statement mixes up two completely different quantities:
- Mass (\(70\,\mathrm{kg}\)) is a scalar measure of inertia and matter. It does not change if you travel to the Moon or orbit in deep space.
- Weight is the downward gravitational force exerted on that mass. On Earth, a mass of \(70\,\mathrm{kg}\) has a weight of \(W = mg = 70 \times 9.81 = 687\,\mathrm{N}\). On the Moon, the same mass has a weight of only \(113\,\mathrm{N}\). Always use kilograms for mass and newtons for weight.

### Mistake 4: Believing the action force precedes the reaction force

The terms "action" and "reaction" are historical phrases that can mislead students into thinking that force 1 occurs first, and force 2 responds a split second later.

In modern physics:
- There is no time delay between the two forces.
- Interaction pairs arise simultaneously.
- Neither force is the "cause" or the "effect"; they are simultaneous aspects of a single mutual interaction between two bodies.

### Mistake 5: Assuming gravitational force changes during vertical acceleration

When an elevator accelerates upwards, passengers feel heavier. Many students mistakenly write that the "gravity on the person has increased".

This is incorrect:
- The gravitational force on the passenger remains \(W = mg\), determined solely by the mass of the person and the Earth's gravitational field.
- What changes is the **normal contact force \(R\)** exerted by the floor. Your sensation of weight comes from the compression of your bones and muscles resisting the floor's upward contact push, not from a direct sensory perception of gravity.

## Core recap

- **Newton's first law of motion:**
  <a id="recap-definition-9702_def_newtons_first_law"></a>
  > a body remains at rest or continues at constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force
- When the resultant force is zero (\(\Sigma \vec{F} = 0\)), an object is in **equilibrium** (either static equilibrium at rest, or dynamic equilibrium at constant velocity).
- **Newton's second law of motion:**
  <a id="recap-definition-9702_def_force"></a>
  > the rate of change of momentum of a body
  \[
  F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = ma \quad \text{(for constant mass } m\text{)}
  \]
- Acceleration is directly proportional to resultant force, inversely proportional to mass, and in the exact same direction as the resultant force.
- **Weight:**
  <a id="recap-formula-9702_formula_weight"></a>
  \[
  W = mg
  \]
  Weight is the downward gravitational force (in \(\mathrm{N}\)) exerted on a mass \(m\) (in \(\mathrm{kg}\)) by a gravitational field of strength \(g\) (in \(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).
- Mass is invariant across different locations, whereas weight varies with the local gravitational field strength \(g\).
- In free fall with negligible air resistance, all bodies experience the same acceleration \(a = \frac{mg}{m} = g\).
- **Newton's third law of motion:**
  <a id="recap-definition-9702_def_newtons_third_law"></a>
  > when two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction
- True Newton's third law interaction pairs satisfy four strict criteria:
  1. Equal in magnitude
  2. Opposite in direction
  3. Act on two different bodies
  4. Same physical nature of force
- Normal contact force and weight acting on a body at rest on a surface do not form a third law pair: they act on the same body and have different physical origins.
- In an accelerating lift, apparent weight is the normal contact force \(R = m(g + a)\). True weight \(W = mg\) remains constant.
