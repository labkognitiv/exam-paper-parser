# Mass is the property of an object that resists change in motion

## Why are some objects harder to move than others?

Imagine pushing an empty shopping trolley across a flat supermarket floor. A gentle push easily sets it into motion. If the trolley approaches a shelf, you can stop it almost instantly with a light pull.

Now imagine the same trolley packed with heavy sacks of flour and tinned goods. If you apply the same gentle push, the trolley barely speeds up. Once it is rolling forward, bringing it to a halt requires a much firmer, sustained effort. If you try to turn a sharp corner, the loaded trolley resists the turn and tends to keep moving straight ahead.

In both situations, the trolley, the wheels, and the floor are the same. What has changed is the amount of matter in the trolley: its **mass**.

Mass gives an object a reluctance to change its state of rest or motion. In physics, this fundamental tendency is called **inertia**. The more mass an object has, the more it resists being sped up, slowed down, or redirected.

## Mass as the resistance to change in motion

In Cambridge International AS Level Physics, mass is defined through this resistance:

<a id="definition-9702_def_mass"></a>

> **Definition to learn: mass.** the property of a body that resists changes in motion

Every word in this definition has precise physical meaning:
- **A body:** Refers to any physical object or collection of particles under consideration.
- **Resists changes in motion:** An object does not resist moving. Once an object is moving at constant velocity, it requires no force at all to keep going. Instead, mass resists changes in velocity. Changing velocity means speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction of motion. In other words, mass resists **acceleration**.

Mass is a **scalar quantity**. It possesses magnitude (numerical size) and an SI unit, but no spatial direction. The base unit of mass in the International System of Units (SI) is the **kilogram**, abbreviated \(\mathrm{kg}\).

Mass is also an intrinsic property of a body. A \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) astronaut has a mass of \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) on Earth, \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) on the Moon, and \(60\,\mathrm{kg}\) floating in deep space. The gravitational pull on the astronaut changes from place to place, but the quantity of matter and its resistance to acceleration remain identical.

## Forces and changing motion

If mass is what resists a change in motion, what causes a change in motion? The answer is a **resultant force**.

A force is a push or a pull exerted on a body. It is a **vector quantity**, which means it has both magnitude (measured in **newtons**, \(\mathrm{N}\)) and a specific direction.

In real situations, several forces often act on an object at the same time:
- A car engine provides a forward driving force.
- Friction from the road and air resistance push backwards.
- Gravity pulls downward, while the road pushes upward.

To determine whether the car speeds up, slows down, or moves at constant velocity, you must find the vector sum of all the individual forces. This single overall force is called the **resultant force** (or net force), often written as \(\Sigma F\) or \(F_{\mathrm{net}}\).

- If the forces cancel each other out completely, the resultant force is **zero** (\(\Sigma F = 0\)). The forces are balanced. The object does not accelerate: it remains at rest if it was already stationary, or continues to move with constant velocity in a straight line if it was already moving.
- If the forces do not cancel out, the resultant force is **non-zero** (\(\Sigma F \neq 0\)). The forces are unbalanced, and the object must accelerate.

## Newton's second law: relating force, mass, and acceleration

Sir Isaac Newton linked resultant force, mass, and acceleration into one of the most fundamental laws of classical physics:

<a id="formula-9702_formula_newtons_second_law"></a>

> **Formula to learn: Newton's second law of motion.**
>
> \[
> F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = ma
> \]

The first part of this relationship, \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\), defines force in terms of the rate of change of momentum (which you will explore in detail in the next lesson).

When the mass \(m\) of the object remains constant during the motion, Newton's second law takes the practical quantitative form:

\[
F = ma
\]

Let us carefully inspect every term in this equation:
- **\(F\)** is the **resultant force** acting on the body, measured in newtons (\(\mathrm{N}\)). It is not just any single force; it is the vector sum of all forces acting on that body.
- **\(m\)** is the **mass** of the body, measured in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)).
- **\(a\)** is the **acceleration** of the body, measured in metres per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).

### Understanding the newton

The equation \(F = ma\) establishes the SI unit of force. If a resultant force acts on a mass of \(1\,\mathrm{kg}\) and produces an acceleration of \(1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the magnitude of that resultant force is defined as \(1\) newton (\(1\,\mathrm{N}\)):

\[
1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg}\times 1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}
\]

This means that \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\) is the SI base unit equivalent of the newton.

### Two key relationships in \(F = ma\)

Rearranging the formula reveals two foundational principles:

1. **Acceleration is directly proportional to resultant force** (\(a \propto F\) for constant mass):
   \[
   a = \frac{F}{m}
   \]
   If you double the resultant force acting on a given object, its acceleration doubles. If there is no resultant force (\(F = 0\)), the acceleration is zero.

2. **Acceleration is inversely proportional to mass** (\(a \propto \frac{1}{m}\) for a constant resultant force):
   For a given resultant force, a body with twice the mass experiences only half the acceleration. This directly expresses mass as the property resisting acceleration.

## Direction: acceleration and resultant force always share the same direction

A vital consequence of \(F = ma\) is the direction of the quantities.

Mass \(m\) is a positive scalar. Therefore, multiplying the acceleration vector \(\vec{a}\) by \(m\) scales its magnitude but cannot change its direction:

\[
\vec{F}_{\mathrm{net}} = m\vec{a}
\]

This leads to an absolute rule in mechanics:

> **The acceleration of a body is always in the exact same direction as the resultant force acting on it.**

Take care not to confuse the direction of **acceleration** with the direction of **velocity**:
- An object can move forward while accelerating forward (speeding up). Here, velocity, resultant force, and acceleration all point forward.
- An object can move forward while accelerating backward (slowing down). For instance, when a driver applies the brakes, the car is still moving forward (positive velocity), but the resultant force acts backward. The acceleration is therefore directed backward, causing the forward speed to decrease.
- An object can move in a curve. The resultant force acts sideways towards the center of the curve, so the acceleration is directed sideways, altering the direction of velocity.

In all cases, **acceleration always follows the resultant force**, never the velocity.

### Resultant force along a straight line

When forces act along a single straight line, choose one direction as positive:
- Forces pointing in the positive direction carry a positive sign.
- Forces pointing in the opposite direction carry a negative sign.

Add the signed forces algebraically to determine the resultant force \(\Sigma F\).

For example, if a force of \(150\,\mathrm{N}\) acts to the right and a resistive force of \(60\,\mathrm{N}\) acts to the left, take right as positive:

\[
\Sigma F = (+150\,\mathrm{N}) + (-60\,\mathrm{N}) = +90\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The resultant force is \(90\,\mathrm{N}\) directed to the right. If the body has a mass of \(30\,\mathrm{kg}\), its acceleration is:

\[
a = \frac{\Sigma F}{m} = \frac{+90\,\mathrm{N}}{30\,\mathrm{kg}} = +3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

The positive sign confirms that the acceleration is directed to the right.

### System mass versus individual mass

When two or more objects touch or are connected together so that they move with the same acceleration, you can analyze the motion at two different scales:
1. **The whole system:** The total resultant external force accelerates the combined mass (\(m_{\mathrm{total}} = m_1 + m_2\)).
2. **An individual object:** The resultant force acting on object 1 alone accelerates only mass \(m_1\) (\(F_{\mathrm{net}, 1} = m_1 a\)).

The external force applied to the first object is not the resultant force acting on the second object. Contact forces between the objects transfer only the force needed to accelerate each individual mass.

### Worked example 1: A vehicle with driving and resistive forces

A light utility cart of mass \(650\,\mathrm{kg}\) travels along a straight horizontal road. The engine provides a forward driving force of \(2200\,\mathrm{N}\). Air resistance and friction exert a combined opposing resistive force of \(900\,\mathrm{N}\).

Calculate:
1. The magnitude and direction of the resultant force acting on the cart.
2. The acceleration of the cart.

#### Strategy and model check
- Motion is along a single horizontal straight line.
- Take the forward direction as positive.
- Identify all horizontal forces and sum them to obtain the resultant force \(\Sigma F\).
- Apply \(F = ma\) to solve for acceleration.

#### Step 1: Calculate the resultant force
Forward driving force: \(F_{\mathrm{drive}} = +2200\,\mathrm{N}\).
Opposing resistive force: \(F_{\mathrm{resist}} = -900\,\mathrm{N}\).

\[
\Sigma F = F_{\mathrm{drive}} + F_{\mathrm{resist}}
\]

\[
\Sigma F = 2200\,\mathrm{N} - 900\,\mathrm{N} = +1300\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The resultant force has a magnitude of **\(1300\,\mathrm{N}\)** (or \(1.3\times 10^3\,\mathrm{N}\) to 2 significant figures) and is directed **forward**.

#### Step 2: Calculate the acceleration
Use Newton's second law rearranged for acceleration:

\[
a = \frac{\Sigma F}{m}
\]

Substitute the values:

\[
a = \frac{1300\,\mathrm{N}}{650\,\mathrm{kg}}
\]

Recall that \(1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\):

\[
a = \frac{1300\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}}{650\,\mathrm{kg}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

**Answer:**
1. Resultant force is **\(1300\,\mathrm{N}\) forward** (or \(1.3\times 10^3\,\mathrm{N}\) forward).
2. Acceleration is **\(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) forward**.

#### Reasonableness check
- An acceleration of \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) means the vehicle increases its speed by \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) every second, reaching a typical city speed of \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (about \(72\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\)) in \(10\,\mathrm{s}\). This is a physically reasonable acceleration for a utility cart.
- The forward force exceeds the resistive force, so the resultant force is forward and the cart speeds up in the forward direction. Units cancel cleanly to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

### Worked example 2: Braking deceleration and stopping force

A steel container of mass \(45\,\mathrm{kg}\) slides across a warehouse floor with an initial horizontal velocity of \(8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) to the right. A retarding mechanism exerts a constant horizontal braking force that brings the container uniformly to rest over a distance of \(16\,\mathrm{m}\).

Calculate:
1. The acceleration of the container during braking.
2. The magnitude and direction of the resultant force acting on the container.

#### Strategy and model check
- Motion is along a straight horizontal line with uniform deceleration, so the constant-acceleration kinematic equations apply.
- Take right as the positive direction.
- Given: initial velocity \(u = +8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), final velocity \(v = 0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), displacement \(s = +16\,\mathrm{m}\), mass \(m = 45\,\mathrm{kg}\).
- Target quantities: acceleration \(a\), then resultant force \(F = ma\).

#### Step 1: Calculate the acceleration using kinematics
Use the kinematic equation connecting \(u\), \(v\), \(a\), and \(s\):

\[
v^2 = u^2 + 2as
\]

Substitute the known values:

\[
0^2 = (8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})^2 + 2 a (16\,\mathrm{m})
\]

\[
0 = 64\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}} + 32\,\mathrm{m} \times a
\]

Rearrange for \(a\):

\[
32\,\mathrm{m} \times a = -64\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}
\]

\[
a = \frac{-64\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}}{32\,\mathrm{m}} = -2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

The negative sign indicates that the acceleration is directed to the left (opposite to the initial motion).

#### Step 2: Calculate the resultant force
Now apply Newton's second law:

\[
F = ma
\]

Substitute \(m = 45\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(a = -2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\):

\[
F = 45\,\mathrm{kg} \times (-2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}) = -90\,\mathrm{N}
\]

The magnitude of the resultant force is \(90\,\mathrm{N}\), and its direction is to the left.

**Answer:**
1. Acceleration is **\(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) to the left** (or \(-2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).
2. Resultant force has magnitude **\(90\,\mathrm{N}\)** and is directed **to the left**.

#### Reasonableness check
- The container is slowing down while travelling to the right. Therefore, both its acceleration and the resultant force must point to the left.
- Unit consistency: \(\mathrm{kg}\times \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{N}\).
- Time to stop: \(t = \frac{v - u}{a} = \frac{0 - 8.0}{-2.0} = 4.0\,\mathrm{s}\). Average speed is \(\frac{8.0 + 0}{2} = 4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Distance covered is \(4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\times 4.0\,\mathrm{s} = 16\,\mathrm{m}\). The kinematic values are completely consistent.

### Worked example 3: Blocks in contact and internal contact forces

Two blocks, block X of mass \(3.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) and block Y of mass \(2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), rest in contact with each other on a frictionless horizontal table. A steady horizontal pushing force \(P = 15\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to block X to the right, pushing both blocks across the table.

Calculate:
1. The acceleration of the two blocks.
2. The resultant force acting on block Y.
3. The contact force exerted by block X on block Y, and the contact force exerted by block Y on block X.

#### Strategy and model check
- The two blocks remain in contact, so they accelerate together with the identical acceleration \(a\).
- First, treat both blocks together as a combined system of mass \(m_{\mathrm{total}} = m_X + m_Y\) to find \(a\).
- Second, isolate block Y to find the resultant force on block Y alone.
- Third, analyze block X to verify Newton's third law and force balance.

#### Step 1: Acceleration of the combined system
The only external horizontal force acting on the combined two-block system is the push \(P = 15\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right.

\[
m_{\mathrm{total}} = m_X + m_Y = 3.0\,\mathrm{kg} + 2.0\,\mathrm{kg} = 5.0\,\mathrm{kg}
\]

Apply \(F = ma\) to the system:

\[
a = \frac{P}{m_{\mathrm{total}}} = \frac{15\,\mathrm{N}}{5.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
\]

Both blocks accelerate to the right at \(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

#### Step 2: Resultant force on block Y
Now isolate block Y alone. Its mass is \(m_Y = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg}\), and its acceleration is \(a = 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

The resultant force required to produce this acceleration on block Y is:

\[
\Sigma F_Y = m_Y a = 2.0\,\mathrm{kg} \times 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 6.0\,\mathrm{N}
\]

Because the surface is frictionless, the only horizontal force acting on block Y is the normal contact force pushed by block X, denoted \(F_{XY}\).

Therefore, the force that block X exerts on block Y is **\(6.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right**.

Notice that \(F_{XY} = 6.0\,\mathrm{N}\) is much less than the original applied force \(P = 15\,\mathrm{N}\). Block X absorbs \(9.0\,\mathrm{N}\) of the push to accelerate itself!

#### Step 3: Contact force on block X
By Newton's third law, block Y pushes back on block X with an equal and opposite force:

\[
F_{YX} = 6.0\,\mathrm{N} \text{ to the left}
\]

Check block X:
- Forward applied force: \(+15\,\mathrm{N}\).
- Backward contact force from block Y: \(-6.0\,\mathrm{N}\).
- Resultant force on block X alone:
  \[
  \Sigma F_X = 15\,\mathrm{N} - 6.0\,\mathrm{N} = +9.0\,\mathrm{N}
  \]
- Acceleration of block X:
  \[
  a_X = \frac{\Sigma F_X}{m_X} = \frac{9.0\,\mathrm{N}}{3.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}
  \]

This matches the system acceleration exactly, verifying the complete analysis.

**Answer:**
1. The acceleration of both blocks is **\(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) to the right**.
2. The resultant force on block Y is **\(6.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right**.
3. Block X exerts a force of **\(6.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the right** on block Y; block Y exerts a force of **\(6.0\,\mathrm{N}\) to the left** on block X.

## Active learning checks

### Check 1: Understanding mass as inertia

Two solid metal cylinders of identical shape and appearance, one made of lead (mass \(4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\)) and one made of aluminium (mass \(1.0\,\mathrm{kg}\)), rest on a smooth, horizontal air table where friction is zero. An identical horizontal push of \(8.0\,\mathrm{N}\) is applied to each cylinder.

Which statement correctly compares their behaviour?

- **A.** The lead cylinder has more inertia, so it experiences a smaller acceleration than the aluminium cylinder.
- **B.** The lead cylinder has more inertia, so it requires a longer time before it starts to move at all.

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Mass is the property of a body that resists changes in motion. Under the same resultant force of \(8.0\,\mathrm{N}\), the \(4.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) lead cylinder accelerates at \(a = \frac{8.0\,\mathrm{N}}{4.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), while the \(1.0\,\mathrm{kg}\) aluminium cylinder accelerates at \(a = \frac{8.0\,\mathrm{N}}{1.0\,\mathrm{kg}} = 8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The larger mass displays greater resistance to acceleration.

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. Any non-zero resultant force produces an immediate acceleration on a frictionless surface, regardless of mass. The lead cylinder begins accelerating the instant the force is applied; it does not delay moving, but accelerates at a smaller rate.

### Check 2: Opposing forces and direction of acceleration

A storage box of mass \(25\,\mathrm{kg}\) is sliding to the right across a rough floor. A worker pushes the box horizontally to the right with a force of \(70\,\mathrm{N}\). Kinetic friction between the box and floor exerts a horizontal force of \(120\,\mathrm{N}\) to the left.

What is the acceleration of the box?

- **A.** \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) to the left
- **B.** \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) to the right

**Feedback for A:** Correct. Take right as positive. The resultant horizontal force is \(\Sigma F = +70\,\mathrm{N} + (-120\,\mathrm{N}) = -50\,\mathrm{N}\), which means \(50\,\mathrm{N}\) to the left. By Newton's second law, \(a = \frac{\Sigma F}{m} = \frac{-50\,\mathrm{N}}{25\,\mathrm{kg}} = -2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Acceleration and resultant force are always in the same direction, so the acceleration is directed to the left, causing the box to slow down.

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. The box is moving to the right, but its acceleration is governed by the resultant force, not the direction of velocity. Because the backward frictional force (\(120\,\mathrm{N}\)) is greater than the forward push (\(70\,\mathrm{N}\)), the resultant force acts to the left, which demands an acceleration to the left.

### Check 3: Connected blocks and internal contact forces

A tractor pulls two trailers, trailer A of mass \(1200\,\mathrm{kg}\) and trailer B of mass \(800\,\mathrm{kg}\), connected by a horizontal tow bar. The tractor exerts a forward horizontal pull of \(6000\,\mathrm{N}\) directly on trailer A. Friction is negligible.

What is the tension force in the tow bar pulling trailer B?

- **A.** \(2400\,\mathrm{N}\)
- **B.** \(6000\,\mathrm{N}\)

**Feedback for A:** Correct. First find the acceleration of the whole system: \(m_{\mathrm{total}} = 1200\,\mathrm{kg} + 800\,\mathrm{kg} = 2000\,\mathrm{kg}\). The acceleration is \(a = \frac{6000\,\mathrm{N}}{2000\,\mathrm{kg}} = 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Trailer B accelerates at \(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and the only horizontal force acting on trailer B is the tension \(T\) in the tow bar. Therefore, \(T = m_B a = 800\,\mathrm{kg} \times 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 2400\,\mathrm{N}\).

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. The \(6000\,\mathrm{N}\) pull is the total force accelerating both trailers combined. The tow bar does not transmit all \(6000\,\mathrm{N}\) to trailer B; trailer A requires \(1200\,\mathrm{kg}\times 3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 3600\,\mathrm{N}\) of resultant force for its own acceleration, leaving \(6000 - 3600 = 2400\,\mathrm{N}\) to accelerate trailer B.

### Check 4: Constant velocity and resultant force

A high-speed passenger train of mass \(400\,000\,\mathrm{kg}\) travels along a straight, level track at a constant velocity of \(50\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The locomotive engines deliver a forward driving thrust of \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\).

What is the resultant force acting on the train?

- **A.** \(0\,\mathrm{N}\)
- **B.** \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\) forward

**Feedback for A:** Correct. The train is moving at constant velocity, so its acceleration is zero (\(a = 0\)). By Newton's second law, \(F = ma = 400\,000\,\mathrm{kg} \times 0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 0\,\mathrm{N}\). The forward thrust of \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\) is exactly balanced by \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\) of resistive forces (air drag and rolling resistance).

**Feedback for B:** Incorrect. \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\) is the forward thrust from the engines, which is an individual force, not the resultant force. If the resultant force were \(140\,\mathrm{kN}\), the train would accelerate at \(a = \frac{140\,000\,\mathrm{N}}{400\,000\,\mathrm{kg}} = 0.35\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), contradicting the condition that velocity is constant.

## Mistakes worth repairing

### Mistake 1: Confusing mass and weight
Many beginners use the words mass and weight interchangeably in everyday speech. In physics, they are entirely different quantities:
- **Mass** is the intrinsic property of a body that resists changes in motion. It is a scalar, measured in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)), and remains constant regardless of location.
- **Weight** is a gravitational force exerted on a mass by a gravitational field. It is a vector, measured in newtons (\(\mathrm{N}\)), and depends directly on the strength of the local gravitational field. A \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) block has a mass of \(10\,\mathrm{kg}\) everywhere, but its weight on the Moon is only about one-sixth of its weight on Earth.

### Mistake 2: Believing a force is needed to keep an object moving
A widespread misconception (dating back to ancient philosophy) is that a forward force is required to sustain a constant velocity. In reality:
- An object moving at constant velocity in a straight line has zero acceleration.
- Zero acceleration requires zero resultant force (\(\Sigma F = 0\)).
- A car cruising at steady speed on the motorway needs engine thrust only to cancel out resistive forces (drag and friction). If all resistive forces could be removed, the car would coast forward forever with the engine switched off!

### Mistake 3: Confusing an individual applied force with the resultant force
When applying \(F = ma\), students frequently take whatever force is named in the question (such as engine thrust or a pulling force) and insert it directly as \(F\), forgetting that friction or gravity also acts.
- **\(F\) in Newton's second law always means the resultant force \(\Sigma F\).**
- Always draw a free-body sketch or list all forces acting along the line of motion, find their vector sum, and only then equate that sum to \(ma\).

### Mistake 4: Assuming acceleration must be in the direction of motion
Students often assume that because a car travels forward, its acceleration must point forward.
- The direction of motion is given by the **velocity** vector \(\vec{v}\).
- The direction of **acceleration** \(\vec{a}\) is determined strictly by the **resultant force** \(\vec{F}_{\mathrm{net}}\).
- When a body is slowing down, its acceleration and resultant force point in the opposite direction to its velocity.

### Mistake 5: Treating \(F = ma\) as the definition of force
It is common to say that "force is mass times acceleration". However, in Cambridge 9702 Physics, force is fundamentally defined as the **rate of change of momentum** (\(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\)), as seen in Cambridge exam questions. The formula \(F = ma\) is a special case valid only when the mass of the body remains constant during the interaction.

## Core recap

- **Mass** is the property of a body that resists changes in motion. It is a scalar quantity measured in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)).
- **Inertia** is the physical reluctance of any object to change its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line. Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia.
- A **resultant force** is the non-zero vector sum of all individual forces acting on a body.
- When resultant force is zero (\(\Sigma F = 0\)), acceleration is zero and velocity remains constant.
- For a body of constant mass, Newton's second law states:
  \[
  F = ma
  \]
  where \(F\) is the resultant force in newtons (\(\mathrm{N}\)), \(m\) is the mass in kilograms (\(\mathrm{kg}\)), and \(a\) is the acceleration in metres per second squared (\(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)).
- One newton is defined as \(1\,\mathrm{N} = 1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\).
- **Acceleration and resultant force are always in the exact same direction.**
- When analyzing connected or contacting bodies, apply \(F = ma\) to the whole system using total mass to determine acceleration, and apply \(F = ma\) to each separate body using only the forces acting directly on that body.
