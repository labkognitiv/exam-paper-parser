# Why an inward force makes a circular path

## Start with a turning bicycle

Imagine riding a bicycle around a smooth circular track at a steady speed. The
speedometer reading does not change. Yet you are not moving with a constant
**velocity**.

Velocity includes both speed and direction. As the bicycle goes around the
track, its direction changes at every point. A changing velocity means there
is an **acceleration**.

The important question is this: which way must that acceleration point?

At any point on a circular path, the velocity points along the **tangent**. A
tangent is a straight line that touches the circle at one point. The
acceleration points towards the centre of the circle. It is at right angles to
the velocity.

Ask yourself: if the speed is steady, what part of the velocity can still be
changing? Its **direction** is changing.

## The three directions to keep separate

Consider an object moving anticlockwise around a horizontal circle.

- Its **velocity** is tangential to the circle, in the direction it would move
  if the circle suddenly disappeared.
- Its **centripetal acceleration** points inwards, towards the centre.
- The **resultant force** that causes this acceleration also points inwards.

The word **centripetal** means centre-seeking. It describes the direction of
the acceleration or resultant force. It is not an extra type of force that is
added to the situation.

For example, the inward resultant force might be tension in a string, friction
between tyres and a road, a magnetic force, or the contact force from a track.
Different physical forces can have the same centripetal role.

> **Term meaning: centripetal acceleration.** It is the acceleration of an
> object moving in a circular path that is directed towards the centre of the
> circle and is perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity.

The word **instantaneous** means at one particular moment. A moment later, the
object is at a new position. The inward direction has turned with it.

## Why a sideways acceleration does not change the speed

Acceleration can change velocity in two ways. It can change the size of the
velocity, which changes the speed. Or it can change the direction of the
velocity, which changes the direction of travel.

For uniform circular motion, the acceleration is perpendicular to the
velocity. It acts sideways relative to the motion. This turns the velocity
without making its magnitude larger or smaller. The speed stays constant.

This is why the phrase **constant speed** is not enough on its own. The
velocity is still changing because its direction is changing.

Suppose the bicycle is at the eastern edge of the track and is moving north.
The centripetal acceleration points west, towards the centre. A short time
later, at the northern edge, the velocity points west and the centripetal
acceleration points south. Both vectors have turned.

The acceleration is always perpendicular to the current velocity, not merely
perpendicular at the start of the motion.

Pause and choose the more accurate statement about an object travelling around
a circle at constant speed.

- **A:** Its velocity is constant because its speed is constant.
- **B:** Its velocity changes because its direction changes.

**Feedback for A:** Not correct. Speed is only the magnitude of velocity. A
vector changes when either its magnitude or its direction changes. The
direction changes continuously here.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. The unchanged speed means the magnitude of the
velocity is constant, but the turning direction means the velocity vector
changes. Therefore the object has an acceleration.

## The force that keeps the turn happening

An object does not curve by itself. If the inward resultant force stopped, the
object would continue in a straight line tangent to the circle at that instant.
This is the direction of its current velocity.

For the object to keep turning, a force of constant magnitude must remain
perpendicular to its direction of motion. For an object of constant mass, that
force produces a centripetal acceleration with the same inward direction.

Because both the force and acceleration point towards the centre, they turn as
the object turns. Their **directions** change continually. Their magnitudes can
remain constant in uniform circular motion.

Do not say that an outward force is needed to balance the inward force for an
object moving in a circle. There must be an inward **resultant force**. A zero
resultant force would mean no acceleration, so the object would not keep
turning.

### Worked example: a ball on a string

A small ball moves at constant speed in a horizontal circle on a string. Air
resistance is negligible. At one instant, the ball is at the right-hand side of
the circle and is moving upwards.

State the directions of the velocity, the centripetal acceleration, and the
resultant force.

**Step 1: identify the velocity.** The velocity points in the direction of
motion. The ball is moving upwards, so its velocity points upwards. This is the
tangent direction.

**Step 2: identify the acceleration.** Centripetal acceleration points towards
the centre. The centre is to the left of the ball, so the acceleration points
to the left.

**Step 3: identify the resultant force.** The string pulls the ball towards the
centre. The tension provides the inward resultant force, so the resultant force
also points to the left.

**Answer:** velocity upwards; centripetal acceleration left; resultant force
left.

**Check:** the acceleration is perpendicular to the upward velocity. It can
therefore turn the direction of motion while the speed remains constant.

## From centripetal acceleration to constant angular speed

In the earlier lesson, you met **angular speed**. It tells us how rapidly an
object turns through an angle. Constant angular speed means that equal angles
are swept out in equal time intervals.

In uniform circular motion, the inward centripetal acceleration continuously
turns the velocity by equal amounts in equal times. The object continues around
the circle at constant speed and constant angular speed.

The path is circular because the acceleration is always directed towards one
fixed centre. The speed is constant because the acceleration is always
perpendicular to the velocity.

Keep these two ideas separate:

- **Inward direction of acceleration:** makes the path curve.
- **Perpendicular direction to velocity:** keeps the speed constant.

This lesson explains the directions. The next lesson will calculate the
magnitude of centripetal acceleration and the required resultant force.

### Worked example: a car on a level circular track

A car travels around a level circular track at constant speed. The road is dry
enough that the tyres do not slide.

Explain which force provides the centripetal acceleration and why the car does
not travel along a straight line.

**Step 1: identify the available horizontal force.** On a level road, the
weight and the upward contact force act vertically. They cannot turn the car
horizontally. Static friction between the tyres and the road acts horizontally
towards the centre of the track.

**Step 2: connect force to acceleration.** This inward friction is the
resultant horizontal force. It produces centripetal acceleration towards the
centre.

**Step 3: connect acceleration to the path.** The inward acceleration changes
the direction of the car's velocity continuously. The car therefore follows a
circular path instead of moving in a straight line.

**Answer:** static friction provides the inward resultant force and hence the
centripetal acceleration. Without it, there would be no inward acceleration,
so the car would continue along the tangent to the track.

**Check:** the friction is not opposing the car's forward motion in this model.
It is perpendicular to the instantaneous direction of motion and points
towards the centre.

Now imagine that the string in the first example snaps. Which path does the
ball take immediately after the string breaks?

- **A:** It continues along the circular path because it was already turning.
- **B:** It moves along the tangent at the point where the string breaks.

**Feedback for A:** Not correct. Continuing around the circle needs an inward
resultant force and centripetal acceleration. Once the string breaks, the
tension is gone.

**Feedback for B:** Correct. At the instant the string breaks, the ball has a
velocity along the tangent. With no inward resultant force, there is no
centripetal acceleration to turn that velocity.

## A common confusion: constant magnitude is not constant direction

The centripetal acceleration can have a constant magnitude, but its direction
is not constant. It always points to the centre, and the centre lies in a
different direction from the moving object at each new position.

The same is true for the centripetal resultant force. Its magnitude can stay
constant while its direction turns continuously.

This distinction explains a common exam question. For an object moving in a
circle at constant speed, the following quantities vary because they have
direction: **velocity**, **momentum**, **centripetal acceleration**, and
**resultant force**. The **speed** remains constant.

## Core recap

- **Uniform circular motion** has constant speed but changing velocity.
- Velocity is tangential to the circle.
- **Centripetal acceleration** points towards the centre and is perpendicular
  to the instantaneous velocity.
- An inward resultant force causes the centripetal acceleration. The force may
  be tension, friction, a magnetic force, or a contact force.
- The inward acceleration continuously changes the velocity direction, making
  the path circular with **constant angular speed**.
- If the inward force disappears, the object travels along the tangent.

acceleration and the resultant force.
