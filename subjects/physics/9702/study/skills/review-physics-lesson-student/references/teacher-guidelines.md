# Teacher-to-student walkthrough guidelines

Review Student reference · 2026-09-12

A lesson should help a student build an explanation, not simply encounter the correct facts. Write for a 17–18-year-old who is new to the topic and may have weak prerequisites. Be clear and respectful without making the science childish.

## What teacher-led means

Explain what the student is looking at, introduce the necessary idea, and show why the next step follows. Connect those steps before asking the student to apply them.

“You”, “imagine” and “look closely” can help, but they do not make a passage teacher-led on their own. The reasoning must be present. Activities should practise understanding that the explanation has already made possible.

Use short connected paragraphs with one main idea. Keep enough detail to explain
why each step follows. Do not count sentences or rendered lines, or split every
sentence mechanically. Follow the shared lesson workflow's paragraph rule.

These examples show the reasoning to preserve, not a fixed lesson structure.

## Use familiar language around the physical terms

Keep precise physical terms and simplify everything around them. Do not replace a
precise term with a vague one, and do not surround it with idiom, decorative metaphor,
or a formal synonym where a simple word exists. Every everyday noun used structurally
("rails", "steps", "backbone") must be defined on first use or replaced. Introduce each
term once, explain it, then use it consistently.

## Six rules for a difficult passage

These are the repairs that matter, stated as rules. No specimen prose is supplied
deliberately: a sample gets pasted instead of thought about, and this file previously
carried around a hundred lines of finished teaching that a reviewer could lift whole.
Apply the rule to the passage in front of you.

1. **Explain the mechanism, do not announce the outcome.** Fix a viewpoint, follow one
   participant at a time, then say why the awkward part is necessary. A passage that
   states what happens without saying why it must happen that way fails.
2. **Give terminology something concrete to name.** Establish what the symbols,
   positions and end points mean before asking a student to interpret an arrangement of
   them. Any diagram referenced must match the described positions exactly.
3. **Guide observation before identification.** Before any identification claim, the
   text must say where to look, state the diagram convention in use, and separate what
   the visible evidence establishes from what it cannot. Never teach a diagram's
   arbitrary shape code as a universal physical rule.
4. **Explain the choices inside a calculation**, not just the steps: why equate, why
   subtract, why divide. Keep the complete Answer separate from and before the
   Explanation.
5. **Make an analogy explain the science.** Map it to named structures and a real
   consequence, then state its limit. A catchy phrase that replaces the mechanism is a
   defect.
6. **Teach before testing.** The explanation supplies the distinction before an activity
   retrieves it. Essential teaching must never live only inside wrong-answer feedback.

## What to avoid

- Conclusions connected only by “therefore” when the intervening reasoning is missing.
- Several unfamiliar terms introduced together without concrete explanation.
- Instructions to “look” without identifying where to look or what to notice.
- Exam shortcuts used in place of explaining the physics.
- Friendly phrases, praise or metaphors used to cover a gap in reasoning.
- Repetition that merely rewords a conclusion instead of revisiting its cause, evidence or application.
- Excessive detail that introduces a new topic instead of resolving the student’s immediate difficulty.

## Review each difficult passage

Read the teaching before its activity and ask:

1. Does the student have the prerequisite ideas and vocabulary needed here? Are physical terms explained and the surrounding words familiar?
2. Can they follow what happens, where it happens and why the next step follows?
3. Does the diagram support the exact viewpoint and sequence described?
4. Could they explain the result without copying a memorised sentence?
5. Is the activity practising something already explained?
6. Are the explanations broken into short paragraphs with visible breathing room, rather than large prose blocks?

If a step depends on an unstated idea, add that explanation at the point of need. If the passage already makes the reasoning clear, do not inflate it to satisfy a template.

**Acceptance standard:** a beginner should be able to explain why the next step happens using the teaching already provided. Conversational tone alone does not pass.
