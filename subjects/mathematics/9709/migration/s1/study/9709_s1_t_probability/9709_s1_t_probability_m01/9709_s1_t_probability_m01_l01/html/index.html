Probability foundations

List, count,
then calculate.

Probability questions become manageable when you describe outcomes clearly, count the right ones, and divide by the correct total.

Start the lesson

Start with equally likely outcomes

  • An outcome is one possible result. Probability measures chance from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • Equally likely means each result has the same chance. A fair die gives each face the same chance.
  • An event is the outcome or collection of outcomes we ask about.
  • The sample space is the complete list of possible outcomes.
  • P(event) means the probability of that event.
  • A fraction divides its top number by its bottom number. The bottom number is the denominator; here it counts all equally likely outcomes.
  • An outcome is favourable when it matches the event.

Basic probability rule

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total equally likely outcomes

Worked example

A fair six-sided die

The sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. For an even number, the favourable outcomes are {2, 4, 6}.

  1. List every possible outcome once.
  2. Count the favourable outcomes: 3.
  3. Divide by the denominator 6: P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2.
  • Check that the list is exhaustive: no possible result is missing.
  • List first; count second; simplify last.

Read a two-way table

  • Rows describe one category and columns describe another.
  • An intersection is one cell where a row and column meet.
  • For “and”, find the intersection cell, then divide by the grand total.

Worked example

Year 13 and Walk

Choose one student at random, giving each student the same chance. Find the probability that the student is in Year 13 and walks.

  1. The intersection cell is 28.
  2. The denominator is 80.
  3. P(Year 13 and Walk) = 28/80 = 7/20.

  1. Tea and not coffee: 31 − 10 = 21.
  2. Not tea and coffee: 14 − 10 = 4.
  3. Not tea total: 40 − 31 = 9.
  4. Not tea and not coffee: 9 − 4 = 5.
  5. Not coffee total: 40 − 14 = 26. Check 21 + 5 = 26 and 31 + 9 = 40.

Build a sample space

  • For a coin, use H for Head and T for Tail.
  • An ordered pair keeps the first experiment and second experiment in fixed positions.
  • Here the first position always records the coin; the second records the die. For example, (H, 1) means Head and a 1. The coin and die results are assumed not to affect each other.

Worked example

A coin and a three-sided die

A fair coin and a fair die numbered 1, 2, 3 are used. Find P(Head and odd number).

  1. List the six outcomes: (H,1), (H,2), (H,3), (T,1), (T,2), (T,3).
  2. Favourable outcomes are (H,1) and (H,3).
  3. P = 2/6 = 1/3.

Probability from a column total

Choose one member at random. Find the probability that the member plays hockey.

Worked example

Blue or green

Choose one bicycle at random. Find the probability that its colour is blue or green. Each bicycle belongs to exactly one colour category.

  1. Blue and green are separate colour categories.
  2. Favourable count = 90 + 50 = 140.
  3. P(blue or green) = 140/200 = 7/10.

Unequal outcome probabilities

  • When outcomes are not equally likely, count ÷ total does not apply directly.
  • If two experiments are independent, one result does not change the probability of the other.
  • For independent experiments, multiply component probabilities for one combined outcome.
  • Add probabilities of separate valid outcomes.

Worked example

A biased spinner and a fair coin

A biased spinner has outcomes with different chances. Here the spinner and coin act independently. Write S for their total score; ≤ means “less than or equal to”.

The spinner gives 1, 2, 3 with probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. A fair coin adds 1 for H and 0 for T. Find P(total score ≤ 2).

  1. Here ≤ means “less than or equal to”. Valid outcomes: (1,T), (1,H), (2,T).
  2. P(1,T) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25; P(1,H) = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.
  3. P(2,T) = 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15.
  4. P(S ≤ 2) = 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.65.

Two dice and intersections

  • The symbol ∩ means “and”: both events happen.
  • Two fair dice produce 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered pairs.
  • Use a fair red die and a fair blue die, rolled independently. Each pair records (red score, blue score). Event A is an even red score: 2, 4 or 6. Event B is a total of at least 10: 10, 11 or 12.

Worked example

Find P(A ∩ B)

  1. Pairs satisfying both: (4,6), (6,4), (6,5), (6,6).
  2. There are 4 favourable outcomes out of 36.
  3. P(A ∩ B) = 4/36 = 1/9.

Key ideas

  • List every equally likely outcome once.
  • Use an intersection cell for “and”.
  • Use totals for one category.
  • For unequal outcomes, multiply independent components and add valid outcomes.
  • Check exhaustive lists, no repeats, totals and simplified fractions.
