Counting probability

Count the sample space
with care.

When outcomes are equally likely, probability is a count. Choose combinations when order does not matter; choose permutations when order matters.

Start the lesson

Choose the right counting tool

  • A factorial is a product down to 1: 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. We use 0! = 1: there is one way to choose or arrange nothing.
  • A combination, written ⁿCᵣ or nCr and read “n choose r”, counts selections of r objects from n when order does not matter. The objects are distinct, even when their colours or letters match.
  • A permutation, written nPr, counts ordered selections of r objects from n. Here n is the number available and r is the number selected.
  • Keep the denominator as the whole random sample space.
  • Restrictions change only the favourable count, unless a condition changes the sample space.

Counting formulas

nPr = n!/(n − r)! and nCr = n!/[r!(n − r)!], for whole numbers 0 ≤ r ≤ n.

  • Ordered selection gives n choices, then n − 1, and so on. Multiply these choices.
  • For an unordered group, its r! different orders describe the same selection. Divide by r! to remove those repeats.
  • For example, 5C2 = (5 × 4)/(2 × 1) = 10 pairs. AB and BA are the same pair.
  • “Disjoint cases” means cases that do not overlap. A complement is every outcome outside the event, with probability 1 − P(event).

Reliable tools

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes

Use direct counting, a complement, disjoint cases or conditional counting.

Worked example: a selection complement

A box contains 5 red and 13 plain biscuits. Six are selected at random, with every group of six biscuits equally likely. Find the probability that fewer than 2 red biscuits are selected.

  1. “Fewer than 2” means 0 red or 1 red.
  2. 0 red: choose all 6 plain: ⁵C₀ × ¹³C₆ = 1716.
  3. 1 red: ⁵C₁ × ¹³C₅ = 6435.
  4. Favourable selections = 1716 + 6435 = 8151.
  5. Total selections = ¹⁸C₆ = 18564.
  6. P = 8151/18564 = 209/476.

Worked example: a restricted arrangement

A random arrangement of the 8 letters in BALLOONS is chosen. Find the probability that the two Ls are not together.

  1. Total arrangements: 8!/(2!2!) = 10080, because L and O each repeat twice. Temporarily labelling the two Ls would count each visible arrangement 2! times, and the two Os add another factor of 2!. Divide out both repeats.
  2. With LL together, treat LL as one object. Arrange LL, B, A, O, O, N, S. There are seven objects, with only the two Os repeated: 7!/2! = 2520.
  3. Not together: 10080 − 2520 = 7560.
  4. P(not together) = 7560/10080 = 3/4.

Worked example: conditional selection

A tray has 6 strawberry, 5 orange and 4 lemon sweets. Three are selected without replacement. Given that no orange sweet is selected, find the probability that at least 2 are strawberry.

  1. Given no orange, the restricted tray has 6 strawberry and 4 lemon: 10 sweets.
  2. Restricted total = ¹⁰C₃ = 120.
  3. Exactly 2 strawberry: ⁶C₂ × ⁴C₁ = 60.
  4. Exactly 3 strawberry: ⁶C₃ = 20.
  5. Favourable = 80, so P = 80/120 = 2/3.

Selections with combinations

A committee of 4 is chosen from 10 people. Find the probability that two particular people are both chosen.

  1. Total committees = ¹⁰C₄ = 210.
  2. The two particular people are fixed, so choose the remaining 2 from the other 8: ⁸C₂ = 28.
  3. P(both chosen) = 28/210 = 2/15.

For a random selection, denominator first; then enforce the condition.

Exactly one from a category

A bag contains 6 red and 5 blue counters. Four are selected without replacement. Find the probability exactly one is red.

  1. Total selections = ¹¹C₄ = 330.
  2. Choose 1 red from 6 and 3 blue from 5: ⁶C₁ × ⁵C₃ = 60.
  3. P(exactly 1 red) = 60/330 = 2/11.

Fixed positions in arrangements

A random arrangement of the 7 letters in BALLOON is chosen. Find the probability B is first and N is last.

  1. Total arrangements = 7!/(2!2!) = 1260.
  2. Fix B first and N last. Arrange A, L, L, O, O in 5!/(2!2!) = 30 ways.
  3. P = 30/1260 = 1/42.

Fix restricted positions before arranging what remains.

Together or not together

A random arrangement of the 6 letters in PEPPER is chosen. Find the probability the two Es are not together.

  1. Total arrangements = 6!/(3!2!) = 60, for three Ps and two Es.
  2. Treat EE as one block. Arrange EE, P, P, P, R; divide by 3! for the identical Ps. Arrangements with Es together = 5!/3! = 20.
  3. Not together = 60 − 20 = 40.
  4. P = 40/60 = 2/3.

Conditional counting

A random arrangement of BALLOON is chosen. The bar | in a conditional probability means “given that”. Given the two Ls are together, find the probability the two Os are together.

  1. Given LL together, arrange LL, B, A, O, O, N: restricted total = 6!/2! = 360.
  2. For LL and OO together, arrange LL, OO, B, A, N: favourable = 5! = 120.
  3. P(OO together | LL together) = 120/360 = 1/3.

The condition changes the denominator: count only arrangements where LL is together.

Enumerate disjoint cases

Four letter tiles are selected uniformly at random from 3 As, 2 Ds and 4 other distinct letters. Each physical tile is distinct for selection counting, including tiles with matching letters. Find the probability of exactly one A and at least one D.

CaseCount
1 A, 1 D, 2 others³C₁ × ²C₁ × ⁴C₂ = 36
1 A, 2 Ds, 1 other³C₁ × ²C₂ × ⁴C₁ = 12
  1. Cases do not overlap, so favourable = 36 + 12 = 48.
  2. Total selections = ⁹C₄ = 126.
  3. P = 48/126 = 8/21.

Two people in the same group

Eleven people are divided at random into a group of 6 and a group of 5. Find the probability Mina and Noor are in the same group.

  1. Both in the group of 6: (6/11)(5/10) = 3/11.
  2. Both in the group of 5: (5/11)(4/10) = 2/11.
  3. These cases are disjoint, so P(same group) = 3/11 + 2/11 = 5/11.

Order creates multipliers

One of four equally likely animal cards is received each week, independently of earlier weeks. Find the probability that after 4 weeks there is exactly one of each animal.

  1. One fixed order needs the specified animal each week. Multiply four factors: (1/4)⁴ = (1/4) × (1/4) × (1/4) × (1/4). The power 4 abbreviates these four factors.
  2. The four different animals can appear in 4! orders.
  3. P(one of each) = 4! × (1/4)⁴ = 24/256 = 3/32.

When category counts repeat, divide the order multiplier by factorials of repeated counts.

Key ideas

  • Use combinations when order does not matter.
  • Use permutations or factorials when order matters.
  • For a condition, restrict the denominator first.
  • For “not”, a complement can be shorter.
  • Separate non-overlapping cases can be added.
