Cumulative frequency curves

Move to the curve.
Read the other axis.

A cumulative frequency curve turns a running total into a picture. Read it in either direction to find counts, values, percentiles and quartiles.

Start the lesson

What the curve means

  • A frequency is a count of values.
  • Cumulative means building a running total.
  • A cumulative frequency at a chosen value counts all observations at or below that value.
  • The horizontal axis shows the measured value. It runs left to right.
  • The vertical axis shows cumulative frequency. It runs bottom to top.
  • A point written (32, 48) uses horizontal value 32 and vertical cumulative frequency 48.
  • Grouped data are joined by a smooth curve. A reading between known plotted points is therefore an estimate, not an exact raw value.
  • The curve must stay level or rise. A running total cannot decrease.

Value to count

  1. Start at value x = 32.
  2. Move vertically to the curve.
  3. Move horizontally to the cumulative-frequency axis.

48 values are at or below 32.

Count to value

  1. Start at cumulative frequency 48.
  2. Move horizontally to the curve.
  3. Move vertically down to the value axis.

The corresponding value is 32.

Counts between two values

  • A cumulative reading is the total up to that value.
  • To count between two values, subtract the lower cumulative reading from the upper cumulative reading.
  • Subtract the counts, not the values on the horizontal axis.

Worked example

Read two counts from one curve

Solution

  1. At x = 18, cumulative frequency is 24.
  2. At x = 38, cumulative frequency is 84.
  3. Between 18 and 38: 84 - 24 = 60.

There are 60 values between 18 and 38.

Worked example

Seedlings between 12 cm and 28 cm

The curve represents the heights of 150 seedlings. Estimate how many seedlings have heights between 12 cm and 28 cm.

Solution

  1. Read an estimated cumulative frequency of 30 at 12 cm.
  2. Read an estimated cumulative frequency of 108 at 28 cm.
  3. 108 - 30 = 78.

About 78 seedlings have heights between 12 cm and 28 cm.

Convert a percentile, then read

  • A percentage describes a number out of 100.
  • The p-th percentile is the data value with p% of the observations at or below it.
  • The letter N means the total frequency.
  • The target cumulative frequency is p/100 × N.
  • The percentile answer is a data value on the horizontal axis, not the target count.
  1. Find total frequency N.
  2. Calculate the target count pN/100.
  3. Move from that count to the curve, then down to the value axis.

Worked example

The 65th percentile of 160 values

Solution

  1. 65/100 × 160 = 104.
  2. Start at cumulative frequency 104.
  3. Move to the curve, then down to read the corresponding data value.

The target count is 104; the percentile is the value read from the curve.

Worked example

The 70th percentile of 140 training times

The training times of 140 runners are shown. Estimate the 70th percentile.

Solution

  1. 70% of 140 = 98. This target count is exact.
  2. Move across from cumulative frequency 98 to the curve.
  3. Move down to the time axis and estimate 42 minutes.

The 70th percentile is about 42 minutes.

For “longer than”, use the complement

  • A cumulative curve counts from the bottom: values at or below a cut-off.
  • A complement is the percentage left after subtracting from 100%.
  • If 30% are longer than a cut-off, then 100% - 30% = 70% are at or below it.

Worked example

Thirty per cent of 180 journeys are longer than T

Estimate the cut-off time T from the cumulative frequency curve.

Solution

  1. 100% - 30% = 70% are at or below T.
  2. 0.70 × 180 = 126. This target count is exact.
  3. Read from cumulative frequency 126 to the curve, then down.

T ≈ 54 minutes.

Read quartiles and the interquartile range

  • A quartile is a data value that divides ordered data into quarters.
  • Q₁, the lower quartile, has 25% of the data at or below it.
  • The median has 50% at or below it.
  • Q₃, the upper quartile, has 75% at or below it.
  • The interquartile range, or IQR, is Q₃ - Q₁. It measures the spread of the middle 50%.

Targets for N values

Q₁: N/4; median: N/2; Q₃: 3N/4

Worked example

Quartiles from a curve with total frequency 120

Estimate Q₁, the median, Q₃ and the interquartile range.

Solution

  1. 120/4 = 30, so estimate Q₁ ≈ 18.
  2. 120/2 = 60, so the estimated median is 28.
  3. 3(120)/4 = 90, so estimate Q₃ ≈ 39.
  4. IQR ≈ 39 - 18 = 21.

The estimated IQR is about 21. It subtracts two data values, not two cumulative frequencies.

Key ideas

  • Value to count: move up to the curve, then across.
  • Count to value: move across to the curve, then down.
  • Between two values: upper cumulative reading minus lower cumulative reading.
  • For the p-th percentile: calculate pN/100, then read the data value.
  • For “more than” or “longer than”: first subtract the percentage from 100%.
  • Quartile targets are N/4, N/2, 3N/4.
  • IQR = Q₃ - Q₁.

Worked example

Counts and percentages for 200 completion times

The curve gives cumulative frequency 44 at 25 seconds and 156 at 55 seconds. Find (a) the number of players with times between 25 and 55 seconds, and (b) the percentage with times longer than 55 seconds.

Solution

  1. Between 25 and 55 seconds: 156 - 44 = 112 players.
  2. Longer than 55 seconds: 200 - 156 = 44 players.
  3. 44/200 × 100 = 22%.

112 players are between the times; 22% are longer than 55 seconds.

Worked example

The 62.5th percentile of 160 delivery times

Use the delivery-time curve to estimate the 62.5th percentile.

Solution

  1. 62.5% of 160 = 100. This target count is exact.
  2. Move from cumulative frequency 100 to the curve, then down and estimate the time.

The 62.5th percentile is about 48 minutes.

Worked example

Thirty-five per cent of 240 swimmers took longer than W

Use the race-time curve to estimate the cut-off W.

Solution

  1. 100% - 35% = 65% are at or below W.
  2. 0.65 × 240 = 156. This target count is exact.
  3. Read from cumulative frequency 156 to the curve, then down and estimate the time.

W ≈ 76 seconds.
