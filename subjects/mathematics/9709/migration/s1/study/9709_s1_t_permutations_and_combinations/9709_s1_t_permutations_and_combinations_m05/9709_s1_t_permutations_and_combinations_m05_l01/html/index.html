Mixed counting synthesis

Choose the model, then count once.

A mixed counting problem becomes manageable when you identify what makes one outcome different, enforce its restrictions, and combine complete cases.

Start the lesson · Key ideas

Choose the counting model

  • Distinct objects can be told apart.
  • An ordered result changes when its positions are swapped. Use a permutation or sequential choices.
  • An unordered selection stays the same when its objects are rearranged. Use a combination.
  • Multiply successive stages that build one outcome.
  • Add disjoint cases, which cannot describe the same outcome.
  • Cases are exhaustive when they include every allowed outcome.

Permutation and combination formulas

  • The symbol ! is read “factorial”: 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. By definition, 0! = 1.
  • n is the number of distinct objects available. r is the number used.
  • Both are whole numbers with 0 ≤ r ≤ n. The symbol ≤ means “less than or equal to”, and ≥ means “greater than or equal to”.
  • nPr counts ordered arrangements of r objects chosen from n.
  • nCr counts unordered selections of r objects chosen from n.
  • In a fraction, the numerator is above the line and the denominator is below it.

nPr = n!(n − r)!   and   nCr = n!r!(n − r)!

Ordered positions

Worked example: concert programme

A concert programme lists 4 performers in order. There are 11 applicants. How many programmes are possible?

  1. The four positions are different, so order matters.
  2. There are 11 choices for the first position, then 10, 9 and 8.
  3. Each selected performer is unavailable at the next stage.
  4. 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 11P4 = 11!7! = 7920 programmes.

Check-in 1

Seven distinct medals fill three labelled display positions. How many displays are possible?

Build cases before calculating

  1. Translate each condition into allowed category totals.
  2. List every whole-number case, such as (a, b, c).
  3. Within one case, choose from each category and multiply.
  4. Add the cases because their category totals differ.
  5. Audit: restrictions enforced, cases exhaustive, no overlap.

At least means the stated value or more. At most means the stated value or less.

One chosen share fixes the other

Worked example: sharing books

An odd whole number is not divisible by 2, such as 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9.

Ten different books are shared between Ava and Ben. Each person must receive an odd number of books. How many shares are possible?

  1. Let Ava receive r books. Then Ben receives 10 − r.
  2. Both totals are odd when r = 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9.
  3. 10C1 + 10C3 + 10C5 + 10C7 + 10C9
  4. = 10 + 120 + 252 + 120 + 10 = 512 shares.

Choosing Ava’s books fixes Ben’s remaining books, so there is no second combination to multiply.

Fix a restricted position first

Worked example: repeated digits

An even number ends in an even digit.

The digits 1, 2, 4, 4, 4, 5 form six-digit numbers. How many different even numbers can be formed? Here the final digit is 2 or 4.

Repeated identical copies are indistinguishable: exchanging two equal 4s does not make a new number. If k identical copies remain, an unrestricted factorial count repeats each number k! times, so divide by k!.

  1. Case 1: end in 2. Arrange 1, 4, 4, 4, 5. The three equal 4s create 3! repeated orders: 5!3! = 20.
  2. Case 2: end in 4. Arrange 1, 2, 4, 4, 5. The two remaining equal 4s create 2! repeated orders: 5!2! = 60.
  3. The cases are disjoint and exhaustive.
  4. Total = 20 + 60 = 80 numbers.

Fix the restricted position before adjusting for repeated copies.

Counting synthesis checklist

1. Model

Ordered positions use permutations or sequential choices. Unordered selections use combinations.

2. Restrict

Fix required positions, objects kept together, odd or even conditions, and minimum category counts before counting.

3. Combine

Multiply stages within one outcome. Add disjoint cases.

4. Audit

Check every case is included with no overlap, divide out repeated objects, and divide if exchanging whole equal groups counts the same division twice.

Ask: What identifies one outcome? Have I counted it exactly once?

More worked examples

Unequal and equal groups

Nine students are divided into a group of 4 and a group of 5. How many divisions are possible?

  1. Choose the group of 4. The remaining 5 are fixed.
  2. The unequal sizes identify the groups.
  3. 9C4 = 126 divisions.

Unlabelled groups have no names or roles.

How does the method change for 8 students divided into two unlabelled groups of 4?

  1. 8C4 = 70 counts every division twice because either group could be chosen first.
  2. That exchange is a symmetry: it changes the description but not the division.
  3. 8C42 = 702 = 35 divisions.

Six unique postcards are divided into two unlabelled groups of 3. How many divisions are possible?

Restricted sports team

Choose a team of 7 from 7 attackers, 6 defenders and 4 midfielders. It must contain at least 3 attackers, at least 2 defenders and at least 1 midfielder. How many teams are possible?

  1. Let (A, D, M) be the category totals. We need A + D + M = 7 with A ≥ 3, D ≥ 2, M ≥ 1.
  2. The minima use 6 places, so the seventh place creates exactly three cases.
All allowed category totals
(A, D, M)CalculationTeams
(3, 2, 2)7C3 × 6C2 × 4C2 = 35 × 15 × 63150
(4, 2, 1)7C4 × 6C2 × 4C1 = 35 × 15 × 42100
(3, 3, 1)7C3 × 6C3 × 4C1 = 35 × 20 × 42800

Total = 3150 + 2100 + 2800 = 8050 teams.

Odd three-digit numbers greater than 600

How many three-digit odd numbers greater than 600 have no repeated digit?

An odd number ends in 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9. “No repeated digit” means all three positions contain different digits.

  1. The hundreds digit is 6, 7, 8 or 9.
  2. If that digit is odd, it cannot also be the final digit.

  4. Total = 40 + 32 + 40 + 32 = 144 numbers.

Mixed gift-box selection

A gift box contains 7 different chocolates, 6 different biscuits and 5 different sweets. Select 7 items with at least 2 of each type. How many selections are possible?

  1. Let (C, B, S) be the type totals. Since 2 + 2 + 2 = 6, the extra item creates three type-count cases.
All allowed type-count cases
(C, B, S)CalculationSelections
(3, 2, 2)7C3 × 6C2 × 5C2 = 35 × 15 × 105250
(2, 3, 2)7C2 × 6C3 × 5C2 = 21 × 20 × 104200
(2, 2, 3)7C2 × 6C2 × 5C3 = 21 × 15 × 103150

Multiply within each type-count case, then add the three disjoint cases.

Total = 5250 + 4200 + 3150 = 12 600 selections.
