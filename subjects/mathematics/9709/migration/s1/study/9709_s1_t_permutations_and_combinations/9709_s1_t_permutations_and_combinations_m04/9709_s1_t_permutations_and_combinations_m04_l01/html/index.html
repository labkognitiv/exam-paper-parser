Choosing groups

Choose within each category, then multiply.

A committee has no first, second or third position. Its members are selected, not arranged. When the required members come from separate categories, make each selection and combine the counts.

Start the lesson · See the key ideas

Order does not make a new group

  • A combination is a selection in which order does not matter.
  • The objects being chosen are distinct: each person or object can be told apart.
  • Choosing A, B and C gives the same committee as choosing C, A and B.
  • Let n be the number of objects available.
  • Let r be the number selected.
  • The notation ⁿCᵣ, read “n choose r”, counts the combinations.

Factorial

For a positive integer n, n! means n × (n − 1) × ⋯ × 2 × 1. Also, 0! = 1.

Combination formula

ⁿCᵣ = n! / [r!(n − r)!]

  • Use whole numbers with 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
  • The factorial division removes repeated orderings of the same selected group.
  • When a final group needs selections from separate categories, count each category separately.
  • The choices are independent when making one selection does not remove choices from the other category.
  • The product rule says to multiply the numbers of ways for successive independent choices.

Worked example

Two category choices

Choose 3 from a group of 7 and 2 from a separate group of 5.

  1. First category: ⁷C₃ = 35.

  2. Second category: ⁵C₂ = 10.

  3. ⁷C₃ × ⁵C₂ = 35 × 10 = 350

    There are 350 combined selections.

Worked example

Exactly two men and four women

A committee of 6 is chosen from 7 men and 9 women. How many committees contain exactly 2 men and 4 women?

Solution

  1. Choose 2 of the 7 men.

    ⁷C₂ = 21

  2. Choose 4 of the 9 women.

    ⁹C₄ = 126

  3. The categories are separate, so multiply.

    ⁷C₂ × ⁹C₄ = 21 × 126 = 2646

  4. 2 + 4 = 6, so the category counts fill the committee.

    There are 2646 committees.

Worked example

Exactly one junior

A team of 5 is chosen from 4 juniors and 7 seniors. Exactly 1 junior must be included. How many teams are possible?

Solution

  1. Exactly 1 junior means the other 5 − 1 = 4 members are seniors.

  2. Choose the junior: ⁴C₁ = 4.

  3. Choose the seniors: ⁷C₄ = 35.

  4. ⁴C₁ × ⁷C₄ = 4 × 35 = 140

    There are 140 teams.

Include a named person first

  • If a particular person must be included, that membership is already decided.
  • The fixed person contributes one way. Do not give that person an ordered seat.
  • Reduce both the available pool and the remaining team size by 1.
  • Then choose the remaining members.

One named person must be included

From n people, choose a team of r that must include one particular person.

The remaining r − 1 members are chosen from the other n − 1 people.

Worked example

Aisha must be included

A committee of 6 is chosen from 13 students. Aisha must be included. How many committees are possible?

Solution

  1. Aisha fills one membership place. Five members remain to be selected.

  2. Choose those 5 from the other 12 students.

    ¹²C₅ = 792

    There are 792 committees.

Key ideas

  • Unordered groupUse combinations because rearranging the same members does not make a new group.
  • Separate categoriesChoose the required number within each category, then multiply.
  • Fixed memberInclude the named person once, then reduce the pool and team size by one.
  • Check totalsThe selected category counts must add to the required group size.

Worked example

Two categories

A committee of 7 is chosen from 8 teachers and 10 students. It must contain exactly 3 teachers.

Solution

  1. The other 7 − 3 = 4 members are students.

  2. Choose 3 teachers and 4 students.

    ⁸C₃ × ¹⁰C₄ = 56 × 210 = 11760

    There are 11 760 committees.

Worked example

Three categories

A group contains 5 adults, 6 teenagers and 4 children. A team of 6 must contain exactly 2 adults, 3 teenagers and 1 child.

Solution

  1. Choose 2 adults: ⁵C₂ = 10.

  2. Choose 3 teenagers: ⁶C₃ = 20.

  3. Choose 1 child: ⁴C₁ = 4.

  4. Check the size: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6.

  5. ⁵C₂ × ⁶C₃ × ⁴C₁ = 10 × 20 × 4 = 800

    There are 800 teams.

Worked example

Allocating passengers to labelled cars

Twelve passengers must be placed into three labelled cars. Their drivers are fixed. Car A needs 5 passengers, car B needs 4 and car C needs 3. How many allocations are possible?

Car A5 passengersCar B4 passengersCar C3 passengers

Solution

  1. Choose 5 of the 12 passengers for car A.

    ¹²C₅ = 792

  2. Seven passengers remain. Choose 4 of them for car B.

    ⁷C₄ = 35

  3. The final 3 passengers go to car C, so ³C₃ = 1.

  4. ¹²C₅ × ⁷C₄ × ³C₃ = 792 × 35 × 1 = 27720

  5. The cars are labelled A, B and C, so exchanging their passenger groups changes the allocation. Do not divide by 3!.

    There are 27 720 allocations.
