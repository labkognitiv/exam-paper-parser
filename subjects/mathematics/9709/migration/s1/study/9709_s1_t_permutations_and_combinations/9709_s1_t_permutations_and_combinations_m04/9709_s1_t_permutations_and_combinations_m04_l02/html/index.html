Repeated-letter selections

Count choices, not positions.

A selection records which letters you take. Order does not matter, and repeated copies of the same letter are indistinguishable.

Start the lesson · Key ideas

What makes a selection different?

  • Distinct items can be told apart.
  • A selection records which items are chosen, without an order.
  • AB and BA are the same selection.
  • Repeated copies of the same letter are indistinguishable: choosing the first A or second A does not produce a new selection.
  • A multiplicity is the number of copies of one letter that are chosen or available.
  • BANANA has inventory A × 3, N × 2, B × 1.
  • Count allowed multiplicities, not physical positions.

Combination notation counts unordered choices

  • The symbol ! is read “factorial”. It means multiply descending whole numbers.
  • For example, 4! = 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24.
  • By definition, 0! = 1.
  • nCr counts ways to select r distinct items from n distinct available items when order does not matter.
  • Here n is the number available and r is the number selected.
  • Both n and r are whole numbers, with 0 ≤ r ≤ n.
  • Each unordered group appears in r! different orders, so the formula divides those orders out.

Combination formula

nCr = n!r!(n − r)!

Use this directly only for genuinely distinct options. Repeated copies need multiplicity cases.

Build complete, non-overlapping cases

  • At least two means two or more.
  • At most two means zero, one or two.
  • Exactly two means two and no other value.
  • Disjoint cases cannot describe the same final selection.
  • Exhaustive cases include every allowed final selection.
  1. Inventory the letter types and their multiplicities.
  2. List the allowed multiplicities for each type.
  3. Build disjoint, exhaustive cases.
  4. Count each case once, then add.

Worked example: BANANA

How many different selections of 4 letters from BANANA contain at least two As?

  1. Inventory: A × 3, N × 2, B × 1.
  2. Case 1: exactly 2 As. Two spaces remain. The valid pairs from N, N, B are NN and NB: 2 selections.
  3. Case 2: exactly 3 As. One space remains. Choose N or B: 2 selections.
  4. The cases are disjoint and cover every allowed A count.
  5. Total = 2 + 2 = 4 selections.

Keep the fixed multiplicity visible

Your turn: SUCCESS

Four letters are selected from SUCCESS. How many selections contain exactly two Ss and at least one C?

  1. Inventory: S × 3, C × 2, U × 1, E × 1.
  2. Every selection must start with exactly SS.
  3. Case 1: exactly one C. Add U or E: SSCU, SSCE. That gives 2 selections.
  4. Case 2: exactly two Cs. Take SSCC. That gives 1 selection.
  5. Total = 2 + 1 = 3 selections.

Fixing can be a safe shortcut

Worked example: MINCEMEAT

How many 5-letter selections contain at least one M and at least one E?

  1. Inventory: M × 2, E × 2, and I, N, C, A, T each × 1.
  2. Fix one M and one E. This guarantees both minimum conditions.
  3. Three letters remain to be selected.
  4. The remaining physical letters M, E, I, N, C, A, T are now seven distinct options.
  5. Select 3 of those 7 options: 7C3 = 35.
  6. Answer: 35 selections.
  • Do not multiply by 5!: order is irrelevant.
  • This shortcut is safe because each final selection leaves one unique set of three letters after the fixed M and E are removed.
  • If fixing could produce the same final selection more than once, use disjoint cases instead.

Key ideas

Inventory

Record each letter type and its available multiplicity.

Translate limits

At least gives a minimum. At most gives a maximum. Exactly fixes one value.

Build cases

Make them non-overlapping and complete, then count each final selection once.

Use combinations carefully

Use nCr for unordered choices among distinct remaining options. Do not distinguish identical copies.

More worked examples

TATTERED

Four letters are selected from TATTERED. Find the number containing exactly one E and at least one T.

  1. Inventory: T × 3, E × 2, A × 1, R × 1, D × 1.
  2. Fix exactly one E.
  3. Case 1: one T. Choose two of A, R, D: 3C2 = 3.
  4. Case 2: two Ts. Choose one of A, R, D: 3 choices.
  5. Case 3: three Ts. No other letter is needed: 1 choice.
  6. Total = 3 + 3 + 1 = 7 selections.

BALLOON

Four letters are selected from BALLOON. Find the number containing at least one L and at least one O.

  1. Inventory: L × 2, O × 2, and B, A, N each × 1.
  2. Case 1: 1L, 1O. Choose two of B, A, N: 3C2 = 3.
  3. Case 2: 2L, 1O. Choose one of B, A, N: 3.
  4. Case 3: 1L, 2O. Choose one of B, A, N: 3.
  5. Case 4: 2L, 2O. Choose no singles: 3C0 = 1.
  6. Total = 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 10 selections.
