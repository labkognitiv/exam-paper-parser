Counting ordered outcomes

How many arrangements are possible?

Count choices one stage at a time. Then decide whether order matters and whether any objects are identical.

Start the lesson · Key ideas

Build a count from choices

  • A counting question asks how many outcomes are possible.
  • An outcome is one complete result, such as the code A1.
  • A stage is one step in making that result.
  • If one stage has a choices and the next has b choices, every first choice can pair with every second choice.
  • That gives a × b outcomes. This is the product rule.
  • Multiply for successive stages. Add only when counting separate cases.

Worked example

A code has one letter from A, B, C, D, followed by one digit from 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

  1. There are 4 choices for the letter.
  2. For each letter, there are 5 choices for the digit.
  3. Total = 4 × 5 = 20 codes.

Write the number of choices at every stage before multiplying.

Factorials shorten descending products

  • Distinct objects can be told apart. Two named people or two different bicycles are distinct.
  • When you arrange distinct objects, a used object cannot be used again.
  • The number of choices decreases by 1 at each stage.
  • The symbol ! is read “factorial”.
  • For a positive whole number n, n! means multiply all whole numbers from n down to 1.
  • By definition, 0! = 1.

Factorial

5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120

Worked example

Six different people stand in a line. Each position is distinct: first, second, and so on.

  1. The first position has 6 choices.
  2. The next positions have 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 choices.
  3. 6! = 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 720.

Choose and order some objects

  • A permutation is an arrangement in which order matters.
  • Gold, silver, bronze is different from bronze, silver, gold.
  • nPr counts ordered arrangements of r objects chosen from n distinct objects.
  • Here n is the number available and r is the number placed.
  • In a fraction, the numerator is above the fraction bar.
  • The denominator is below it.
  • Here the denominator removes the unused tail of the factorial.

Permutation formula

nPr = n!(n − r)!

Use it when objects and positions are distinct, no object repeats, and order matters.

Worked example

Choose and order 3 medal winners from 8 runners.

  1. There are 8 choices for gold, then 7 for silver, then 6 for bronze.
  2. 8P3 = 8!5! = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336.

Check-in 1

Seven runners compete for three different podium places. Which count is correct?

Worked example: filling spaces

Six different bicycles are parked in 10 numbered spaces in a line.

  1. The first bicycle has 10 choices.
  2. The next bicycles have 9, 8, 7, 6 and 5 choices.
  3. 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 = 10P6
  4. 10P6 = 10!4! = 151 200.
  • The four spaces left empty need no separate choice.
  • Choosing the six occupied positions in order already identifies both each bicycle and its space.

Repeated objects cause overcounting

  • If all n objects were distinct, there would be n! arrangements.
  • Swapping two identical objects does not make a new visible arrangement.
  • If one object repeats a times, each visible arrangement was counted a! times.
  • Divide by one factorial for every repeated object type.

Repeated-object arrangements

Number = n!a! b! …

Worked example

LEVEL has 5 letters. L occurs twice and E occurs twice.

  1. Start with 5! as if every letter were distinct.
  2. Divide by 2! for the two Ls.
  3. Divide by 2! for the two Es.
  4. 5!2! 2! = 1204 = 30.

Your turn

BALLOONS has 8 letters. L occurs twice and O occurs twice. B, A, N and S each occur once.

  1. Count every letter before writing the denominator.
  2. 8!2! 2! = 40 3204 = 10 080.

Check-in 2

MAMMA has three Ms and two As. Which denominator removes every identical-letter overcount?

Key ideas

Successive choices

Multiply the number of available choices at each stage.

a choices, then b choices → ab outcomes

Order matters

Use a factorial or a permutation.

n!   or   nPr = n!(n−r)!

Identical objects

Divide by the factorial of each repeat count.

n!a!b!…

Before calculating

Ask: what is each stage, does order matter, and are any objects identical?

More worked examples

Parking spaces

Four different cars occupy four of 15 spaces in a line.

  1. Choose and order 4 of the 15 spaces.
  2. 15P4 = 15 × 14 × 13 × 12 = 32 760.
  3. Check: 15P4 = 15!/11! = 32 760.

Different cars and different positions make order matter.

Word arrangements

SUCCESSOR has 9 letters. S occurs 3 times and C occurs 2 times; U, E, O and R occur once.

  1. Begin with 9!.
  2. Divide by 3! for the Ss and by 2! for the Cs.
  3. 9!3! 2! = 362 88012 = 30 240.

Two labelled rows

Eight different students sit in rows A and B, four seats in each. Lina and Omar must both sit in row A.

  1. Six other students remain. Choose 2 of them to join Lina and Omar.
  2. There are 6 × 5 ordered choices for the pair.
  3. Each pair is counted twice because choosing Kai then Noor gives the same pair as Noor then Kai.
  4. Divide by 2: (6 × 5)/2 = 15. This is written 6C2 = 15.
  5. 6C2 counts a choice of 2 from 6 where the order of choosing does not matter.
  6. Arrange the 4 students in row A in 4! ways.
  7. Arrange the remaining 4 students in row B in 4! ways.
  8. 15 × 4! × 4! = 15 × 24 × 24 = 8640.

Rows A and B are labelled, so they are different positions. Do not divide by 2.
