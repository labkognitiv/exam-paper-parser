Foundations & exact values

Find the angle.
Keep the exact value.

You know one side of a right triangle and one angle. How can you find another side without measuring it? Three ratios connect the picture to a calculation.

Start the lesson · Return to the essentials

Start with the angle you care about

  • Imagine a straight ramp meeting level ground.
  • Draw the ramp, a line along the ground underneath it, and a line straight down from its top to the ground.
  • Those three straight sides enclose a triangle.
  • The ground line is horizontal: it goes across.
  • The line straight up or down is vertical.
  • We want to connect the steepness of the ramp to the lengths of these sides.
  • A triangle has three corners.
  • A corner is called a vertex;
  • more than one are vertices.
  • Two sides meet at each corner.
  • The angle measures the turn from one of those sides to the other, inside the triangle.
  • A wider opening means a larger angle.
  • A right angle is a quarter-turn, written 90°.
  • The little ° means degrees.
  • A corner of a rectangular sheet of paper is a right angle.
  • A right-angled triangle has one such corner;
  • the small square drawn at that corner marks it.
  • An acute angle is greater than 0° and less than 90°.
  • We use the Greek letter θ\thetaθ (theta) to name the angle we are looking from.

Definition · Triangle side names

The hypotenuse is the side opposite the right angle.

  • First put your finger on the corner marked θ.
  • The opposite side is the one that does not touch that corner.
  • Two sides do touch it.
  • One is the hypotenuse;
  • the other is the adjacent side.
  • The hypotenuse is also the longest side of a right-angled triangle.
  • If you choose the other acute angle, opposite and adjacent swap names.
  • The hypotenuse stays the same.
  • A ratio compares two lengths by dividing one by the other.
  • A rise of 3 cm and a run of 4 cm give rise ÷ run = 3/4.
  • The top number of a fraction is the numerator;
  • the bottom number is the denominator.
  • Here 3/4 means 3 divided by 4.
  • Measure both sides in the same unit: cm divided by cm cancels, so the ratio is a number without a length unit.
  • Now double every side of that triangle.
  • The rise becomes 6 and the run becomes 8, but 6/8 = 3/4.
  • The angles stay the same.
  • Triangles with the same three angles are called similar: one is an enlarged or reduced copy of the other.
  • This is why a ratio can depend on an angle without depending on how large the triangle is.

  • CAH remembers cosine, adjacent, hypotenuse;

  • Recall that a half-turn is 180° or π radians.

Build the two triangles, one step at a time

  • We will use two particular right-angled triangles to find exact values.
  • First, let us see why their angles and side lengths have those values.

Definition · Equilateral triangle

An equilateral triangle has three equal sides. Its three angles are equal too.

  • The three inside angles of any triangle add to 180°.
  • If all three are equal, each is 180° ÷ 3 = 60°.
  • We can choose an equilateral triangle whose sides are each 2 units long;
  • only the ratios will matter.
  • Label its top corner A and its bottom corners B and C.
  • A letter names a point;
  • BC means the straight side joining B to C.
  • Call BC the base, the side we have chosen to draw along the bottom.
  • A midpoint is halfway along a side.
  • Put M halfway along BC.
  • Since BC is 2, both BM and MC have length 1.
  • Draw a straight line from A to M.
  • This is what “halve the triangle” means here: divide the picture into two equal triangles using that line.
  • You do not halve all three side lengths.
  • The outside sloping sides still have length 2.
    • The equilateral triangle is symmetric about AM: imagine folding along the dashed line, and the two halves match.
    • The 60° angle at A splits into two equal 30° angles.
    • At M, the two equal angles sit on a straight line, so together they make 180°.
    • Each is 90°.
    • The angle at B stays 60°.
    • Each half therefore has angles 30°, 60° and 90°.
    • In either half, the side opposite the new right angle is an original sloping side, length 2.
    • This is the hypotenuse.
    • The half-base, opposite 30°, is length 1.
    • The new line AM is the remaining side.

Pythagoras: find the missing side

  • For a right-angled triangle, square each of the two shorter side lengths and add them.
  • The result equals the hypotenuse squared.
  • This is Pythagoras’ theorem.
  • It applies only when the triangle has a right angle.
short side2+other short side2=hypotenuse2\text{short side}^2+\text{other short side}^2=\text{hypotenuse}^2short side2+other short side2=hypotenuse2

  1. In triangle ABM, the shorter sides are 1 and AM, and the hypotenuse is 2:

    12+AM2=221^2+AM^2=2^212+AM2=22

  2. Work out the squares, then subtract 1 from both sides:

    1+AM2=41+AM^2=41+AM2=4
    AM2=4−1=3AM^2=4-1=3AM2=4−1=3

  3. Find the positive number whose square is 3. A side length is positive, so:

    AM=3AM=\sqrt3AM=3​

Definition · Isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has two equal sides. The angles opposite those equal sides are equal.

  • Now choose a right-angled triangle whose two shorter sides both have length 1.
  • These equal sides make it isosceles.
  • Its right angle uses 90° of the total 180°, leaving 90° for the other two equal angles.
  • Each is 90° ÷ 2 = 45°.

  1. Use Pythagoras again. This time the hypotenuse is the missing side:

    hypotenuse2=12+12=2\text{hypotenuse}^2=1^2+1^2=2hypotenuse2=12+12=2

  2. Take the positive square root:

    hypotenuse=2\text{hypotenuse}=\sqrt2hypotenuse=2​

  • Turning a triangle on the page does not change its angles or side lengths.
  • Find the marked angle and the right-angle square each time, rather than relying on which way the triangle faces.

Read the cosine column as the sine column in reverse.

Keep the size. Choose the sign.

  • For angles beyond 90°, imagine a line from the centre of a circle to its edge.
  • This line is a radius.
  • Start with it pointing right.
  • Turn it anticlockwise, opposite to the direction of clock hands, to measure a positive angle.
  • Clockwise turns give negative angles.
  • Draw a horizontal line and a vertical line through the centre.
  • These reference lines are called axes.
  • They divide the circle into four regions, called quadrants.
  • I, II, III and IV mean 1, 2, 3 and 4;
  • count anticlockwise from the upper-right region.
  • Choose a circle of radius 1, called a unit circle.
  • The moving end of the radius marks a point on it.
  • A point’s coordinates tell you how far across and how far up or down it is from the centre.
  • Cosine gives the across coordinate;
  • sine gives the up/down coordinate.
  • Right and up count as positive, while left and down count as negative.
  • In the upper-left quadrant, for example, the point is left of the centre but above it: cosine is negative and sine is positive.
  • Tangent is sine divided by cosine, provided cosine is not zero.
  • The reference angle, also called the related acute angle, is the small angle between the turned radius and the horizontal line to its left or right.
  • Imagine drawing a vertical line from the circle point to that horizontal line: this makes a small right triangle.
  • Its acute angle supplies the size of the ratio.
  • The point’s position supplies the positive or negative sign.
  • If the point lies on an axis, use the axis value directly.

  • The notation sin⁡2θ\sin^2\thetasin2θ means (sin⁡θ)2(\sin\theta)^2(sinθ)2: square the sine value, not the angle.

A ratio in. One angle out.

  • Sine takes an angle and returns a ratio.
  • Inverse sine, written sin⁡−1\sin^{-1}sin−1, takes a ratio and returns one chosen angle.
  • The raised −1 here means inverse function;
  • it does not mean reciprocal.
  • 1/sin⁡θ1/\sin\theta1/sinθ and sin⁡−1(θ)\sin^{-1}(\theta)sin−1(θ) are different operations.

Definition · Principal value

The inverse-trig functions return ONE angle, the principal value, not every possible angle.

  • Different angles can have the same sine.
  • For example, sin 30° and sin 150° both equal 1/2.
  • To give one answer, inverse sine always chooses an angle from a fixed interval, meaning all the numbers between two limits.
  • A domain lists allowed inputs;
  • a range lists allowed outputs.
  • In the table, x is the input ratio and y is the output angle in radians.
  • The sign ≤ means “less than or equal to”, so the limiting value is allowed.
  • The sign < means “less than”, so that value is excluded.
  • For example, −1 ≤ x ≤ 1 allows any number from −1 to 1, including both ends.

Two expressions for the same length

  • Perpendicular lines meet at 90°, like the two edges at the corner of a rectangular sheet.
  • The symbol ⟂ means “is perpendicular to”.
  • In the triangle below, start at B and draw a line to side AC so that it meets AC at 90°.
  • Name the meeting point D.
  • This is what “drop a perpendicular from B to AC” means;
  • the line need not point down the page.
  • D is called the foot of the perpendicular: it is the point where the new line meets AC.
  • “D lies on AC” means D is partway along that side, so AC consists of AD and DC.
  • The new line BD divides the original triangle into ABD and DBC.
  • Trace each outline separately.
  • Both new triangles have a right angle at D, and both contain the very same side BD.

    • An expression is a number or a calculation written with numbers and symbols.
    • We now have two expressions for the length of the same segment BD.
    • They must have the same value, so put them on opposite sides of =:
    20sin⁡θ=9tan⁡θ20\sin\theta=\frac9{\tan\theta}20sinθ=tanθ9​
    • Replace tangent by sine divided by cosine.
    • Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal: 9 ÷ (sin θ / cos θ) = 9 × (cos θ / sin θ).

    20sin⁡θ=9cos⁡θsin⁡θ20\sin\theta=\frac{9\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}20sinθ=sinθ9cosθ​

Worked example

Two inverse functions in one equation

  • Find x exactly if π/6 + tan⁻¹(4x) = −cos⁻¹(√3/2).
  • The input x is a number;
  • all angles here are radians.

Solution

  1. Inverse cosine returns π/6 for √3/2, so the right-hand side is −π/6.

  2. Subtract π/6 from both sides:

    tan⁡−1(4x)=−π6−π6=−π3\tan^{-1}(4x)=-\frac\pi6-\frac\pi6=-\frac\pi3tan−1(4x)=−6π​−6π​=−3π​

  3. The angle −π/3 lies strictly between −π/2 and π/2. Apply tangent:

    4x=tan⁡(−π/3)=−tan⁡(π/3)=−34x=\tan(-\pi/3)=-\tan(\pi/3)=-\sqrt34x=tan(−π/3)=−tan(π/3)=−3​

    Tangent changes sign under a clockwise reflection, just as sine does.

  4. Divide both sides by 4:

    x=−34x=-\frac{\sqrt3}{4}x=−43​​

  5. Check: π/6 + (−π/3) = −π/6, the required right side.
