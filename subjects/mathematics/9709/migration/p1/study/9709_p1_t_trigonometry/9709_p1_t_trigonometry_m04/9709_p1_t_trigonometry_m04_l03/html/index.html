DISGUISED TRIGONOMETRIC EQUATIONS

Spot the shape underneath.

A complicated-looking equation often hides a familiar quadratic, linear equation or product. Reveal that shape first, then use the standard solving method.

Start · Return to key ideas

Six disguises

  • A factor is one piece of a product. If a product is zero, either factor may be zero. Dividing by a factor too early can discard its solutions.
  • A substitution gives a repeated expression a shorter name. For example, u = cos²x turns cos⁴x into u². A fourth-power equation with only even powers is called biquadratic.
  • When u = √(cos x), the square-root symbol makes u nonnegative. After solving for u, reject negative roots before returning to cosine.
  • Keep original denominators nonzero. Sine and cosine values lie from −1 to 1, and their squares lie from 0 to 1. Check these restrictions before finding angles.
  • Grouping: pair four terms so both pairs share a factor, then factor the common bracket.
  • Clear fractions: multiply every term by the denominator, then simplify.

Key ideas

  • Identify the disguise before calculating.
  • Keep every branch after factoring, squaring or taking roots, then test it in the original equation.
  • Reject invalid sine/cosine values, but never reject tangent merely because its magnitude exceeds 1.
  • In P1, factor directly rather than using a double-angle formula.
  • Use the stated interval at every stage, especially after a substitution changes the range.
