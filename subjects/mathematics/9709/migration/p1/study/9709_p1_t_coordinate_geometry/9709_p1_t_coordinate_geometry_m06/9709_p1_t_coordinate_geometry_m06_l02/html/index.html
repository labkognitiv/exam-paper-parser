Two circle equations contain matching squared terms. Subtract them to reveal a line, then use the centres to translate or reflect whole circles.

The radical line

Write both circle equations so the coefficients of x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2 are each 1. If an entire equation was multiplied, divide every term first. Then they have the form

x2+y2+ax+by+c=0x^2+y^2+ax+by+c=0x2+y2+ax+by+c=0

Subtracting cancels x2x^2x2 and y2y^2y2, leaving a linear equation. This infinite radical line contains every common point of the circles.

  • If two circles intersect twice, their finite common chord lies on the radical line. The whole infinite line is not the finite chord.
  • If they touch, the radical line passes through the single contact point.
  • If their centres are different and they do not meet, the radical line still exists, but it contains no common real point.
  1. Expand both circles and normalize their squared-term coefficients.
  2. Subtract. Simplify the remaining linear equation.
  3. To find common points, substitute that line into either circle.
  4. Solve the quadratic, then use the line to find each matching coordinate.

These steps assume distinct centres. Concentric circles have the same centre. For concentric circles, subtraction removes both variables: a contradiction means different radii and no intersection; 0=00=00=0 means the equations describe the same circle, with infinitely many shared points.

Same x-coordinate, horizontal line

If distinct centres share an x-coordinate, subtraction can leave only yyy. The radical line is then horizontal. This does not mean the circles share a centre.

Key ideas

  • Normalize both equations, expand, then subtract.
  • The radical line is an infinite line. A finite common chord lies on it when there are two intersections.
  • Substitute the line into either circle to find the common points.
  • Distinct concentric circles give a contradiction; identical circle equations give 0=00=00=0.

Translate a circle

A translation moves every point by the same vector (h,k)(h,k)(h,k): add hhh to each x-coordinate and kkk to each y-coordinate. The radius does not change.

(a,b)⟼(a+h,b+k)(a,b)\longmapsto(a+h,b+k)(a,b)⟼(a+h,b+k)

(x−a)2+(y−b)2=r2⟼[x−(a+h)]2+[y−(b+k)]2=r2(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2\longmapsto[x-(a+h)]^2+[y-(b+k)]^2=r^2(x−a)2+(y−b)2=r2⟼[x−(a+h)]2+[y−(b+k)]2=r2

Algebra shortcut: in the original equation, replace every xxx by x−hx-hx−h and every yyy by y−ky-ky−k. This gives the same translated circle.

Worked example: translate by (8,4)(8,4)(8,4)

C1:(x−2)2+(y+1)2=41C_1:(x-2)^2+(y+1)^2=41C1​:(x−2)2+(y+1)2=41.

Solution

  1. Centre (2,−1)(2,-1)(2,−1), with r2=41r^2=41r2=41.
  2. New centre (2+8,−1+4)=(10,3)(2+8,-1+4)=(10,3)(2+8,−1+4)=(10,3).
  3. The radius is unchanged.
  4. Answer: (x−10)2+(y−3)2=41(x-10)^2+(y-3)^2=41(x−10)2+(y−3)2=41.

Reflect a circle in its tangent

A reflection mirrors the circle across the tangent at contact point BBB. The contact stays fixed. The original centre CCC, contact BBB, and reflected centre C′C'C′ are collinear, meaning they lie on one straight line. BBB is the midpoint of CC′CC'CC′.

C′=2B−CC'=2B-CC′=2B−C

The two displacements are equal: B−C=C′−BB-C=C'-BB−C=C′−B. Therefore C′=C+2(B−C)=2B−CC'=C+2(B-C)=2B-CC′=C+2(B−C)=2B−C.

The equal-radius circles touch at BBB. Their common tangent there is perpendicular to the centre line CC′CC'CC′.

Worked example: reflect in the tangent

(x−3)2+(y−1)2=25(x-3)^2+(y-1)^2=25(x−3)2+(y−1)2=25 has tangent 4x+3y=404x+3y=404x+3y=40 at B(7,4)B(7,4)B(7,4).

Solution

  1. Original centre C=(3,1)C=(3,1)C=(3,1); r2=25r^2=25r2=25.
  2. BBB is fixed and is the midpoint of CC′CC'CC′.
  3. C′=2B−C=(2(7)−3,2(4)−1)=(11,7)C'=2B-C=(2(7)-3,2(4)-1)=(11,7)C′=2B−C=(2(7)−3,2(4)−1)=(11,7).
  4. Check: B−C=(4,3)=C′−BB-C=(4,3)=C'-BB−C=(4,3)=C′−B.
  5. Answer: (x−11)2+(y−7)2=25(x-11)^2+(y-7)^2=25(x−11)2+(y−7)2=25.
