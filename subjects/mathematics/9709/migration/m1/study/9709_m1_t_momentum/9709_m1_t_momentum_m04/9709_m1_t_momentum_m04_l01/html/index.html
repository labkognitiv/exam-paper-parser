Two-case collisions

When a final direction is unknown, test both signed velocities.

Momentum lessonsKey ideasMethodWorked exampleRecapMore worked examples

A speed tells you how fast. It does not tell you which way.

Why two cases appear

  • An impact is the collision between the particles.
  • A speed is a size, so it is never negative.
  • A stationary object is at rest: its speed and velocity are 0.
  • A velocity includes direction, so it may be positive, negative or zero.
  • A case is one possible directional setup.
  • Choose one direction as positive before writing momentum.
  • If a particle keeps moving in that direction, its velocity is positive.
  • If it rebounds, it reverses direction and its velocity is negative.
  • In the diagram, v is the non-negative final speed. Its signed velocity is +v or −v.

Momentum reminder

  • p means momentum.
  • m means mass in kilograms.
  • u means velocity before impact; v means velocity after impact.
  • The system is the particles considered together.
  • An external impulse is a momentum change caused by something outside that system.
p=mv

With no external impulse on the chosen system, total momentum before impact equals total momentum after impact.

The two-case method

  1. Choose one positive direction.
  2. Calculate total momentum before impact using signed velocities.
  3. Case A: give the unknown final velocity a positive sign.
  4. Case B: give it a negative sign to represent a rebound.
  5. Solve both momentum equations.
  6. Check the assumed speed. A negative value for that speed contradicts the case; zero is allowed.
  7. Report each remaining non-negative speed with its direction.

Why reject a negative assumed speed?

  • Writing +v or −v already chooses the direction.
  • The letter v then represents a speed, so it must satisfy v≥0.
  • If solving gives v<0, the result points opposite to the direction assumed for that case.

Worked example

A particle P has mass 0.20 kg and moves right at 5 m s−1. It collides with a stationary particle Q of mass 0.30 kg. After impact, P and Q have equal speeds. Find the two possible speeds.

  • Take P's original direction as positive.
  • Q is stationary before impact, so its initial velocity is 0.
  • Let the common final speed be v.

Solution

  1. Initial momentum: 0.20(5)+0.30(0)=1.0 N s.
  2. Case A, both move right: 1.0=0.20v+0.30v.
  3. 1.0=0.50v, so v=2.0 m s^-1.
  4. Case B, P rebounds left: 1.0=−0.20v+0.30v.
  5. 1.0=0.10v, so v=10 m s^-1.

Momentum equations give possible speeds 2.0 m s−1 and 10 m s−1.

Check physical feasibility

  • Momentum conservation alone gives both algebraic branches.
  • Kinetic energy is energy due to motion. It is measured in joules, written J.
  • Use E_k=1/2 mv²; the square means the direction sign does not affect kinetic energy.
  • Before impact: E_ki=1/2(0.20)(5²)=2.5 J.
  • For the 10 m s−1 branch: E_kf=1/2(0.20)(10²)+1/2(0.30)(10²)=25 J.
  • That branch requires 22.5 J of energy released from another store. Momentum alone cannot establish it as an ordinary passive collision.

Key ideas

  • Speed is non-negative; velocity carries a sign for direction.
  • When a final direction is unknown, test +v and −v.
  • Use the same positive direction in every momentum term.
  • A negative assumed speed rejects that direction. Also check any other physical conditions in the question.
  • If two final speeds are unknown, momentum alone is one equation; another independent relationship is needed.
  • Momentum conservation does not by itself prove energetic feasibility.

More worked examples

Worked example

A 3 kg particle P moves right at 4 m s−1 and collides with a stationary 5 kg particle Q. After impact, P has speed 1 m s−1. Find the two possible speeds of Q.

  1. Take P's original direction as positive. Initial momentum is 3(4)=12 N s.
  2. Case A, P continues right: 12=3(1)+5v.
  3. v=1.8 m s^-1.
  4. Case B, P rebounds left: 12=3(−1)+5v.
  5. v=3.0 m s^-1.

Answer: Q may move right at 1.8 m s−1 or 3.0 m s−1.

Worked example

After impact, Q's speed is twice P's speed. P has mass 0.4 kg, Q has mass 0.5 kg, and the given total momentum before impact is 0.25 N s. Find both possible speed pairs.

  • The initial velocities are not supplied. Use the given total momentum 0.25 N s directly.
  • Let P's final speed be v. Then Q's final speed is 2v.
  • Q moves in the positive direction in both cases.
  1. Case A, P also moves positive: 0.25=0.4v+0.5(2v)=1.4v.
  2. v=5/28 m s^-1, so Q's speed is 2v=5/14 m s^-1.
  3. Case B, P rebounds: 0.25=−0.4v+0.5(2v)=0.6v.
  4. v=5/12 m s^-1, so Q's speed is 2v=5/6 m s^-1.

Answer: (P, Q) = (5/28, 5/14) m s−1, or (5/12, 5/6) m s−1 with P reversed.

Worked example

Suppose P and Q have masses m and M have initial velocities u and 0. After impact, P has speed v and Q has speed w to the right.

  1. If P continues right: mu=mv+Mw.
  2. If P rebounds left: mu=−mv+Mw.
  3. Solve the missing branch as well as the first.
  4. Keep a branch only when its assumed speed satisfies v≥0.
  5. If both v and w are unknown, these are separate cases, each with two unknowns. A speed ratio or another independent relationship is needed.

A negative solved value of v rejects that assumed direction; zero remains a valid speed.

Worked example

A 0.10 kg particle P moves right at 6 m s−1 and collides with a stationary 0.20 kg particle Q. After impact, P has speed 2 m s−1. Find the two possible speeds of Q.

  1. Take P's original direction as positive. Initial momentum: 0.10(6)=0.60 N s.
  2. Case A, P continues right: 0.60=0.10(2)+0.20v.
  3. 0.40=0.20v, so v=2.0 m s^-1.
  4. Case B, P rebounds left: 0.60=0.10(−2)+0.20v.
  5. 0.80=0.20v, so v=4.0 m s^-1.

Answer: Q may move right at 2.0 m s−1 or 4.0 m s−1.
