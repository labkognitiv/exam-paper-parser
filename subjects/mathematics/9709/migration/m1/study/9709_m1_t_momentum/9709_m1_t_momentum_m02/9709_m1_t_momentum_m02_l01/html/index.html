Coalescence and common velocity

When two bodies stick together in a collision, how fast do they move afterwards?

Start the lesson
MeaningMethodExamplesKey ideasMore examples

What coalescence means

  • Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity.
  • Mass is measured in kilograms, kg.
  • Velocity is speed with a direction, measured in metres per second, m s−1.
  • An impulse measures the effect of a force over time. For a constant force, multiply force by the time it acts. The impulse equals the change in momentum.
  • The conservation equation below applies during the short impact when the impulse from forces outside the two-body system is negligible.
  • Coalesce means the bodies stick together after impact.
  • “Move together”, “stay in contact” and “combined particle” describe this shared motion. An embedded object is lodged in another object.
  • Before impact, each body has its own mass and velocity.
  • m₁ and m₂ are the two masses.
  • u₁ and u₂ are their velocities before impact. The small numbers are subscripts; they match each velocity to body 1 or body 2.
  • v is their one common velocity after impact.
  • After impact, the combined mass is m₁ + m₂.

Conservation of linear momentum

Total momentum before = total momentum after

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v

The five-step method

  1. Draw or describe the situation before and after impact.
  2. Choose one direction as positive.
  3. Write every initial momentum term with its sign.
  4. After impact, add the masses because the bodies move together.
  5. Solve for the unknown and include units and direction.

The minus sign comes from direction, never from mass.

Stationary means at rest, so its velocity and momentum are zero.

Worked examples

Worked example

Find a common speed

A 3 kg particle moving right at 5 m s−1 collides with a stationary 2 kg particle. They coalesce.

Solution

  1. Take right as positive.
  2. Momentum before: 3(5) + 2(0) = 15.
  3. Combined mass: 3 + 2 = 5 kg.
  4. Conserve momentum: 15 = 5v.
  5. Divide both sides by 5: v = 3.

Answer: 3 m s−1 to the right.

Worked example

Work backwards for a mass

A 0.25 kg particle moving at 4 m s−1 hits a stationary particle of mass m kg. They coalesce at 1.6 m s−1.

Solution

  1. Take the original direction as positive.
  2. Write momentum before and after: 0.25(4) + 0 = (0.25 + m)(1.6).
  3. Expand: 1.00 = 0.40 + 1.6m.
  4. Subtract 0.40: 0.60 = 1.6m.
  5. Divide by 1.6: m = 0.375.

Answer: m = 0.375 kg.

Worked example

Opposite directions

A has mass 2m and velocity +6 m s−1. B has mass m and velocity −2 m s−1. They coalesce.

Solution

  1. Choose right as positive, so B's velocity is −2.
  2. Write signed momentum: 2m(6) + m(−2) = (2m + m)v.
  3. Simplify: 12m − 2m = 3mv.
  4. Cancel the common non-zero mass m: 10 = 3v.

Answer: v = 10/3 m s−1 to the right.

Check 1

Two bodies move towards each other. Right is positive. Which velocity must carry a minus sign?

Key ideas

  • Coalescing bodies stick together and share one velocity.
  • Choose a positive direction before writing momentum terms.
  • Opposite motion gives a negative velocity.
  • Add the masses after impact.
  • Use momentum conservation during the short impact when the external impulse is negligible.

More worked examples

Worked example

Hammer and nail

A 1.2 kg hammer head strikes a stationary 0.006 kg nail at speed u. They move together at 25 m s−1.

Solution

  1. Take the direction of motion as positive.
  2. 1.2u = (1.2 + 0.006)(25).
  3. 1.2u = 30.15.
  4. u = 25.125, which is 25.1 to 3 significant figures.

Answer: u = 25.1 m s−1.

Worked example

Exam-style signed momentum

P has mass 0.4 kg and speed 3.5 m s−1. Q has mass 0.3 kg and moves oppositely at 1.0 m s−1. They coalesce.

Solution

  1. Take P's direction as positive.
  2. 0.4(3.5) + 0.3(−1.0) = (0.4 + 0.3)v.
  3. 1.4 − 0.3 = 0.7v.
  4. 1.1 = 0.7v, so v = 11/7.

Answer: 11/7 m s−1 in P's original direction.
