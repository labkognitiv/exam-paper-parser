Two limits make a range

  • A force is measured in newtons, N. Mass mmm is measured in kilograms, kg.
  • Weight is W=mgW=mgW=mg downward. ggg is acceleration due to gravity, measured in metres per second squared, m s−2\mathrm{{m\,s^{{-2}}}}ms−2.
  • A rough plane can exert friction along its surface. Friction opposes actual or impending sliding.
  • The coefficient of friction μ\muμ measures the maximum friction per unit normal reaction and has no units. The normal reaction RRR is the surface's push on the particle; it acts perpendicular, at 90°, away from the plane.
  • Equilibrium means the resultant force is zero.
  • Parallel lines point in the same direction and never meet. For these axes, the parallel direction is along the plane. Perpendicular means at 90°.
  • A component is the part of a force along one chosen direction. An axis is that chosen direction.
  • Choose axes along the slope and perpendicular to it.
  • α\alphaα names the angle between the slope and the horizontal.
  • The weight and its two components form a right-angled triangle. The hypotenuse is opposite the 90° angle. The adjacent side is the shorter side touching the marked angle. The opposite side is the shorter side across from it.
  • Sine is opposite ÷ hypotenuse. Cosine is adjacent ÷ hypotenuse.
  • The horizontal line rotates 90° into the vertical weight line. The slope line rotates 90° into the inward normal line. Equal rotations preserve α\alphaα, so the weight triangle contains the incline angle.
  • Therefore weight has components mgsin⁡αmg\sin\alphamgsinα down the slope and mgcos⁡αmg\cos\alphamgcosα into it.
  • Limiting equilibrium means the particle is just about to move. Then friction is at its maximum: F=μRF=\mu RF=μR.
  • An endpoint is an included boundary value. The symbol ≤\le≤ includes equality.

At the least applied force, impending motion is down the plane, so friction acts up. At the greatest applied force, impending motion is up, so friction acts down.

Pmin⁡≤P≤Pmax⁡P_{{\min}}\le P\le P_{{\max}}Pmin​≤P≤Pmax​

Every value between the endpoints permits equilibrium. If PPP is a force magnitude, also require P≥0P\ge0P≥0.

  1. Draw the real forces.
  2. Find RRR from perpendicular balance.
  3. Set F=μRF=\mu RF=μR at each limiting endpoint.
  4. Reverse friction for the other endpoint.
  5. Solve both cases and state the included range.

When the applied force is parallel to the plane, RRR is the same at both endpoints. An angled force changes RRR as its magnitude changes.

Do not assume a limit

  1. Ignore friction first and find the direction of the non-friction resultant along the plane. This gives the tendency to move.
  2. Friction must oppose that tendency.
  3. Find the friction magnitude required for equilibrium, FreqF_{\mathrm{req}}Freq​.
  4. Find the normal reaction and maximum available friction, Fmax⁡=μRF_{\max}=\mu RFmax​=μR.
  5. If Freq≤Fmax⁡F_{\mathrm{req}}\le F_{\max}Freq​≤Fmax​, equilibrium is possible.
  6. If Freq>Fmax⁡F_{\mathrm{req}}>F_{\max}Freq​>Fmax​, friction is insufficient and the particle moves in the direction of the non-friction resultant.
  7. Equality means it is at the limiting endpoint.

Static friction adjusts below its maximum, so a resting particle need not be at limiting equilibrium.

Key ideas

  • Decide the impending direction before assigning friction.
  • Use a separate normal reaction for each angled-force endpoint.
  • Compare required friction with μR\mu RμR before deciding whether equilibrium is possible.
  • Include limiting endpoints with ≤\le≤.
  • Keep bounds exact, then state rounded endpoint values as approximations.
  • Apply P≥0P\ge0P≥0 and contact conditions when required.
