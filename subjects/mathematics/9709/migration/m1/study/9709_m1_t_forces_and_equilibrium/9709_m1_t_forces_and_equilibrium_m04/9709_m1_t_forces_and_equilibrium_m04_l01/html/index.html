What the symbols mean

  • A force is a push or pull, measured in newtons, written N.
  • Mass measures how much matter an object contains, measured in kilograms, written kg.
  • Weight is the downward force of gravity: W=mgW=mgW=mg.
  • ggg is gravitational acceleration, measured in metres per second squared, written m s−2\mathrm{{m\,s^{{-2}}}}ms−2.
  • The normal reaction RRR acts at 90° to the contact surface. A right angle is 90°.
  • Relative motion means one surface sliding against the other at their contact.
  • Impending motion is motion that is about to occur.
  • Friction FFF acts along the contact surface and opposes actual or impending relative motion.
  • The coefficient of friction μ\muμ is a dimensionless number, meaning it has no units, that compares friction with the normal reaction.

Static friction

F≤μRF\leq \mu RF≤μR

For a block at rest, friction adjusts up to its maximum value. At limiting equilibrium, the block is just about to move, so F=μRF=\mu RF=μR.

A moving block

Constant speed along a straight line means zero acceleration, so the forces balance. It does not by itself mean limiting static friction.

For the moving examples below, we explicitly adopt the usual sliding-friction model F=μRF=\mu RF=μR.

If there is no other vertical force and the surface is horizontal, vertical balance gives R=mgR=mgR=mg. An angled pull changes RRR.

A reliable method

  • A sloping force and its horizontal and vertical components form a right-angled triangle.
  • The hypotenuse represents the force and lies opposite the 90° angle.
  • The shorter side touching the marked angle is adjacent and uses cosine.
  • The shorter side across from the angle is opposite and uses sine.
  1. Draw weight mgmgmg downward and reaction RRR upward.
  2. Put friction along the surface, opposite motion or impending motion.
  3. Resolve an angled force. From a horizontal angle θ\thetaθ, the adjacent horizontal component is Pcos⁡θP\cos\thetaPcosθ and the opposite vertical component is Psin⁡θP\sin\thetaPsinθ.
  4. Use vertical equilibrium first to find RRR.
  5. Use horizontal equilibrium to find FFF.
  6. Apply F=μRF=\mu RF=μR only for limiting equilibrium or the stated sliding-friction model.
  7. Rearrange by dividing both sides by RRR: μ=F/R\mu=F/Rμ=F/R.

Worked example 1

A 500 kg crate

A 500 kg crate is pulled horizontally by 2500 N at constant speed. Take g=10 m s−2g=10\ \mathrm{{m\,s^{{-2}}}}g=10 ms−2. Use the usual sliding-friction model.

  1. Its weight is W=mg=500×10=5000 NW=mg=500\times10=5000\ \mathrm{{N}}W=mg=500×10=5000 N.
  2. There is no other vertical force, so R=5000 NR=5000\ \mathrm{{N}}R=5000 N.
  3. Constant speed gives horizontal balance: F=2500 NF=2500\ \mathrm{{N}}F=2500 N.
  4. Apply the stated sliding model: F=μRF=\mu RF=μR.
  5. Divide by RRR: μ=F/R=2500/5000=0.50\mu=F/R=2500/5000=0.50μ=F/R=2500/5000=0.50.

Answer: μ=0.50\mu=0.50μ=0.50

Worked example 2

An angled pull changes the reaction

A 4 kg block is pulled by 30 N at 24° above horizontal and moves at constant speed. Take g=10g=10g=10 and use the usual sliding-friction model.

  1. Weight: W=4×10=40 NW=4\times10=40\ \mathrm{{N}}W=4×10=40 N.
  2. The upward component of the pull is 30sin⁡24∘30\sin24^\circ30sin24∘.
  3. Vertical balance: R+30sin⁡24∘=40R+30\sin24^\circ=40R+30sin24∘=40.
  4. Subtract the upward component: R=40−30sin⁡24∘=27.7979… NR=40-30\sin24^\circ=27.7979\ldots\ \mathrm{{N}}R=40−30sin24∘=27.7979… N.
  5. The horizontal component of the pull is 30cos⁡24∘30\cos24^\circ30cos24∘.
  6. Horizontal balance: F=30cos⁡24∘=27.4063637… NF=30\cos24^\circ=27.4063637\ldots\ \mathrm{{N}}F=30cos24∘=27.4063637… N.
  7. μ=F/R=30cos⁡24∘40−30sin⁡24∘=0.9859149…\mu=F/R=\frac{{30\cos24^\circ}}{{40-30\sin24^\circ}}=0.9859149\ldotsμ=F/R=40−30sin24∘30cos24∘​=0.9859149….

Key ideas

  • Friction acts along the surface and opposes actual or impending motion.
  • The normal reaction acts at 90° to the surface.
  • An upward component of a pull reduces RRR.
  • Use F=μRF=\mu RF=μR at limiting equilibrium or when a sliding-friction model is stated.
  • Keep full calculator values until the final answer.

Worked example 3

Limiting equilibrium

A 0.2 kg block rests on a rough horizontal surface. A horizontal force of 1.2 N keeps it in limiting equilibrium. Take g=10g=10g=10.

  1. Weight: W=0.2×10=2 NW=0.2\times10=2\ \mathrm{{N}}W=0.2×10=2 N.
  2. Vertical balance: R=2 NR=2\ \mathrm{{N}}R=2 N.
  3. Horizontal balance: F=1.2 NF=1.2\ \mathrm{{N}}F=1.2 N.
  4. Because equilibrium is limiting, F=μRF=\mu RF=μR.
  5. μ=F/R=1.2/2=0.60\mu=F/R=1.2/2=0.60μ=F/R=1.2/2=0.60.

Answer: μ=0.60\mu=0.60μ=0.60
