Mathematics 9709 · Mechanics 1

Single body motion with constant acceleration

How far does a cyclist travel before stopping? Start with the direction and the quantities you know.

Describe the motion before calculating

  • Imagine a car moving along a straight road. We want to connect how fast it starts, how its velocity changes, and how far it travels.
  • A particle is a model that treats an object as a point. Its size does not affect these motion calculations.
  • Choose one direction as positive. Here, right is positive. A minus sign tells us a quantity points left.
  • Displacement is the signed change in position. Moving 5 metres right gives displacement +5 m; moving back 5 metres gives −5 m. Distance is the total path length, so those two movements cover 10 m but give zero overall displacement.
  • Speed describes how fast position changes. Velocity includes direction: +8 m/s means 8 metres per second to the right; −8 m/s means the same speed to the left. Speed is non-negative, including zero at rest.
  • Acceleration is the change in velocity per second. An acceleration of +3 m/s² means the velocity increases by 3 m/s each second. Read m/s² as metres per second squared; it is also written m s⁻².
  • The unit m means metre and the unit s means second. In an equation, the quantity letter s instead names displacement; the context distinguishes it from the time unit.
  • Constant or uniform acceleration means the acceleration stays the same throughout the chosen interval. The equations below require that condition.
  • If an object moves right at 8 m/s and slows at 2 m/s², its initial velocity is +8 m/s and its acceleration is −2 m/s². Negative acceleration means leftwards; whether it slows an object depends on the direction of motion.

Name the five quantities

SymbolMeaning in one intervalUnit
uInitial velocity, at the startm/s
vFinal velocity, at the endm/s
aConstant accelerationm/s²
sDisplacement during the intervalm
tTime elapsed during the intervals
  • “From rest” means u = 0. “Comes to rest” means v = 0.
  • Letters next to each other mean multiplication: at means a × t. Brackets also group multiplication: 3(2) means 3 × 2.
  • A small raised 2 means square: t² = t × t. The symbol ½ means divide by two. Calculate squares and brackets before completing products and sums.

The four equations

Use these only for a straight-line interval with constant acceleration.

EquationLetter omitted
v = u + ats
s = ut + ½at²v
v² = u² + 2ast
s = ½(u + v)ta
  • The first equation adds the velocity change at to the starting velocity u.
  • In the last equation, ½(u + v) is average velocity for constant acceleration. Multiplying average velocity by elapsed time gives displacement.
  • The displacement equation uses starting velocity, acceleration and time. The squared-velocity equation connects displacement and velocities without needing time.

Choose an equation

  1. Sketch the line of motion.
  2. Choose a positive direction.
  3. List u, v, a, s and t, including units and signs.
  4. Identify the quantity requested.
  5. Choose the equation that contains that quantity and the known values, leaving out the unused quantity.

For u = 4 m/s, a = 2 m/s² and s = 42 m, with v required, time is unused. Choose v² = u² + 2as.

Worked examples

Speed after an interval

A particle starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 3 m/s² for 2 s. Find its speed.

Solution

  1. Choose the motion direction as positive. From rest means u = 0; a = 3 m/s² and t = 2 s.
  2. We need v and do not need s, so choose v = u + at.
  3. Substitute the given numbers: v = 0 + 3 × 2.
  4. Multiply 3 × 2 = 6, then add zero: v = 6 m/s.
  5. Velocity is positive in our chosen direction, so its speed is 6 m/s.

Speed = 6 m/s.

Distance from acceleration and time

A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 1.4 m/s² for 6 s. Find the distance travelled.

Solution

  1. Choose forwards as positive: u = 0, a = 1.4 m/s², t = 6 s. The cyclist does not reverse, so distance equals positive displacement.
  2. Final velocity is unused. Use s = ut + ½at².
  3. Substitute: s = 0 × 6 + ½ × 1.4 × 6².
  4. Square the time first: 6² = 36. Then ½ × 1.4 = 0.7.
  5. Calculate s = 0 + 0.7 × 36 = 25.2 m.

Distance = 25.2 m.

Stopping without knowing the time

A cyclist moves at 8 m/s and decelerates uniformly at 0.25 m/s² until stopping. Find the stopping distance.

Solution

  1. Choose forwards as positive. Then u = 8 m/s, v = 0 and a = −0.25 m/s².
  2. Time is unused. Use v² = u² + 2as.
  3. Substitute: 0² = 8² + 2 × (−0.25) × s. This becomes 0 = 64 − 0.5s.
  4. To isolate s, add 0.5s to both sides: 0.5s = 64. Applying the same operation to both sides keeps an equation balanced.
  5. Divide both sides by 0.5: s = 64 ÷ 0.5 = 128 m.
  6. The distance is positive, as expected for forwards motion before the stop.

Stopping distance = 128 m.

Use average velocity

A particle moves with constant acceleration from 4 m/s to 10 m/s in 6 s, without reversing. Find its displacement.

Solution

  1. Here u = 4 m/s, v = 10 m/s and t = 6 s. Acceleration is unused.
  2. For constant acceleration, average velocity is ½(u + v) = (4 + 10) ÷ 2 = 7 m/s.
  3. Multiply by time: s = 7 × 6 = 42 m.

Displacement = 42 m in the positive direction.

Read a velocity-time graph

  • The horizontal axis shows time t in seconds. The vertical axis shows velocity v in m/s.
  • A point written (3, 0.24) means that at 3 s the velocity is 0.24 m/s. The time comes first.
  • Gradient means rise divided by horizontal run. On this graph it is change in velocity divided by elapsed time, so it gives acceleration.
  • The straight line means its gradient, and therefore acceleration, stays constant.

Solution

  1. Change in velocity = 0.24 − 0 = 0.24 m/s.
  2. Elapsed time = 3 − 0 = 3 s.
  3. Acceleration = 0.24 ÷ 3 = 0.08 m/s².
  • The area between a velocity-time graph and the time axis gives displacement, with area below the axis counted negatively.
  • For this graph, the region is a triangle. Its area is ½ × base × height = ½ × 3 × 0.24 = 0.36 m.
  • Velocity never becomes negative here, so distance is also 0.36 m.

Check the signs and the interval

  • A car moving forwards at 12 m/s brakes at 3 m/s². With forwards positive, a = −3, not +3.
  • For the stop, 0 = 12² + 2(−3)s = 144 − 6s. Add 6s to both sides, then divide by 6: s = 24 m.
  • Using +3 would give s = −24 m, inconsistent with travelling forwards to stop. Check the model as well as the arithmetic.
  • Split the motion whenever acceleration changes. Each stage has its own u, v, a, s and elapsed t. If velocity is continuous at the boundary, the next stage starts with the previous final velocity.
  • “Distance in the fourth second” refers to the interval from t = 3 s to t = 4 s. If there is no reversal, subtract displacement at 3 s from displacement at 4 s. If the object reverses, split at the turning point before adding path lengths.

You know initial velocity, acceleration and displacement, and need final velocity. Which quantity can your equation leave out?

Key ideas

  • Choose a positive direction; distinguish displacement from distance and velocity from speed.
  • For one constant-acceleration interval, list u, v, a, s and t with units.
  • Choose the equation that omits the unused quantity. Substitute, calculate and keep the equation balanced when rearranging.
  • The four equations are v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as; s = ½(u + v)t.
  • Velocity-time gradient gives acceleration; signed area gives displacement.
  • Use a new stage when acceleration changes. Finish by checking units, sign and physical meaning.

More worked examples

Already moving at the start

A block moves at 0.4 m/s and accelerates uniformly at 0.6 m/s² for 5 s. Find the distance and final speed.

Solution

  1. Take forwards as positive: u = 0.4 m/s, a = 0.6 m/s², t = 5 s.
  2. Use s = ut + ½at²: s = 0.4 × 5 + ½ × 0.6 × 5².
  3. Compute the two terms: 0.4 × 5 = 2 and ½ × 0.6 × 25 = 7.5. Thus s = 2 + 7.5 = 9.5 m.
  4. For final velocity, use v = u + at = 0.4 + 0.6 × 5 = 3.4 m/s.
  5. There is no reversal, so distance equals displacement and speed equals positive velocity.

Distance = 9.5 m; final speed = 3.4 m/s.

Speed after braking through a distance

A car moves at 18 m/s and brakes uniformly at 5 m/s² for 20 m. Find its speed after 20 m.

Solution

  1. Choose forwards positive: u = 18 m/s, a = −5 m/s², s = 20 m.
  2. Time is not needed. Substitute into v² = u² + 2as: v² = 18² + 2(−5)(20).
  3. Calculate v² = 324 − 200 = 124.
  4. A square root reverses squaring. The positive square root √124 is the speed because speed is non-negative.
  5. √124 = 11.1355… m/s, which rounds to 11.1 m/s to three significant figures.
  6. The car has not stopped: its stopping distance would be 324 ÷ 10 = 32.4 m, greater than 20 m.

Speed = √124 m/s ≈ 11.1 m/s.

Acceleration from two plotted points

A velocity-time graph is a straight line from (0, 0) to (5, 0.30), with time in seconds and velocity in m/s. Find acceleration.

Solution

  1. A straight line has constant gradient, so the acceleration is constant.
  2. Velocity change = 0.30 − 0 = 0.30 m/s; time change = 5 − 0 = 5 s.
  3. Divide velocity change by time change: a = 0.30 ÷ 5 = 0.06 m/s².

Acceleration = 0.06 m/s².

Starting from rest

A particle starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 1.5 m/s² for 4 s. Find its final speed.

Solution

  1. Choose the motion direction as positive: u = 0, a = 1.5 m/s², t = 4 s.
  2. Displacement is not needed, so use v = u + at.
  3. Substitute: v = 0 + 1.5 × 4 = 6 m/s.

Final speed = 6 m/s.

A second stopping distance

A cyclist travels at 6 m/s and decelerates uniformly at 0.3 m/s². Find the distance before stopping.

Solution

  1. With forwards positive, u = 6 m/s, v = 0 and a = −0.3 m/s².
  2. Use v² = u² + 2as: 0 = 36 − 0.6s.
  3. Add 0.6s to both sides: 0.6s = 36.
  4. Divide by 0.6: s = 36 ÷ 0.6 = 60 m.

Stopping distance = 60 m.

Displacement from endpoint velocities

A particle accelerates uniformly from 2 m/s to 8 m/s in 7 s without reversing. Find displacement.

Solution

  1. Here u = 2 m/s, v = 8 m/s and t = 7 s; acceleration is unused.
  2. Average velocity = (2 + 8) ÷ 2 = 5 m/s.
  3. Multiply by time: s = 5 × 7 = 35 m.

Displacement = 35 m.

Find the time

A car accelerates uniformly from 12 m/s to 18 m/s while travelling 150 m. Find the time taken.

Solution

  1. Choose forwards positive: u = 12 m/s, v = 18 m/s and s = 150 m.
  2. Acceleration is unused. Use s = ½(u + v)t.
  3. Substitute: 150 = ½(12 + 18)t = 15t.
  4. Divide both sides by 15: t = 150 ÷ 15 = 10 s.

Time = 10 s.

Find distance and speed

A particle starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 0.8 m/s² for 5 s. Find distance travelled and speed reached.

Solution

  1. Choose the motion direction positive: u = 0, a = 0.8 m/s², t = 5 s.
  2. Distance uses s = ut + ½at² = 0 × 5 + ½ × 0.8 × 5².
  3. Calculate 5² = 25, then s = 0.4 × 25 = 10 m.
  4. Final speed uses v = u + at = 0 + 0.8 × 5 = 4 m/s.

Distance = 10 m; speed = 4 m/s.

Compare two distances without finding acceleration

A particle moves in a straight line with constant deceleration. Its speeds at A, B and C are 16 m/s, 10 m/s and 0 m/s respectively. Find the ratio AB : BC.

Solution

  1. AB and BC name the path lengths between consecutive points. A ratio compares their sizes. Forwards is positive.
  2. Let d be the positive magnitude of deceleration, so the signed acceleration is −d in both intervals. We use d to avoid confusing a positive magnitude with signed a.
  3. For AB, use v² = u² + 2as: 10² = 16² − 2d × AB.
  4. Subtract 100 from 256 and rearrange: 2d × AB = 156. Divide by 2d, which is positive: AB = 78/d.
  5. For BC: 0² = 10² − 2d × BC. Therefore 2d × BC = 100 and BC = 50/d.
  6. The ratio is (78/d) : (50/d). Multiplying both sides of a ratio by the same positive number preserves it, so multiply by d to obtain 78 : 50.
  7. Divide both entries by 2 to simplify: 39 : 25. The first distance is longer because the particle moves faster during that interval.

AB : BC = 39 : 25.
