Part 1

Points on a grid

Where is a point, and how do you get from one point to another?

Reading a point · Moving between points · Negative numbers

Reading a point

Along the corridor, then up the stairs

Every point on a grid has an address made of two numbers, like (1, 2). Think of finding a room in a building. You walk along the corridor first. Then you climb up the stairs.

A point works the same way. The first number, x, tells you how far along. The second number, y, tells you how far up.

So to reach A(1, 2), walk 1 along, then climb 2 up. Watch the dot do it.
Along first, then up. Never the other way round.

Moving between points

Getting from A to B

Now add a second point, B(7, 10). How far is the walk from A to B?

Along: B is at 7 and A is at 1. That is 7 − 1 = 6 along.

Up: B is at 10 and A is at 2. That is 10 − 2 = 8 up.

Each time, take A's number away from B's. These two answers are called the change in x and the change in y. Almost everything in this lesson starts with them.

Your turn: from P(2, 3) to Q(5, 7)

Negative numbers

Left and down

The grid carries on past 0. A minus sign turns you round: minus along means go left, minus up means go down.

So C(−3, −2) is 3 left, then 2 down.

From C to A(1, 2), do the same as before: A's number take away C's.

Along = 1 − (−3) = 1 + 3 = 4 Up = 2 − (−2) = 2 + 2 = 4

Taking away a minus is the same as adding. Count the squares on the grid: 4 each way.

Gradient · a new word

How steep is the line?

Gradient

IN PLAIN WORDS
Join A to B with a straight line. How steep is it? Steepness means: how much do you go up for every 1 you go along?
EXAMPLE
From A to B we go 8 up for 6 along. Divide the 8 by the 6:
8 ÷ 6 = 43 ≈ 1.33
So for every 1 along, the line goes up about 1.33. The staircase on the grid shows this. This number is called the gradient.
IN SHORT
Gradient = up ÷ along.

Gradient formula

The same idea, written as a formula

Call the first point (x₁, y₁), said "x one, y one". Call the second point (x₂, y₂). The letter m stands for the gradient.

m = y₂ − y₁x₂ − x₁ = change in ychange in x

Try it with P(−2, 5) and Q(4, −1):

Up = −1 − 5 = −6 Along = 4 − (−2) = 6 !m = −6 ÷ 6 = −1

The answer is negative because the line goes down as you move right.

Which point goes first?

Either order, but the same order twice

Does it matter if you start from B instead of A? Try it.

✓ B first

(2 − 10) ÷ (1 − 7) = −8 ÷ −6 != 4/3

The two minus signs cancel, so you get the same answer.

✗ Mixed

What goes wrong is mixing the order: B first on top, A first underneath.

(10 − 2) ÷ (1 − 7) = 8 ÷ −6 = −4/3

That says downhill, but the line clearly goes uphill.

Pick a point to start from, and start from it on the top and on the bottom.

Reading the sign

Uphill, downhill, flat, straight up

Always read a line from left to right, like reading a sentence.

Check your answer against the picture: uphill must give a positive number.

Part 3

Midpoint

The point exactly halfway between two others.

Halfway along · Halfway up · The add-and-halve shortcut

Midpoint

Exactly halfway

The midpoint M sits exactly halfway between A(1, 2) and B(7, 10). Do the walk, but stop halfway.

Along: the full walk is 6, so half is 3. Start at 1: 1 + 3 = 4.

Up: the full walk is 8, so half is 4. Start at 2: 2 + 4 = 6.

So M = (4, 6). A quicker way gives the same answer: add the two numbers and halve. (1 + 7) ÷ 2 = 4 and (2 + 10) ÷ 2 = 6.

Part 4

Distance

How long is the straight line from one point to another?

Distance

The shortcut is a triangle

Going 6 along and then 8 up is the long way round. How long is the straight line from A to B?

Look at the picture. The along and the up meet at a square corner. Add the straight line, and you have a right-angled triangle. So use Pythagoras: square the two short sides and add.

AB² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 !AB = √100 = 10

100 is the shortcut squared, so take the square root at the end.

AB = 10. Distance = √(along² + up²).

Distance with negatives

Minus signs vanish when you square

Find the distance from C(−3, 2) to D(2, 14).

Along = 2 − (−3) = 5 Up = 14 − 2 = 12 CD² = 5² + 12² = 169 !CD = √169 = 13

What if you took them away the other way round and got −5? It doesn't matter: (−5)² = 25 too. Squaring always gives a positive answer. Just use brackets on your calculator.

Your turn: distance from P(2, 1) to Q(5, 5)

Exact answers

When the square root isn't a whole number

From P(1, 1) to Q(3, 5) is 2 along and 4 up. So PQ = √(4 + 16) = √20.

√20 is about 4.47. But questions often say "exact". Exact means: don't round. Keep the square root.

You can still make it simpler. Find a square number that goes into 20. 4 does, because 20 = 4 × 5.

Take the square root of the 4 and bring it out the front:

√20 = √4 × √5 != 2√5

Check on a calculator: 2 × √5 ≈ 4.47. It's the same number.

Part 5

The equation of a line

A rule that every point on the line obeys.

A line is a rule · y = mx + c · Where the formula comes from · Two worked examples · ax + by + c = 0

A line is a rule

Every point on the line obeys the same rule

Look at the points on this line: (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 7).

Is there a pattern? Each time, y is double x, plus 1. For (2, 5): 2 × 2 + 1 = 5.

So we write the rule as y = 2x + 1. This is the equation of the line. Every point on the line makes it true.

A point off the line breaks the rule. Try (3, 5): 2 × 3 + 1 = 7, not 5. So (3, 5) is not on the line.

To test if a point is on a line, put its x in and see if you get its y.

y = mx + c

The two numbers in y = 2x + 1

The 2 in front of x is the gradient. Go 1 along and you go 2 up. You can see the little step on the grid.

The + 1 on the end is where the line crosses the y-axis. At the y-axis, x = 0, so y = 2 × 0 + 1 = 1.

Almost every straight line can be written like this:

y = mx + c

m is the gradient, c is where it crosses the y-axis. For y = −x + 4: m = −1 (downhill), c = 4.

Where the formula comes from

Building a line from one point and a gradient

Say a line goes through A(1, 2) and has gradient 4/3. Now pick any point P(x, y) on the line.

Wherever P is, the gradient from A to P is 4/3. Watch P move and see.

y − 2x − 1 = 43

Multiply both sides by (x − 1): y − 2 = 4/3 (x − 1).

With any point (x₁, y₁) and gradient m, it is the same idea:

y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)

Worked example · a point and a gradient

Gradient −2, through (3, 1)

Find the equation of the line, in the form y = mx + c.

  1. Put m = −2 and the point (3, 1) into y − y₁ = m(x − x₁):
    y − 1 = −2(x − 3)
  2. Multiply out the bracket (−2 × −3 = +6). Then add 1 to both sides:
    y − 1 = −2x + 6 !y = −2x + 7
  3. Check with the point (3, 1):
    −2 × 3 + 7 = 1 ✓

Worked example · two points

The line through A(1, 2) and B(7, 10)

Find the equation of the line AB, in the form y = mx + c.

  1. Find the gradient first. Up 8, along 6:
    m = 8 ÷ 6 = 4/3
  2. Put A(1, 2) into the formula. Multiply out, then add 2:
    y − 2 = 4/3 (x − 1) y − 2 = 4/3 x − 4/3 !y = 4/3 x + 2/3
  3. Check with the other point, B(7, 10):
    4/3 × 7 + 2/3 = 28/3 + 2/3 = 30/3 = 10 ✓

The midpoint M(4, 6) is on this line too: 16/3 + 2/3 = 6. That makes sense. It's halfway along the line.

Another way to write it

The form ax + by + c = 0

Some exam questions want whole numbers only, with everything on one side and 0 on the other.

  1. Start from y = 4/3 x + 2/3. The fractions are thirds, so times every term by 3:
    3y = 4x + 2
  2. Move 3y to the other side, so one side is 0:
    0 = 4x − 3y + 2 !4x − 3y + 2 = 0
  3. Check with A(1, 2):
    4 − 6 + 2 = 0 ✓

Part 6

What if there's a letter?

Exam questions give you the answer and hide a coordinate as k. The tools don't change: you run them backwards.

Midpoint backwards · A gradient with k · Three points in a line · A point with letters on a line · When k is squared · Two possible answers · Midpoint with letters · Equal distances · When the letter cancels

The idea

A letter is a point that hasn't settled yet

Say a point is (2, k). We know it is 2 along. We don't know how far up. It could be anywhere on the dashed line.

The question then gives you one more fact, like "the gradient is 3" or "the distance is 5". That fact tells you where the point is. The same four steps work every time:

Use the tool as normal, with k written in.

Set it equal to the fact you're told.

Solve for k.

Put k back in and check.

Nothing new to learn. Same tools, used backwards.

Midpoint backwards

You know the middle and one end

M(3, 1) is the midpoint of A(−2, 4) and B. Find B.

  1. Picture it. A to M is 5 along and 3 down. M is halfway, so B is the same jump again from M:
    !B = (3 + 5, 1 − 3) = (8, −2)
  2. Or use the midpoint rule backwards. For x:
    (−2 + x) ÷ 2 = 3 −2 + x = 6 !x = 8
  3. For y:
    (4 + y) ÷ 2 = 1 4 + y = 2 !y = −2

A gradient with k

You know the gradient, not the point

The line through P(2, k) and Q(5, 11) has gradient 3. Find k.

  1. Write the gradient with k in it. Set it equal to 3:
    (11 − k) ÷ (5 − 2) = 3 (11 − k) ÷ 3 = 3
  2. Times both sides by 3, then solve:
    11 − k = 9 !k = 2
  3. Check:
    (11 − 2) ÷ 3 = 9 ÷ 3 = 3 ✓

P could be anywhere on the dashed line. The gradient picks one place.

Three points in a line

"On the same straight line" means the same gradient

A(1, 2), B(4, 8) and C(6, k) lie on one straight line. Find k.

  1. In a straight line, AB and BC are equally steep. Gradient of AB:
    m = 6 ÷ 3 = 2
  2. Gradient of BC, with k in it. Set it equal to 2:
    (k − 8) ÷ (6 − 4) = 2 (k − 8) ÷ 2 = 2 k − 8 = 4 !k = 12

Your turn, with letters

A point with letters on a line

Both coordinates contain k

The point (k, 3k − 1) lies on the line y = 2x + 4. Find k.

  1. "Lies on the line" means the point fits the rule. Put x = k and y = 3k − 1 into y = 2x + 4:
    3k − 1 = 2k + 4 k − 1 = 4 !k = 5
  2. So the point is (5, 14). Check:
    2 × 5 + 4 = 14 ✓

The dashed line shows every place (k, 3k − 1) could be. It crosses our line once, so there is one answer.

When k is squared

A squared letter usually means two answers

The point (k, k² − 1) lies on the line y = x + 5. Find the possible values of k.

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Put x = k and y = k² − 1 into y = x + 5:
    k² − 1 = k + 5
  2. There is a k², so move everything to one side:
    k² − k − 6 = 0
  3. Factorise. Find two numbers that times to −6 and add to −1. They are −3 and 2:
    (k − 3)(k + 2) = 0 !k = 3 or −2
  4. The points are (3, 8) and (−2, 3). Check: 3 + 5 = 8 ✓ and −2 + 5 = 3 ✓

(k, k² − 1) moves along a curve, and the line crosses it twice.

Distance with k

A distance often gives two answers

P is (1, 2) and Q is (k, 5). PQ = 5. Find the possible values of k.

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Along is k − 1. Up is 5 − 2 = 3. Use Pythagoras:
    (k − 1)² + 3² = 5² (k − 1)² + 9 = 25 (k − 1)² = 16
  2. Square root both sides. It can be + or −, because 4² and (−4)² are both 16:
    k − 1 = 4 or −4 !k = 5 or −3
Your turn: A(0, 0), B(k, 8) and AB = 10. Find both values of k.

Midpoint with letters

The letter is inside the points

A is (k, 1) and B is (3k, 7). The midpoint of AB is (8, 4). Find k.

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. The x's have k in them, so use those. Add and halve:
    (k + 3k) ÷ 2 = 8 4k ÷ 2 = 8 2k = 8 !k = 4
  2. So A is (4, 1) and B is (12, 7). Check with the y's:
    (1 + 7) ÷ 2 = 4 ✓

Use the coordinate with k in it. Use the other one to check.

Equal distances

The same distance from two points

P(k, 0) is on the x-axis. It is the same distance from A(1, 4) as from B(5, 2). Find k.

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Write both distances squared. Then there are no square roots:
    PA² = (k − 1)² + 4² PB² = (k − 5)² + 2²
  2. The distances are equal, so set them equal. Multiply out:
    (k − 1)² + 16 = (k − 5)² + 4 k² − 2k + 17 = k² − 10k + 29
  3. Both sides have k², so take it away. Then solve:
    −2k + 17 = −10k + 29 8k = 12 !k = 1.5
  4. Check:
    PA² = 0.25 + 16 = 16.25 PB² = 12.25 + 4 = 16.25 ✓

When the letter cancels

A gradient that doesn't depend on t

P is (1 + t, 3t) and Q is (4 + t, 3t + 6). Show the gradient of PQ does not depend on t.

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Up. The 3t's cancel:
    Up = (3t + 6) − 3t = 6
  2. Along. The minus goes on both the 1 and the t:
    Along = (4 + t) − (1 + t) = 4 + t − 1 − t = 3
  3. Gradient. There is no t left, so it is 2 whatever t is:
    m = 6 ÷ 3 != 2

Changing t moves PQ to a new place, but it never changes how steep it is. All of them are parallel.

Part 7

A full exam question

Four parts, one part per slide. Try each on paper before you reveal it.

(a) Exact length · (b) Midpoint · (c) Equation · (d) A point with k

Exam-style question · 9 marks

The points A(−1, 2) and B(3, 10)

(a) Find the exact length of AB.[2]

(b) Find the coordinates of the midpoint M of AB.[1]

(c) Find the equation of AB, in the form ax + by + c = 0.[3]

(d) The point C(k, k² − 4) lies on AB. Find the possible values of k.[3]

Get a pen. Do all four parts on paper, then go to the next slide.

Part (a) · 2 marks

Exact length of AB

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Find along and up:
    Along = 3 − (−1) = 4 Up = 10 − 2 = 8
  2. Use Pythagoras:
    AB = √(4² + 8²) = √(16 + 64) = √80
  3. It says "exact", so simplify. Don't round. 16 goes into 80:
    √80 = √16 × √5 != 4√5

Writing 8.94 would lose the mark, because the question said exact.

Part (b) · 1 mark

The midpoint M

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Add the x's and halve. Then do the same with the y's:
    x = (−1 + 3) ÷ 2 = 1 y = (2 + 10) ÷ 2 = 6
  2. So:
    !M = (1, 6)

Quick sense check on the grid: M should sit right in the middle of the line.

Part (c) · 3 marks

The equation of AB

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Gradient. Up 8, along 4:
    m = 8 ÷ 4 = 2
  2. Use A(−1, 2). Careful: x − (−1) is x + 1. Multiply out, then add 2:
    y − 2 = 2(x + 1) y − 2 = 2x + 2 y = 2x + 4
  3. Move y to the other side:
    !2x − y + 4 = 0
  4. Check with B(3, 10):
    6 − 10 + 4 = 0 ✓

Part (d) · 3 marks

C(k, k² − 4) lies on AB

Try it on paper first. Then show the steps one at a time.

  1. Use part (c): y = 2x + 4. Put x = k and y = k² − 4 in, then move everything to one side:
    k² − 4 = 2k + 4 k² − 2k − 8 = 0
  2. Factorise. Two numbers that times to −8 and add to −2 are −4 and 2:
    (k − 4)(k + 2) = 0 !k = 4 or −2
  3. Check both points on y = 2x + 4:
    (4, 12): 2 × 4 + 4 = 12 ✓ (−2, 0): 2 × (−2) + 4 = 0 ✓

This is the same as the k² example in Part 6. It comes up a lot in exams.

Common mistakes

Six slips that cost marks

Mixing the order in a gradient

✗ (10 − 2) ÷ (1 − 7) = −4/3

✓ Same start top and bottom: (10 − 2) ÷ (7 − 1) = 4/3

Subtracting for a midpoint

✗ (7 − 1) ÷ 2 = 3

✓ Add, then halve: (1 + 7) ÷ 2 = 4

Losing the minus when squaring

✗ Typing −3² gives −9

✓ Type (−3)² with brackets. It's 9.

Forgetting the second answer

✗ (k − 1)² = 16, so k − 1 = 4

✓ k − 1 = 4 or −4. A square root has two answers.

Stopping at distance squared

✗ AB = 100

✓ AB = √100 = 10

Rounding when it says "exact"

✗ AB = 4.47

✓ AB = 2√5
